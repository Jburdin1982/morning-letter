In field and woods: the wild turkeys of Crowley’s Ridge have long since gathered into their winter flocks, hens and young birds together, and on a raw day like this one they keep to the hardwood draws, scratching through the leaves for acorns; a flock of twenty crossing a gravel road near Bono or Lake City is a fine sight for the drive home. Pecans are falling in the old yard trees around Jonesboro, and the morning after a windy rain is the best time to gather them before the squirrels and crows do. At the feeder, the slate-gray juncos, the little birds our grandparents called snowbirds, have arrived from the north for the winter, and they feed on the ground beneath it; scatter a little millet there for them. In the kitchen, save the turkey carcass: simmered tomorrow with an onion, a carrot and a bay leaf, it makes the stock for the best soup of the year.

Weather lore, not a forecast: the old fisherman’s rhyme runs, “When the wind is in the north, the skillful fisher goes not forth; when the wind is in the east, ’tis neither fit for man nor beast; when the wind is in the south, it blows the bait in the fish’s mouth; when the wind is in the west, then ’tis at the very best.” Today’s cold, wet wind would have kept the old-timers by the fire, which, on Thanksgiving, is where most of us mean to be anyway.

CWL tells data centers they must pay their own way

City Water and Light, the city-owned utility that supplies electricity, water and sewer service to Jonesboro, has adopted a Customer Pledge and Data Center Utility Service Policy meant to keep data centers, the warehouse-sized buildings full of computer servers that run the internet and artificial intelligence, from driving up the bills of ordinary customers. The CWL Board of Directors approved the policy Tuesday, and it took effect at once. The utility says it has been approached by large data centers in the past, and that in the last month or so several smaller ones, needing 5 to 20 megawatts, have asked about service; a 20-megawatt center could use about twice the electricity of one of Jonesboro’s largest existing industrial customers.

Under the policy, a data center must pay for its own infrastructure and any upgrades it needs, sign a long-term contract for the service it asks for whether or not it uses all of it, and put up financial security, such as payment bonds, letters of credit, parent-company guarantees or cash in escrow, so that CWL and its other customers are not left holding the bill. It must also agree to cut back its water use during a system-wide shortage, and its power is not to come from energy supplies reserved for existing customers. A CWL official said a handful of such centers “could really limit our ability to serve our existing customers,” and that the utility has told those that contacted it that they “would bear all the risk and exposure for the costs.”

The policy is modeled on the “Ratepayer Protection Pledge” promoted by President Trump, which several utilities and technology companies have signed voluntarily; in Jonesboro it is mandatory. Bradley Isbell, an attorney with the Waddell, Cole & Jones law firm, put the policy in writing. The question is a live one in Arkansas, where a federal judge is still weighing a dispute over a large data center planned in Pulaski County.

From the State

DNA ties a Texas inmate to three unsolved Little Rock stabbings

Little Rock police said Wednesday that DNA evidence has linked Antwon Lewis, 34, to three fatal stabbings that unsettled the city in 2020 and 2021. Mr. Lewis is now in a Texas prison, and Arkansas authorities have placed a detainer on him, a formal request that he be held and returned here to face three counts of capital murder, the state’s most serious homicide charge. The charges are allegations; he has not been tried. Police said DNA recovered from one victim matched Mr. Lewis, and further investigative work tied him to the other two killings.

The victims were Larry McChristian, 64, of Yellville in north Arkansas, found dead in a front yard on South Gaines Street about 2 a.m. on Aug. 24, 2020, two days after University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences police reported him missing; Jeff Welch, 62, found with stab wounds to the neck on West 12th Street about a month later; and Marlon Franklin, 40, found dead on Wright Avenue early on April 12, 2021. At the time, then-Police Chief Keith Humphrey warned of a “known threat,” saying the victims had been walking when they were “randomly attacked,” and the city offered a $20,000 reward and worked with the FBI.

A woman, Debra Walker, 43, survived being stabbed more than 15 times near 19th and Marshall streets the day before Mr. Franklin was killed; police had linked that attack to the series but did not say Wednesday whether it has been tied to Mr. Lewis. In Texas, Mr. Lewis was convicted in December 2022 of two counts of aggravated assault with a deadly weapon, from attacks in May 2021, and sentenced to 15 years on each; Texas records list his projected release as 2036, with parole eligibility in November 2028. No date has been set for his return to Arkansas.

Flu reaches “high” level in Arkansas; deaths now reported in four counties

Influenza activity in Arkansas has climbed to “high,” the Arkansas Department of Health reported this week, up from “moderate” a week earlier and “minimal” just two weeks ago. The department’s dashboard, updated Wednesday, now shows at least one flu death this season in Benton, Craighead, Pope and Pulaski counties; a week earlier only Benton and Craighead had reported deaths. Exact totals are not published, because the state suppresses counts from one to nine for privacy. Last season’s first confirmed death did not come until mid-December, and that season ended with 94 deaths and 2,133 hospitalizations.

So far this season 342 Arkansans have been hospitalized with flu-like illness, 111 of them in the past week, led by Washington County (53), Pulaski (44) and Benton (33). Absences in public schools rose to 8.7 percent from 7.3 percent the week before, well above the five-year average for this time of year; Mississippi County had the highest rate, 12.5 percent. Two more outbreaks were reported at nursing homes or similar group settings, for eight this season.

Health officials are urging flu shots, which are free on a walk-in basis, with no appointment, at local health units in all 75 counties; people with insurance are asked to bring their cards. Flu season runs from October to May and usually peaks between December and February, so the worst may still be ahead as families gather for the holidays.

The Arkansas State Police said Wednesday that a three-day operation with the Texas Department of Public Safety aimed at protecting children produced 105 traffic stops, 54 arrests and 76 warnings in the Texarkana area. The effort, run Nov. 19 through Saturday, was part of the Interdiction for the Protection of Children initiative, a training and enforcement program that teaches officers to spot endangered or exploited children, and the people who prey on them, during routine contacts such as traffic stops.

Murphy Oil co-founder Bertie Murphy Deming Smith dies at 100

Bertie Murphy Deming Smith, one of the four siblings who founded the company that became Murphy Oil Corp., died Monday at her home in Alexandria, La. She was 100. In 1946 she joined her brother, Charles H. Murphy Jr., and her sisters, Caroline M. Keller and Theodosia M. Nolan, in pooling the family’s timber, land and oil holdings into C. Murphy & Co., the forerunner of Murphy Oil, which for decades was one of the best-known companies in El Dorado in south Arkansas before moving its headquarters to Houston in 2020. Born Dec. 26, 1925, she was the daughter of Charles H. Murphy Sr., an El Dorado banker, landowner and oilman, and Bertie Wilson Murphy.

She was also a noted philanthropist, giving $15 million in 2011 to Mary Baldwin College in Virginia for a college of health sciences and $25 million to Tulane University to create a department of medicine named for her late husband, John W. Deming. Her son Claiborne Deming, chairman of Murphy Oil, described her as a voracious reader who put family first and was open-minded and nonjudgmental. Survivors include four children, a stepson, 11 grandchildren and 17 great-grandchildren. Service arrangements were not reported.

State buys the archive of “King Biscuit Time,” the Helena blues show on the air since 1941

Arkansas Heritage, the history and culture division of the state Department of Parks, Heritage and Tourism, has acquired the archives and broadcast rights of “King Biscuit Time,” the daily blues program that has aired on AM station KFFA in Helena since Nov. 21, 1941, and is billed as the longest-running daily radio show in American history. The state paid $25,000, from the Arkansas Natural and Cultural Resources Council’s funds, for the recordings, photographs, rights and other property. Division Director Marty Ryall and Jim Howe Jr., president of Delta Broadcasting, signed the deal Nov. 20, the show’s 85th anniversary.

The show began when bluesmen Robert Lockwood Jr. and Sonny Boy Williamson pitched it to the station’s owner, with Interstate Grocery Company, maker of King Biscuit Flour, sponsoring it on the condition that the musicians plug its products. “Sunshine” Sonny Payne hosted it from 1951 until his death in 2018; today it is hosted by Thomas Jacques of the Delta Cultural Center in Helena and still airs weekdays at 12:15 p.m. A sign in the booth counts 17,000 shows, and more than 10,000 recordings, going back to 1986, are expected eventually to be put online or made available on request once they are cataloged. No timeline has been set. Mr. Ryall said the purchase keeps the program “accessible for generations to come.”

From the Nation

Storm postpones the Macy’s parade for the first time in its history

A powerful coastal storm bringing heavy rain, wind gusts of up to 65 mph, flash flooding and coastal flooding to the East Coast today has forced Macy’s to postpone its Thanksgiving Day Parade in New York City from this morning to Saturday at 8:30 a.m. Eastern (7:30 a.m. Central). It is the first weather postponement since the parade began in 1924; it has never been canceled for weather, though it was suspended from 1942 to 1944 during World War II, when helium was needed for the war effort. This year’s parade is the 100th. Macy’s made the call Wednesday “after close consultation with our city and agency partners,” and New York Mayor Zohran Mamdani said “safety has to come first.” New York Gov. Kathy Hochul declared a state of emergency for New York City and Nassau, Suffolk and Westchester counties, warning of heavy rain and strong winds that could make travel dangerous. The giant balloons, inflated Wednesday, were deflated again, and the police department must call in more than 1,000 officers who had been scheduled off Saturday, when the weather is expected to be dry with light winds.

The same storm is snarling the busiest travel days of the year. By early Thursday at least 335 U.S. flights had been canceled and 289 delayed, on top of more than 150 cancellations and about 6,000 delays Wednesday; the Federal Aviation Administration warned of ground stops and delays around New York, Philadelphia and Washington, and Delta, United and American are letting travelers rebook for free on some Northeast routes. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said Tuesday set a 16-year high of 52,000 flights. In Miami, a FedEx cargo plane ran off the end of a runway Wednesday night with no injuries, closing all but one runway for a time. The storm’s back side brought snow to parts of Missouri and Illinois overnight.

Iowa blast goes off with no airborne lead detected

The Army on Wednesday morning blew up nearly five tons of degraded lead azide, a highly sensitive explosive used in detonators, at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown in southeastern Iowa, and federal officials say air monitors found no lead in the air afterward. The 68 barrels, which the Army said had crystallized and could go off on their own, were set off in two simultaneous detonations at about 9 a.m., inside barriers of water, foam and gel blocks meant to hold down the toxic dust. The Environmental Protection Agency said its real-time air monitors were reporting “ZERO detection of airborne lead,” and the Federal Emergency Management Agency issued an all-clear, telling Iowans and Illinoisans in the expected path that they could stop sheltering indoors. Maj. Gen. Eric Shirley of the Army Sustainment Command said the blast “exceeded expectations,” and Missouri Gov. Mike Kehoe said it appeared well contained.

The Army says sampling of the ground is under way and results will be released when complete; air readings alone cannot show how much lead may have settled on soil, so the final word on contamination is not in. Families who left the evacuation zone are to be notified by phone when they can return. Iowa’s governor-elect, Rob Sand, a Democrat, called for full transparency about what he described as a chaotic lead-up to the detonation.

Administration proposes new tariffs on China, Europe and 12 others

The U.S. Trade Representative’s office, the agency that negotiates and enforces trade deals, on Wednesday proposed new import taxes on goods from 14 economies it says flood world markets with excess, often government-subsidized, factory output: China, the European Union, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, India, Vietnam, Thailand, Malaysia, Indonesia, Singapore, Cambodia, Bangladesh and Switzerland. The proposed rates mostly run from 15 to 20 percent, with a top proposed rate of 35 percent for Vietnam. China would face 20 percent on top of the roughly 25 percent tariffs left from President Trump’s first term, bringing many Chinese goods to about 45 percent. For partners with existing trade deals, such as the EU and Japan, the new rates are meant to count existing duties, so Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said they would “top up a few percentage points” rather than stack.

The move uses Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, a law against unfair foreign trade practices, after the Supreme Court in February struck down the near-worldwide tariffs Mr. Trump had imposed under an emergency-powers law; critics say the administration is rebuilding those tariffs by other means. Nothing takes effect yet: public comments are due Jan. 5 and hearings are set for January, and Mr. Greer said any tariffs are likely a couple of months away or more. China’s foreign ministry said the “so-called ‘overcapacity’ does not really exist” and urged that disputes be settled by negotiation. Technology stocks fell sharply after the news before recovering much of the loss by the close.

Second judge blocks Trump’s new limits on birthright citizenship

U.S. District Judge Christopher Cooper in Washington, D.C., on Wednesday blocked the administration from enforcing President Trump’s August executive orders restricting birthright citizenship, the rule in the Constitution’s 14th Amendment that children born on U.S. soil are citizens. The orders were Mr. Trump’s second attempt after the Supreme Court, by a 6-3 vote in June, struck down the first one, which he had signed on his first day back in office; the new orders sought to narrow who qualifies and to combat what the administration calls “birth tourism,” traveling here to give birth so a child gains citizenship. In a 67-page opinion, Judge Cooper, an appointee of President Barack Obama, wrote that “the President cannot carve out new exceptions to birthright citizenship for children born within the United States and subject to its laws,” calling the merits “clear-cut.”

The case is a class action brought by immigrant-rights groups including the American Civil Liberties Union on behalf of children who would be affected, and the injunction also bars other steps to deny those children’s citizenship. A Maryland judge, Deborah Boardman, blocked the same orders in September, so the administration must overturn both rulings before it can enforce them. White House spokeswoman Lauren Bis called the decision “just another ruling from an activist judge” and said the orders follow the Supreme Court’s reasoning.

Judge bars FCC from early review of ABC station licenses

A federal judge in Washington ruled Wednesday that the Federal Communications Commission, which licenses the nation’s broadcast television and radio stations, may not go forward with an early review of the licenses of eight ABC-owned stations, finding that ABC is likely to prove the review was illegal retaliation for its programming. FCC Chairman Brendan Carr ordered the review in April, years before the licenses were due for renewal, the day after President Trump attacked a joke by ABC late-night host Jimmy Kimmel and demanded his firing; Judge Loren AliKhan, an appointee of President Joe Biden, called the timing “no coincidence” and wrote, “This is not a close case.” She said the FCC may enforce the law but cannot punish license holders for disfavored speech. She turned down ABC’s broader request to bar any informal pressure on its news decisions. ABC News called the ruling “an important victory for the First Amendment”; the FCC said it is wrong on the facts and the law and expects to win in the end. The preliminary order stands while ABC and its parent, Disney, pursue the lawsuit they filed in August.

Treasury’s financial-crimes agency hacked

The Treasury Department’s Financial Crimes Enforcement Network, or FinCEN, which collects banks’ reports of suspicious transactions to fight money laundering and terrorist financing, has taken some of its computer systems offline after hackers gained unauthorized access to what it called a “limited subset” of them. The agency, which disclosed the intrusion Tuesday evening, says it has found no evidence so far that its databases of bank-secrecy reports or of the true owners of companies were breached, but forensic work continues and it has not said what was taken or who was responsible. Treasury is working with the FBI and the federal Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency. FinCEN extended some filing deadlines to Dec. 30 for banks affected by the outage. The disclosure came hours after reports that hackers broke into an unclassified computer system shared by the CIA and the Office of the Director of National Intelligence, and after the FBI confirmed a hack of its national crime database; officials have not linked the incidents.

From Abroad

Iran says two more tankers hit mines near Hormuz

Iran’s Revolutionary Guards said Thursday that two more tankers exploded and caught fire after striking sea mines north of Khasab, a port in Oman’s Musandam peninsula at the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow mouth of the Persian Gulf that normally carries a large share of the world’s oil; it said one was a supertanker carrying more than 2 million barrels of crude and was still burning. The claim, carried by the semi-official Tasnim news agency, has not been independently confirmed, and the ships were not named. Separately, the British military’s maritime monitoring agency reported an explosion from an “unknown projectile” near a tanker about 50 miles west of Dubai early Thursday, with no damage to the ship; on Wednesday the Guards claimed an LNG carrier had hit a mine. A Guards spokesman, Hossein Mohebbi, said Iran is using “heavy firepower” to keep the strait closed, and the Guards’ navy commander said Iran has “full operational control” of it.

The U.S. is enforcing a naval blockade of Iran’s ports, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent called the Iranian government a “sinking ship.” Weekly ship crossings through Hormuz fell from 217 to 147 between Nov. 1 and Nov. 23, according to the ship-tracking firm Kpler. Israel’s defense minister, Israel Katz, said this week that Iran is developing chemical weapons and did not rule out preemptive strikes; Iran’s foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, called the claim fabricated and accused Israel of trying to draw the U.S. into another war.

Houthis widen attacks on Saudi ports and refinery

Yemen’s Houthi rebels, an Iran-aligned movement that controls the capital, Sanaa, and much of the country’s north, claimed attacks Wednesday and overnight on the Saudi Red Sea ports of Jeddah, the kingdom’s largest, and Jizan, as well as on Ras Tanura, Saudi Arabia’s biggest refinery, on the Persian Gulf coast. The Saudi-led coalition fighting the Houthis said a projectile hit Jizan port, causing minor damage and no civilian injuries, and that it shot down a drone near Jeddah’s port; it said it destroyed four drones over Ras Tanura but did not say the refinery was hit, though satellite pictures showed a large smoke plume. The coalition also said it intercepted ballistic missiles fired at Riyadh, where a Houthi drone struck a skyscraper Tuesday and injured 11 people. The Houthis called the port attacks retaliation for coalition air strikes on Hodeidah, the Yemeni port that handles about 90 percent of the country’s imports; the Houthis said the coalition carried out 105 strikes across Yemen Wednesday, a figure that could not be verified. The United Nations relief chief, Tom Fletcher, said half of Yemen’s people now need aid and more than 364,000 have been driven from their homes since August.

Judges cast doubt on the case against Netanyahu

The three judges hearing Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s long-running corruption trial in the Jerusalem District Court signaled Wednesday that much of the case may not hold up, and Mr. Netanyahu, who denies wrongdoing, said “the trial must end immediately.” The judges suggested prosecutors drop the most serious count, Case 4000, in which he is charged with bribery for allegedly giving regulatory favors to the telecom company Bezeq in exchange for favorable news coverage, with Judge Rivka Friedman-Feldman saying proving bribery faced “considerable difficulties.” They questioned whether a crime occurred in Case 2000, an alleged deal with a newspaper publisher for friendlier coverage, and suggested Case 1000, over luxury gifts from wealthy friends, be settled by agreement. The judges stressed their remarks were preliminary and not a verdict.

Attorney General Gali Baharav-Miara, unusually summoned to the hearing, said she would consider the recommendations and was ready for either a verdict or a plea deal; a former Netanyahu lawyer rejected the idea of a plea deal. Mr. Netanyahu is serving as caretaker prime minister after inconclusive Oct. 27 elections, and opposition leader Gadi Eisenkot has until Dec. 6 to form a government, with a possible 14-day extension. Separately, Israeli television reported that Mr. Netanyahu told Jared Kushner, President Trump’s son-in-law and Middle East adviser, in a call this week that he is willing to move forward on the U.S. peace plan for Gaza, which Washington wants to advance by letting an international peacekeeping force deploy.

Russian missiles hit Kyiv; leaked records show huge Russian losses

Russia fired ballistic missiles at Kyiv late Wednesday, hitting a shopping center and other buildings and injuring at least seven people, according to Mayor Vitali Klitschko, along with 113 drones launched across Ukraine overnight, of which Ukraine said it downed or jammed 87. Russian attacks killed 10 people and injured 58, including three children, across Ukraine over the past day, Ukrainian officials said, with the heaviest tolls in the Kharkiv and Donetsk regions. Ukraine has struggled to stop ballistic missiles amid shortages of interceptors for its American-made Patriot air-defense systems. Russian President Vladimir Putin said this week that retaliation for Ukraine’s drone strikes on Russian oil refineries was “inevitable.”

A BBC analysis of leaked records from Rosstat, Russia’s state statistics agency, taken by Belarusian and Ukrainian hacker groups, found at least 490,334 Russian military deaths registered since the February 2022 invasion through September of this year; the independent Russian outlet Mediazona, adjusting for registration delays and missing records, estimates the true toll near 700,000. The figures have not been independently confirmed. In Libya, the eastern-based Libyan National Army, which has close ties to Russia, vowed to retaliate against Ukraine after two of its naval officers died of wounds from Ukraine’s sea-drone attack on Russian ships at Benghazi; Ukrainian officials have said Libyan personnel were not the target.

Solomon Islands signs security treaty with Australia

The Solomon Islands, a Pacific island nation northeast of Australia, signed a security treaty with Australia on Wednesday that names Australia its “primary security partner” and states “there will be no foreign military or security base” in the islands, a move widely seen as limiting China’s reach in the region. Prime Minister Matthew Wale and Australian Prime Minister Anthony Albanese signed the pact, under which Australia will provide 740 million Australian dollars, about $490 million, over 10 years for free education and other programs, and the Solomons must consult Australia before security or critical-infrastructure deals with countries outside the Pacific Islands Forum. The Solomons signed a security pact with China in 2022 that alarmed Washington and Canberra; a diplomat said the Solomons told Beijing last month that it will not be renewed when it expires next year, but Mr. Wale said only that talks with China were “ongoing.” Opposition leader Manasseh Sogavare, the former prime minister who signed the China pact, criticized the treaty as “fast-tracked.”

Fuel truck fire kills at least 30 in Cairo

At least 30 people were killed and 15 injured Wednesday when a vehicle carrying petroleum products overturned and burst into flames on the ring road around Cairo, Egypt’s Health Ministry said. The fire broke out above a tunnel in the Al-Warraq district of Giza, across the Nile from central Cairo. The ministry sent 39 ambulances, and local media said firefighters brought the blaze under control; the health minister visited the injured in hospital. Early reports put the toll at 21 before it was raised. No cause has been given.

European spacecraft sends first close-ups of asteroid struck by NASA

The European Space Agency’s Hera spacecraft, launched in October 2024, has reached the asteroid Didymos and its small moon, Dimorphos, and the agency on Wednesday released its first close-up pictures, showing the crater left when NASA deliberately crashed its DART spacecraft into Dimorphos in 2022. That collision was the first test of whether striking an asteroid could nudge it off course, a possible way to protect Earth someday, and it did change the moonlet’s orbit. The new images show boulders and craters on Didymos, Dimorphos casting a shadow on its larger partner, and trails of boulders apparently orbiting the pair. Hera will spend several months measuring the crater and mapping both bodies, and in about six weeks is to release two small satellites, Milani and Juventas, to study Dimorphos’s surface makeup and interior.

  • Analysts leaned toward buying: SNY (Sanofi, the Paris-based drugmaker behind the asthma and eczema drug Dupixent, which this week agreed to buy the lung-disease drugmaker Insmed for about $41 billion, or $175 a share in cash). Risk to the cautious view: hospital demand from an aging population remains strong, and HCA has a long record of steady profits.

On the Field

Red Wolves sweep the Miami tournament, beating FIU 92-88

Arkansas State men’s basketball (6-2) won its third game in three days Wednesday afternoon, holding off the host Florida International University (FIU) Panthers 92-88 at the Ocean Bank Convocation Center in Miami to sweep the Thanksgiving-week tournament there. Guard Layne Taylor led again with 26 points on 9-of-15 shooting, and Jordan Pyke added 21 points and seven rebounds. Amadou Doumbia pulled down nine rebounds and Tameron Ferguson handed out seven assists.

The game was close throughout. A-State led 41-38 at the half on a Ferguson layup at the buzzer, built its first double-digit lead early in the second half, and saw FIU tie the score at 61 before a Chaz Watson 3-pointer put the Red Wolves back ahead for good. FIU, a former Sun Belt rival now in Conference USA, pulled within a point on a 3 with 29 seconds left, but Taylor made two free throws and Pyke one more to close it out. The Red Wolves forced 24 turnovers and had 10 steals; they have reached 10 steals in seven of their eight games, the most in the nation. FIU missed 14 free throws, which its coach called the difference.

The Red Wolves come home to First National Bank Arena to play Rice on Sunday at 1 p.m., on ESPN+ and the Red Wolves Sports Network.

A-State football closes a hard season against Troy on Senior Day

Arkansas State football (4-7, 2-5 Sun Belt) plays its last game of the season Saturday against Troy (4-7, 2-5) at Centennial Bank Stadium. Senior Day ceremonies begin at 1:30 p.m., and kickoff is at 2 on ESPN+. Both teams have lost three straight; Troy lost 45-7 at home to Georgia Southern last week, and A-State lost 48-3 at Louisiana Tech in interim coach Jalen Ortiz’s first game, its worst Sun Belt loss since 2001.

Ortiz, the associate head coach and cornerbacks coach who took over when Butch Jones was let go Nov. 17, said the problems in Ruston were “blatantly obvious,” among them turnovers and a season-high 12 penalties, and that running the ball “is going to be a huge piece” Saturday. A-State ranks 122nd of 138 major-college teams in rushing and managed 26 yards on 28 carries against Louisiana Tech. Solomon Baggett leads the team with 378 rushing yards.

The search for a permanent coach, aided by the firm TurnkeyZRG, has no announced timeline. The Arkansas Democrat-Gazette reported that fans and online reports point to Hugh Freeze, who won a Sun Belt title at A-State in 2011 and was fired at Auburn last year, as the leading candidate; athletic director Chris Pezman has not commented.

Razorbacks host LSU for the Golden Boot Saturday morning

Arkansas Razorbacks football (4-7, 2-6 SEC) ends its first season under coach Ryan Silverfield Saturday at 11:45 a.m. against LSU (6-5) at Reynolds Razorback Stadium in Fayetteville, on the SEC Network. It is Senior Day for the Hogs, and the winner takes the Golden Boot, the gold-plated trophy LSU has kept for four straight years. Arkansas has not beaten the Tigers since a 16-13 overtime win in 2021, though five of the last six meetings were decided by one score.

The Razorbacks cannot reach a bowl after last week’s 45-3 loss at Texas, but a fifth win would make Silverfield the first Arkansas coach since Bobby Petrino in 2008 to win five games in his first year. Quarterback KJ Jackson has thrown for 2,231 yards and 10 touchdowns, and receiver Chris Marshall needs 133 yards for a 1,000-yard season. Silverfield called the game a possible springboard into the offseason, when heavy roster turnover through the transfer portal is expected.

No. 5 Tennessee is one win from the playoff at Vanderbilt

Tennessee Vols football (9-2, 6-2 SEC), fifth in the College Football Playoff ranking, plays at Vanderbilt (4-7, 1-7) Saturday at 6 p.m. on ESPN in Nashville. A win should put the Vols in the 12-team playoff, would give coach Josh Heupel his third 10-win season and could lift them into the top four and a first-round bye; a loss could cost them the playoff. Afield & Afloat

Ratings are rule-of-thumb estimates from the forecast, the rut calendar and the sun, not measurements of animal or fish behavior.

Deer outlook: Fair

Three things help today: the rut, now in its late, post-peak phase when older bucks still check for the last does; a cold dawn near 33; and a barometer that has been falling since yesterday, which often keeps deer feeding ahead of weather. Against them stands the rain, likely by midday and heaviest around three o’clock. Best windows: from 6:20 a.m. to about two hours after sunrise, the driest part of the day, and from 2:49 p.m. until sunset at 4:49 for anyone willing to sit in the wet.

Today’s tip: make it a short dawn hunt before the turkey goes in the oven. The rain chance is only about one in five at daylight but better than one in two by nine, so be in the stand early and plan to be home by midmorning. Hunt the edges of thick bedding cover, where deer will slip back as the weather arrives, and wear a quiet rain shell over wool. In Zone 4, around Jonesboro, it is bows only; in Zones 3, 6 and 8 the rifles are still out.

Tomorrow, Friday (estimate: Fair). A cold, gray dawn near 34, the barometer holding steady, a breeze near 10 miles an hour that fades through the day, and no rain to speak of. Deer that sat out Thursday’s weather should be on their feet. Windows: 6:21 a.m. to about two hours after sunrise, and 2:49 p.m. to sunset at 4:49.

Tomorrow’s tip: after a soaking rain the leaves are soft and quiet underfoot, the best still-hunting conditions of the fall. Ease along slowly, a few steps and a long look, through the oak flats and along field edges, where hungry deer will be making up for a day of lying low. Fresh scrapes freshened after the rain are worth an evening sit.

Fishing outlook: Fair

The barometer has fallen fast since yesterday and keeps sliding slowly through today, which can set off a short feed, but the solid overcast, the rising wind and an afternoon no warmer than about 38 will slow Craighead Forest Lake and Lake Frierson. Best times: late morning, before the heavier rain around midafternoon, and the last hour before sunset at 4:49 if the weather breaks.

Today’s tip: if you can slip away between the parade and the meal, keep it short and slow. Crappie should be suspended near brush piles and the old creek channels in deeper water; a small minnow under a slip float, set just above the cover, needs no casting skill and little patience in the cold.

Tomorrow, Friday (estimate: Good). A steady barometer, a light chop in the morning from a 10-mile-an-hour breeze, and an afternoon near 47 under cloud make Friday the better day on the water. Best times: late morning through midafternoon, as the water warms a little, and the last hour before sunset at 4:49.

Tomorrow’s tip: Thursday’s rain will muddy the inflows, and that runoff carries food; bass and catfish often stack up just where stained water meets clearer water. Work a slow-rolled spinnerbait or a jig along that line, or along the rocks of the dam, which hold the day’s warmth longest. A morning on the lake is a pleasant alternative to the Black Friday crowds.
