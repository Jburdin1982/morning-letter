The moon is a waning gibbous, 88 percent lit, coming up a little later each evening now and climbing high by the small hours; it reaches its last quarter around the first days of December; Tuesday, Dec. 1, is also the first day of what weather forecasters call meteorological winter.

In field and woods: pecans are coming down in the yards and along the old fencerows, and the ones that drop on their own, with the husks split wide, are the ripe ones; spread them out in a dry place for a couple of weeks and they will crack cleaner and taste sweeter. At the feeders, watch for the winter visitors that come down from the North about now: dark-eyed juncos, the little slate-gray birds the old folks called snowbirds, hopping on the ground beneath the feeder, and white-throated sparrows, whose thin whistle sounds like someone singing “Old Sam Peabody, Peabody.” With a mild day and a hard rain behind it, this is a good afternoon to drain and coil the garden hoses, empty the birdbath of leaves, and bring in the last tender pots before the colder nights of December.

Weather lore, not a forecast: wild persimmons are ripe and soft after the frost, and in the Ozarks and the hill country the old custom is to split a seed and read the kernel inside: a spoon shape means plenty of snow to shovel, a fork a mild winter, and a knife a winter of cold that cuts like a blade. Gather a few at a fencerow and see what your own trees are saying.

Black Friday crowds keep Jonesboro shops busy, and the small stores get their turn today

Some Northeast Arkansas shoppers were out before sunrise Friday for Black Friday, the day after Thanksgiving that traditionally opens the Christmas shopping season. Christy Castaneda of Paragould drove to Jonesboro early, got into Kohl’s before its morning coupon giveaway, and said she found good prices at Target, HomeGoods and TJ Maxx. Drew Kellums, vice president of sales at Gamble Home, the furniture and appliance store, said it was too early in the day to judge but that the store had seen more early shoppers than in past years.

The locally owned shops said the weekend matters even more to them. At Delta T Boutique, owned by Holly Koen and her mother, Kay Noel, customers began lining up at about 4:30 Thanksgiving afternoon, and Koen called it “our busiest time of the year.” Andrew Brown, manager of Gearhead Uptown, the outdoor clothing and gear store, said Black Friday alone brings in more than a quarter of the store’s business for the month. Cherish Meacham, a co-owner of Gamers Haven, said the store stocks extra inventory for the season, and Maria Shaver, owner of Market Bliss, said opening while the big-box stores were closed brought a rush of shoppers.

Today is Small Business Saturday, the national day for shopping at independent stores (events are listed under “Around town” below). And one more word from the holiday: a Jonesboro master plumber, Cody Tubbs of Knuckles and Son Plumbing, says the day after Thanksgiving is the busiest of the year for clogged drains. His advice is to scrape leftovers into the trash, feed a garbage disposal slowly with plenty of running water, and keep eggshells out of it, because they collect in the curved pipe under the sink and harden into a blockage.

Monday is the last day to vote early in the Ward 5 and Lake City runoffs

Early voting for Tuesday’s two Craighead County runoff elections reopened Friday after the Thanksgiving break, and Monday, Nov. 30, is the last chance to vote early. By the end of Wednesday, 315 people had voted in the first two days, including 128 on Wednesday.

In Jonesboro, voters in Ward 5 are choosing between City Council member L.J. Bryant, who holds Position 3, and Patti Lack; Bryant led the Nov. 3 field with 38 percent to Lack’s 29 percent. In Lake City, Daniel Haynes, who drew 38 percent, faces Jackie Davis, who drew 36 percent, for mayor; the incumbent mayor, Cameron Tate, finished third and is out. A runoff is needed in Arkansas city races when no candidate wins a majority.

Any registered voter living in Ward 5 or inside the Lake City limits may vote, whether or not they voted on Nov. 3. Early voting runs 8 a.m. to 5 p.m. Monday at the Craighead County Election Annex, 315 W. Jefferson Ave., Jonesboro, and the Eastern Annex, 113 Cobean Blvd., Lake City. On Tuesday the polls are open 7:30 a.m. to 7:30 p.m., with Ward 5 voting at Valley View Church of Christ, 4500 Southwest Drive, and Lake City at the Eastern Annex. Results Tuesday night will be unofficial until certified.

Trial set in February for Caraway woman accused of forging a custody order

A Caraway woman accused of giving a school resource officer a fake court order, in what police describe as an attempt to interfere with a court-ordered custody arrangement, is scheduled for trial Feb. 1 to 12 in Craighead County Circuit Court. Ashley Jordan Davis, 36, is charged with second-degree forgery, a Class C felony, and criminal attempt, a Class D felony.

Police say that on Oct. 9 Davis showed the officer paperwork, said to be from a court in Dunklin County, Mo., stating that she had sole physical custody. Officials with that court later said the order did not come from them and that the judge had not signed it.

District Judge Tommy Fowler set bond at $100,000, with a GPS ankle monitor and no contact with the victims if she is released; as of Nov. 19 she had not posted it. A scheduling order filed that day sets a motion hearing for Jan. 14 and a plea day for Jan. 29. The charges are allegations; she has not been convicted.

From the State

State Republican chairman steps aside after November losses

Joseph Wood, who has led the Republican Party of Arkansas since August 2023, says he will not seek another term as chairman. Mr. Wood, a former Washington County judge and former state secretary of Transformation and Shared Services, said he has “made a decision to pursue another challenge in my career” and that his “heart for Arkansas and the Republican Party is unwavering.”

His announcement follows a rough Nov. 3 general election for the party at the legislative level. Republicans lost 10 state House seats and one state Senate seat, leaving the 100-member House at 70 Republicans and 30 Democrats and the 35-member Senate with at least 27 Republicans and at least seven Democrats; one Senate seat, District 18, will be filled in a Dec. 15 special election. Republicans did keep Sen. Tom Cotton’s U.S. Senate seat, all four U.S. House seats and all seven statewide constitutional offices.

The party’s state committee will choose a new chair at its Dec. 5 meeting. Julie Harris of Springdale, a former party secretary and past president of the National Federation of Republican Women, has the backing of Gov. Sarah Huckabee Sanders; Ms. Harris said the party “did not get our voters to the polls.” Christian Olson, a Little Rock real estate investor who lost the March primary for land commissioner, is also running, and Jonathan Barnett of Siloam Springs, a former state representative and highway commissioner, is said by both to be in the race, though he did not respond to the newspaper. On the other side, the Democratic State Committee will consider re-electing its chairman, Marcus Jones of Little Rock, on Dec. 12.

Oklahoma and poultry companies ask appeals court to wipe out Illinois River ruling

The State of Oklahoma and 11 Northwest Arkansas poultry companies jointly asked the 10th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals in Denver on Wednesday to set aside a federal judge’s ruling in their 21-year-old fight over pollution in the Illinois River, which rises in Northwest Arkansas and flows into eastern Oklahoma.

U.S. District Judge Gregory Frizzell in Tulsa had found the companies liable for phosphorus washing off poultry litter that their contract growers spread on farmland in the watershed. His final judgment would put a court-appointed special master in charge of the cleanup for 30 years, with no cap on what the companies could be made to pay.

The two sides have since settled. The companies would pay almost $44 million for river restoration, lawyers’ fees and other costs, plant natural buffer strips to slow runoff, and reduce the litter applied to growers’ land. They argue the settlement cannot stand alongside the judgment; Judge Frizzell has already refused a similar request, most recently on Nov. 18. Blake Rollins, president of The Poultry Federation, a trade group for the industry, said the deal would let farm families move past the lawsuit. If the appeals court declines, the companies’ appeal goes on.

UA trustees meet in Harrison to weigh National Park College merger and a rule on donor deals

The University of Arkansas System Board of Trustees meets Tuesday and Wednesday at North Arkansas College in Harrison, the first time the board has met at the two-year college since it joined the system this year. It is also the board’s first regular meeting since System President Jay Silveria announced he is leaving for Purdue University in January.

Trustees will vote on letting Dr. Silveria negotiate and finalize a merger with National Park College in Hot Springs, whose board voted in late October to join; it would become the system’s 23rd institution, and Dr. Silveria has recommended approval.

They will also consider a policy barring employees and donors from tying money to whether a particular administrator keeps a job. A coach, for example, could not negotiate a buyout waiver that kicks in if the athletic director is fired, and a donor could not pledge a gift on condition that an administrator leaves; the University of Arkansas Foundation and the Razorback Foundation would have to follow it too. The proposal comes amid public criticism of Fayetteville athletic director Hunter Yurachek after several losing football seasons, though board papers do not say what prompted it.

Also on the agenda: voluntary retirements for 49 employees, including 13 at UA Pine Bluff, which is under financial strain, and a $15.5 million project to turn space at a Fayetteville research institute into biomedical laboratories.

Driver charged with murder in Bentonville crash that killed two

A 22-year-old man, Javier Edgardo Reyes Orellana, was arrested on Thanksgiving Day and booked into the Benton County jail on two counts of first-degree murder, three counts of first-degree battery, aggravated assault and fleeing. The charges are allegations.

Police say witnesses saw him driving at high speed on Southwest 14th Street in Bentonville last Saturday afternoon, Nov. 21, and run a red light at Tunbridge Drive. His vehicle struck a car driven by Ashokkmunar Somasundaran, 43, which then hit an e-bike ridden by Jacob Staly, 39. Mr. Staly died at the scene and Mr. Somasundaran died that night at a hospital; Mr. Somasundaran’s two children, who were in his car, were seriously hurt.

Arkansas State Police special agents and troopers investigated the crash and are presenting their case to the Benton County prosecutor. No bond or court date has been reported.

Fired UALR professor seeks to remove attorney general’s office from her lawsuit

Felicia Branch, a former University of Arkansas at Little Rock law professor who was fired over Facebook posts celebrating the September 2025 killing of conservative activist Charlie Kirk, has asked a federal judge in Little Rock to disqualify Attorney General Tim Griffin’s office from defending the university in her free-speech lawsuit.

Ms. Branch, who directed the law school’s Low-Income Taxpayer Clinic, sued the school, Mr. Griffin and two university officials in May, saying her firing violated the First Amendment. Mr. Griffin and Gov. Sanders had publicly called for her dismissal, and a faculty appeals panel later found the firing was an error and a warning would have sufficed. The university argues a public employer may limit an employee’s speech to protect its reputation and operations.

Her attorney, Verona Swanigan, argues that Mr. Griffin’s public remarks were political, that lawyers in his office could be witnesses to them, and that the university might settle while he would not. If the office were removed, the governor could appoint outside counsel. The attorney general’s office had not responded to a request for comment. Ms. Branch seeks reinstatement and damages.

Delta school district builds its own housing to keep teachers

In Lee County, on the Mississippi River in east Arkansas, the school district has turned to building homes to solve a teacher shortage. The district, with 652 students and 50 certified teachers last year, had the state’s greatest demand for qualified teachers of any traditional public district, and Superintendent Micheal Stone says about 64 percent of local housing is low-income, leaving newcomers few places to live.

Using about $3.9 million in leftover federal pandemic-relief school money, the district built four duplexes with eight furnished three-bedroom, two-bath units that rent for $860 a month, utilities and lawn care included. Four teachers hired right after the units opened in 2024 said the housing was why they came, and a unit opening in December will let a long-distance commuter stay on.

Mr. Stone says keeping veteran teachers is still hard because the district has the state’s lowest property-tax millage and can pay little above the state minimum. The state Board of Education returned the district to local control in April after about seven years under state authority.

From the Nation

Macy’s parade steps off this morning as the East Coast dries out

The 100th Macy’s Thanksgiving Day Parade, postponed from Thursday for the first time in its history because of the powerful coastal storm, steps off this morning at 8:30 Eastern (7:30 Jonesboro time) in New York City. It will follow its usual 2½-mile route from the Upper West Side down Central Park West and Sixth Avenue to Macy’s flagship store on 34th Street, and NBC will carry it live, with streaming on Peacock. Organizers chose to move the whole parade rather than fly the giant balloons in gusts forecast near 70 mph; today’s forecast calls for partly cloudy skies, temperatures near 49 degrees and winds around 10 mph. Organizers say the same balloons and floats and most of the scheduled performers will take part, and nearly every visiting marching band found a way to stay in the city. Balloon crews have had about a day to get ready instead of the usual weeks, and more than 1,000 New York police officers were called in to work.

Elsewhere, the cleanup from the Thanksgiving storm went on Friday. Power outages, which peaked at more than 900,000 homes and businesses on Thursday, had fallen to about 264,000 by Friday evening, according to the tracking site PowerOutage.us, with Pennsylvania still hardest hit, followed by Missouri and New Jersey. Utilities in Pennsylvania warned that some customers could wait into Sunday. Virginia Gov. Abigail Spanberger declared a state of emergency, joining New Jersey and New York. Air travel was still unsettled on Friday, with about 1,600 delays and more than 500 cancellations by the count of the flight-tracking service FlightAware, and the National Weather Service expected lingering showers and patches of freezing rain in the Northeast through today.

Hackers broke into the Air Force’s main cloud system, report says

Hackers have broken into Cloud One, the U.S. Air Force’s flagship cloud computing platform, according to a report Friday by the Reuters news agency citing three people familiar with the matter. Launched in 2017, Cloud One combines computing power rented from Microsoft, Amazon, Oracle and Google’s parent company to run Air Force programs for personnel, pay, logistics and maintenance. It also hosts the system that pulls together air-defense radar data across North America and one that tracks bombs and missiles at more than 200 Air Force locations around the world.

Investigators discovered the intrusion within about the past month while upgrading the system, the report said, and U.S. Cyber Command, the military’s computer-warfare branch, has been called in to assess the damage. Updates to programs running on the platform have been frozen and some users have lost administrator access. It is not known who the hackers are, how far they got or whether any data was stolen. The Air Force said it “does not comment on the security posture, vulnerabilities or operational status of specific networks and systems.” It is the latest in a string of recently disclosed break-ins at federal agencies, including two at the FBI, one at the Treasury Department and one affecting at least two spy agencies. (single source: Reuters)

Civil-liberties group sues to stop the government unmasking ICE critics online

The American Civil Liberties Union has gone to federal court in Washington to stop what it calls a secret grand jury investigation aimed at identifying people who anonymously criticize Immigration and Customs Enforcement, or ICE, on social media. A grand jury is a panel of citizens that hears evidence in private to decide whether to bring criminal charges, and its subpoenas, or orders to hand over information, are normally secret. The lawsuit, filed on behalf of four unnamed users, says prosecutors in the office of Jeanine Pirro, the U.S. attorney for the District of Columbia, have sent grand jury subpoenas to Meta, Bluesky and X demanding the identities of account holders, and that whatever the companies turn over goes to ICE investigators. One plaintiff posted “Burn ICE to the ground” on Bluesky after a shooting involving ICE in New York; another, a college student, cursed at the Homeland Security Department, ICE and President Trump on X.

The ACLU says it has found 35 such subpoenas tied to an effort Ms. Pirro’s office calls “Investigation 18” and believes there are many more. It says the government turned to grand juries after people challenged hundreds of earlier subpoenas the Homeland Security Department issued on its own, which the department withdrew. The suit names Ms. Pirro, Attorney General Todd Blanche and Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin and calls the inquiry a “bad-faith fishing expedition” that violates free-speech rights. Chief U.S. District Judge James Boasberg ordered the case unsealed. The government must respond by Dec. 14, and a hearing is set for Jan. 6. Neither the department nor Ms. Pirro’s office commented.

Online Black Friday spending headed past $13 billion

Americans spent $9.7 billion online on Black Friday through 6:30 p.m. Eastern, 12.8 percent more than a year earlier, according to Adobe, the software company that tracks sales at major online stores, and the full day was expected to reach $13 billion to $13.4 billion. That is more than the $12.9 billion Adobe forecast in September, a sign that bargain hunters are spending despite inflation and gasoline prices pushed up by the war with Iran. Thanksgiving Day itself set a record of $7.1 billion online, and phones accounted for a record 63 percent of purchases.

Adobe expects $6.1 billion in online spending today and $6.4 billion Sunday, and predicts Cyber Monday will again be the biggest online shopping day of the year, at $15.1 billion. Over the five days from Thanksgiving through Monday it projects $47.5 billion, up 7.4 percent. Salesforce, another firm that tracks shopping, measures sales differently and projected $18.7 billion in U.S. online sales for Black Friday, up 4 percent. Final Black Friday figures are expected today.

Los Angeles schools switch off Google’s AI answers; Google warns of losing search

The Los Angeles Unified School District, the nation’s second-largest with about half a million students, has quietly turned off the artificial-intelligence summaries that Google puts at the top of search results on school-issued devices, making it the largest district so far to push back against the company’s AI features in classrooms. The district said the change keeps students’ experience “age-appropriate and safe.” It had already turned off Google’s “AI Mode” for students this fall and in September banned generative AI on student devices for a year while it writes rules. Teachers and staff still have the features.

Google says AI and search can no longer be separated. “Search and AI are deeply integrated,” said Chris Phillips, its vice president for education, and under the company’s policy schools that block its AI tools would see a message that search is unavailable. The district’s chief information officer, Jack Kelanic, answered that it is not bound to Google and could “change the default search engine literally overnight.” Other districts, including Fairfax County, Virginia, have used add-on software to strip out the summaries, and hundreds of Google employees have signed a petition asking the company to let schools turn off AI without losing search.

First tests find no lead in water after the Iowa explosives blast

The Environmental Protection Agency said Friday its first laboratory results found no detectable lead in 38 samples from public water supplies near the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, in southeastern Iowa, where the Army on Wednesday blew up about five tons of old, unstable lead azide, an explosive used in detonators that had been stored there since 2009. Thirty straight hours of air monitoring after the blast also found no airborne lead, and early soil and water results showed lead levels no higher than before the detonation. Federal officials told nearby residents they could resume normal activities.

The work is not finished. The EPA has collected more than 3,200 air, soil, dust and water samples from before and after the blast, and as of Friday had completed 88 percent of follow-up sampling at homes tested beforehand. Outside experts cautioned that the early results are a single snapshot and should be followed by more testing. The agency is posting results online and expects more updates in the coming days.

Florida gets federal disaster aid after record November rains

President Trump approved a major disaster declaration for Florida on Thanksgiving Day and a second one Friday after weeks of record-setting rain flooded neighborhoods along the state’s Atlantic coast and in central Florida. A disaster declaration lets the Federal Emergency Management Agency, or FEMA, pay for debris removal, emergency work and, in some counties, grants and low-cost loans to families for temporary housing and home repairs not covered by insurance. Friday’s declaration extends help to individuals in Brevard and Broward counties after a second round of heavy rain that began Nov. 16; earlier aid covered counties including Palm Beach, Martin, St. Lucie, Indian River and Okeechobee. Mr. Trump said Florida would receive about $100 million in federal disaster aid in all, including money tied to Hurricane Isaias in October. Residents are told to file insurance claims first, then apply at DisasterAssistance.gov or 1-800-621-3362.

From Abroad

Kuwait protests to Iran over deadly drone strike as U.S. disables a blockade runner

Kuwait summoned Iran’s ambassador on Friday and handed him a formal protest over Thursday’s drone strike on a Kuwaiti military site that killed two Italian technicians, among the first confirmed European deaths in the war that began in February when the United States and Israel attacked Iran. Deputy Foreign Minister Hamad Al-Mashaan condemned what he called “continued Iranian attacks,” held Iran fully responsible and said Kuwait reserves the right to defend itself. Italy says the two men, employees of Rheinmetall Italia, were maintaining radar antennas for an air-defense system; a Kuwaiti and a Pakistani were also hurt. Iran again denied any role; Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi called the attack “suspicious” and offered to work with Kuwait and Italy to establish what happened. United Nations Secretary-General António Guterres condemned the strike and urged “all parties” to show restraint.

Conservatives sweep Labor from power in Australia’s Victoria

Voters in Victoria, Australia’s second most populous state, which includes Melbourne, threw out the Labor Party government on Saturday after 12 years in power, in what Australian Broadcasting Corp. projections described as a landslide. Explosion beside an Afghan school kills at least six students

Canada’s highest court upholds Quebec’s ban on religious symbols

The Supreme Court of Canada on Friday upheld most of Quebec’s 2019 secularism law, known as Bill 21, which bars public employees in positions of authority, including teachers, police officers and judges, from wearing religious symbols such as Muslim headscarves, Sikh turbans and Jewish skullcaps at work. Chief Justice Richard Wagner wrote that the law is rooted in Quebec’s “legitimate and historical commitment” to a secular state, and a majority found the province validly used the “notwithstanding clause,” a provision of Canada’s constitution that lets a legislature shield a law from parts of the Charter of Rights and Freedoms, even before any court has ruled.

All seven justices struck down one part, a ban on face coverings for members of Quebec’s legislature, saying it violated the right to run for office. Quebec Premier Paul St-Pierre Plamondon called it “a good day for secularism”; Prime Minister Mark Carney said Ottawa respects the ruling and will not use the clause itself. Rights groups said they would keep fighting.

Uganda begins testing a vaccine against Congo’s Ebola strain

Uganda began Africa’s first trial of an mRNA vaccine against Ebola on Friday, testing a shot made by the U.S. company Moderna against the Bundibugyo strain driving the outbreak in neighboring Congo, which has passed 12,000 confirmed cases and 5,900 deaths. No vaccine or treatment is approved for that strain. The early-stage trial at the Uganda Virus Research Institute will enroll about 224 volunteers aged 1 to 65, the first time the vaccine is being tried in children, and will test only whether it is safe and stirs an immune response, not whether it prevents disease. It follows a study in Canada that began in August; the vaccine has also been recommended by a World Health Organization advisory group for a larger trial in Congo, where cases linked to the outbreak have also turned up in Uganda’s capital, Kampala.

Afield & Afloat

Ratings are rule-of-thumb estimates from the forecast, the rut calendar and the sun, not measurements of animal or fish behavior.

Deer outlook: Good

The late rut is still on, and three things help today: a cool dawn near 38, a quiet morning breeze, and a barometer that begins sliding through the day ahead of Sunday’s storm, which tends to put deer on their feet to feed before the weather turns. Best windows: from 6:22 a.m. to about two hours after sunrise at 6:52, and from 2:49 p.m. until sunset at 4:49.

Today’s tip: after two weeks of gun pressure across the region, mature deer are moving mostly in thick cover and at the edges of light. Zone 4 bowhunters should set up on a well-used trail between a bedding thicket and whatever green food remains, a winter wheat field or a clover plot, rather than out in the open. Bucks are still checking does in the late rut, so a soft doe bleat or a few short grunts every half hour can coax a cruising buck the last 20 yards. Play the wind first, as always: hunt a stand only when the breeze carries your scent away from where you expect the deer.

Tomorrow, Sunday (estimate: Poor). Rain is likely through the morning, and the dawn will be warm, near 56, with wind building to around 20 miles an hour behind the front by evening. Windows, for those who go: 6:22 a.m. to about two hours after sunrise, and 2:49 p.m. to sunset at 4:49.

Tomorrow’s tip: sleep in, or spend a dry hour pulling trail-camera cards and checking straps and ladders, which are slick and treacherous in the rain. Monday looks like the morning to be in the stand: clearing skies, a sharp drop to near 36 at dawn and a rising barometer behind the front are the kind of change that gets deer moving again. Duck hunters, take note: Sunday is the last day of the first segment, and a windy, rainy day is often a good one in the blind.

Fishing outlook: Excellent

This is the best fishing day of the week by the rule of thumb: a steady barometer that eases down slowly ahead of the weather, an afternoon near 63 after a cold week, clouds to soften the light by midday and a gentle breeze to put a ripple on Craighead Forest Lake and Lake Frierson. Best times: late morning through midafternoon, as the sun and the mild air warm the shallows, and the last hour before sunset at 4:49.

Today’s tip: a warm day after a cold snap draws baitfish up onto the flats and points, and the bass follow. A suspending jerkbait twitched two or three times and then left to sit still for a slow count of five or ten, worked over points that drop into deeper water, is a classic late-fall approach. For crappie, set a slip bobber so a small tube jig or minnow hangs just above the brush piles, usually 8 to 15 feet down now, and let the ripple do the work.

Tomorrow, Sunday (estimate: Poor). A falling barometer, heavy rain through the morning and gusty wind behind the front make for a rough day on the water. Best times, if you go between showers: late morning through midafternoon, and the last hour before sunset at 4:49.

Tomorrow’s tip: stay off the water while there is any chance of lightning, and keep in mind that small lakes get choppy fast in a 20-mile-an-hour wind. A rainy Sunday is a fine time to restring reels with fresh line, sharpen or replace dull hooks, and sort the tackle box for the winter crappie season.
