The moon is a hair-thin crescent in the pre-dawn east this morning, and the new moon arrives around Monday, which means the darkest nights of the month are just ahead. The Leonid meteors usually peak around November 17 and 18, and a dark moon will help; the best viewing comes after midnight. The full moon is due around the 24th.

Out in the fields of Craighead and Poinsett counties the combines have mostly stopped, and the stubble is being disked or left for ducks; winter wheat that went in late is just showing a green haze. In the woods the oaks have dropped most of their acorns and the sweetgums and hickories have turned, so the last color of the year hangs on the ridges. Along the creeks, sparrows and juncos are arriving from the north to work the brush piles, and an early frost like this morning's finishes off whatever tender things are left in the garden.

Weather lore, not a forecast: “When the woodpecker is busy in autumn, plenty of cold is coming.” Country people read thick husks, busy squirrels and heavy hickory crops the same way.

Of Local Interest

Jonesboro voters reject the public safety tax

Voters in Jonesboro turned down a proposed public safety tax on Tuesday, 59 percent against and 41 percent for, according to complete but unofficial results reported by Jonesboro Right Now. The tax, which the City Council had placed on the ballot in September, would have paid for the city's police and fire departments. The city now has to find another way to fund them, and no replacement has been announced. Results remain unofficial until the Craighead County Election Commission certifies them.

In Ward 5 of the City Council, no candidate won a majority, so the top two, incumbent L.J. Bryant (38 percent) and challenger Patti Lack (29 percent), go to a runoff on December 1. Two other candidates, Vince Pearcy and James Carl Wilson Jr., were eliminated. In Lake City, a runoff on the same day will pit Daniel Haynes (38 percent) against Jackie Davis (36 percent); Mayor Cameron Tate finished third.

Quorum Court and legislative seats change hands

Democrats unseated four Republican incumbents on the Craighead County Quorum Court, the county's legislative body: Jolene Mullet won District 1, Blake Walker District 3, Roseann Askeland District 5 and Ross Kerley District 6. Republican Doug Formon won the open District 9 seat, and incumbents David Tennison (District 8) and Steve Cline (District 10) won re-election.

In the Arkansas House, Democrat Erika Askeland defeated six-term Republican Jack Ladyman by 109 votes to represent most of Jonesboro, while Republican Johnny Rye kept his seat over Democrat Shamal Carter. Curtis Hitt won the Second Judicial District circuit judge race, Division 4, with 63 percent. Voters also approved, by 63 percent, combining the county collector and treasurer offices, a change that takes effect in 2031.

Brookland bonds will fund $27.6 million in projects

Brookland voters approved restructuring and refinancing the city's bonds so it can pay for $27.555 million in projects without raising taxes. The list includes second phases of the sportsplex and the municipal building, sewer and storm-shelter work, and traffic changes at Highway 49 and School Street and at Highway 49 and Pine Log Road. Mayor Kenneth Jones said the bonds will be sold in January and construction could begin as early as March 2027.

Acme Brick to end Jonesboro production on December 31

Acme Brick will stop making brick at its Jonesboro plant on December 31, affecting 27 employees, the company's human resources director confirmed to NEA Report. An October 23 letter from company president Ed Watson listed Jonesboro among three plants being closed, with Montgomery, Alabama, and Tulsa, citing a need to align operations with market conditions; he wrote that business peaked in 2022 and profits have not recovered. The Jonesboro sales office, yard and distribution operation will stay open.

Eligible full-time workers will receive severance based on years of service, capped at 26 weeks, but must sign a release, and no severance is paid to anyone offered a comparable job elsewhere in the company. Watson said more details on benefits and job-placement help will follow.

Grain and Gather festival honors rice farmers ahead of the Rice Bowl

The Arkansas Rice Federation, led by executive director Kelly Robbins, held the first Grain and Gather festival in downtown Jonesboro on Friday night to thank rice farmers before the second annual Rice Bowl, the Arkansas State game against Louisiana-Monroe. The block party had a kids' zone, live music, rice-inspired dishes from downtown restaurants and a rice-brat eating contest, judged in part by Mayor Harold Copenhaver; the Lambda Chi Alpha fraternity won a $500 prize for the loudest cheering section. A tailgate followed on Saturday. Robbins said she hopes it becomes a Jonesboro tradition.

Probation in a Jonesboro gunfire case

A 21-year-old West Memphis woman pleaded guilty and was sentenced to 36 months of probation on two counts of aggravated assault and two counts of first-degree terroristic threatening, KAIT reported Sunday. Police said she fired a pistol on the 300 block of Reeves Street on May 27 while children were present, then drove off; officers later found her vehicle on East Washington Street, with a handgun, an AR-style pistol and suspected marijuana inside. As part of the sentence she must have no contact with the victim, pay court costs and complete a course toward a GED.

From the State

Republicans lose their House supermajority

Democrats flipped seats in Tuesday's election that cost Republicans their supermajority (a share large enough to pass certain bills alone) in the 100-member Arkansas House for the first time in a decade. Tallies in early reports differed, but by the most recent count Republicans hold about 70 seats and Democrats about 30, according to States Newsroom, and the figures are unofficial. Most appropriations bills need 75 votes, so Republicans will now need some Democratic support to fund state agencies.

House Minority Leader Andrew Collins said more leverage does not mean Democrats will hold up spending bills, while Republican Rep. David Ray warned Democrats not to overplay their hand. The first test comes in the 2027 session, when lawmakers take up the governor's roughly $7 billion budget proposal on Medicaid, SNAP and school vouchers.

Statewide results

Gov. Sarah Huckabee Sanders won a second term with 54 percent, U.S. Sen. Tom Cotton was re-elected with 55 percent over Democrat Hallie Shoffner, and Rep. Rick Crawford took the First Congressional District with 65 percent. Kim Hammer, a Republican, won secretary of state with nearly 56 percent. Statewide Issues 1, 2 and 4 passed and Issue 3 failed. All figures are unofficial until certified.

Jury awards damages over a 2020 protest shooting

A Little Rock jury awarded compensatory and punitive damages to a man who was hit in the face by a police beanbag round during the 2020 protests over police brutality, the Arkansas Democrat-Gazette reported this weekend. The case took six years to reach a verdict. We could not confirm the amounts, and the ruling may be appealed.

Chase and group-home runaways

Arkansas State Police arrested a teenager Saturday after a pursuit of about 100 mph that began in Forrest City after a shooting and ended in a crash on Interstate 40 in North Little Rock; a second suspect was still being sought. Separately, two of four teens who walked away from a Monticello group home Friday night were back in custody by Saturday evening, a state Department of Human Services official said.

From the Nation

Democrats win Congress; Trump shakes up his staff

Democrats won control of both chambers of Congress in Tuesday's midterm elections. CBS News estimates they will hold at least 54 Senate seats and about 256 House seats when the new Congress convenes in January, up from 214 in the House now; more than 20 House races and two Senate races, in Alaska and Mississippi, were still uncalled. Among the notable results, Democrat James Talarico beat Republican Ken Paxton for a Texas Senate seat, and Democrats flipped more than 40 Republican-held House districts. Counting continues, and Alaska's ranked-choice tally is set for November 18.

President Trump said on Sunday that the nation had taken a “giant step back in history,” and said it was “largely true” that Republicans lost because of the Iran war, which he defended. In a 60 Minutes interview airing tonight he said he expects some firings. White House chief of staff Susie Wiles resigned and Stephen Miller, the president's longtime policy adviser, takes over on Monday, November 9. CBS reports that officials do not expect Miller's promotion to ease the immigration crackdown, and that several Justice Department officials have resigned.

Democrats are already planning their agenda. Rep. Robert Garcia, the incoming House Oversight chair, plans investigations of the Trump family's businesses, immigration enforcement and the Epstein matter, and Sen. Elizabeth Warren plans to examine the financial sector as chair of the Senate Banking Committee. Both parties face leadership contests; Senate Democrats are debating whether to replace Chuck Schumer. Trump has called talk of impeachment “extortion” and asked the Justice Department to look into it. (Reported by CBS News; other outlets should be consulted as results are finalized.)

Employers added 165,000 jobs in October

Employers added 165,000 jobs in October, far above the 59,000 economists expected, and the unemployment rate held at 4.1 percent, the Labor Department reported Friday. The strong report raised the odds that the Federal Reserve will keep interest rates high, which weighed on some stocks.

From Abroad

Six killed in a shooting in Krakow, Poland

A 24-year-old Polish man opened fire in Krakow's Main Square, a crowded tourist area in the historic Old Town, on Saturday, killing six people and wounding 15. Police shot and wounded him, and he is in critical condition. Officers later found the bodies of his parents and sister at the family home about 93 miles away; he is also suspected in those deaths. His father legally owned more than 10 guns, and 11 weapons were recovered.

Poland's interior minister said the suspect held “very radical” far-right views, and prosecutors have classified the attack as terrorism. Poland declared two days of national mourning, and one of the two officers who stopped the gunman remains in a coma.

The Iran war: blockade continues, talks paused

The United States and Israel went to war with Iran on February 28. Heavy fighting has largely stopped, but Iran still restricts shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway that carries much of the world's oil, and the U.S. keeps a blockade of Iranian ports. On Saturday U.S. forces disabled a cargo ship, the M/V Huda, in the Gulf of Oman after it allegedly tried to run the blockade, and U.S. Central Command announced a small new task force, Iron Gate, aimed at cutting Iran's overland trade routes. Iran's Revolutionary Guard called it “wordplay.”

Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, said mediated talks have paused since the American elections and that Iran's proposal could reopen the strait within two days if U.S. conditions are met. President Trump told CNN that renewed all-out attacks are “highly unlikely” if talks progress. Secretary of State Marco Rubio is due at a G7 foreign ministers' meeting in Paris on Monday and Tuesday. Tanker explosions reported near the strait have not been independently confirmed.

Ukraine: power outages ahead of winter

Russian missile and drone strikes on Ukraine's energy system overnight left more than 600,000 people without power in the Dnipropetrovsk region and nearly 250,000 customers in the Kharkiv region, with 140 apartment buildings in Zhytomyr losing heat while temperatures hovered near freezing. President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said the death toll from a strike on an aid distribution center in Zaporizhzhia region on Monday had risen to 55. He accused Russia of stepping up attacks ahead of talks with Russia planned for next week.

Train collision near Cairo

One train crashed into the rear of another near Cairo, in the Giza area, killing at least seven people and injuring more than 100, according to CBS News. (Single source; the toll may change.)

Deer outlook: Excellent

Everything lines up for the last day of the youth weekend: the rut is in its peak window, dawn is a frosty 35 degrees after a sharp cool-down from yesterday, the wind is light and the barometer is slipping ahead of a change. Hunt first light at about 6:02 a.m. for roughly two hours, and the evening from 3:00 to 5:00 p.m. Ten to two is realistic for cruising bucks, so a sit through the middle of the day is worth it.

Today's tip: with almost no wind, scent will drift in odd swirls; get to the stand well before light, wear it quietly, and watch the downwind side of field corners and the trails between bedding and food, where bucks trail does. A grunt call and a few rattles from the stand can pull a cruising buck over.

Tomorrow, Monday (estimate: Good): a milder dawn near 41 and a warm 69 in the afternoon, still peak rut, with light wind. Watch 6:03 to about 8 a.m. and 2:59 to 4:59 p.m. Tomorrow's tip: on warm afternoons deer hold in shade until late, so favor the last hour in the evening and the cooler morning hours.

Fishing outlook: Good

For the Crowley's Ridge lakes such as Craighead Forest Lake and Lake Frierson, steady pressure, a slow fall ahead of a change and a warm afternoon near 63 make a good day. Fish late morning through mid-afternoon as the shallows warm, and again in the last hour before sunset at 5:00 p.m.

Today's tip: after the cold snap, crappie will hold deeper around brush piles and docks in 8 to 12 feet; try small minnows or 1/16-ounce jigs under a float and fish slowly. Bass are likely to take a slow spinnerbait or jig along sunny, rocky banks as the afternoon warms.

Tomorrow, Monday (estimate: Good): steady pressure and a warm afternoon near 69; best late morning through mid-afternoon, with another window in the last hour before sunset at 4:59 p.m. Tomorrow's tip: a sunny afternoon should push bream and crappie toward shallow water on the sun side of the lake; shorten your line and move slowly.

Postscript

The Hawthorn Gate.

Every November, old Mr. Pruitt walked the fence line of his hundred acres with a hammer and a coffee can of staples, and every November the neighbors said it was no use. The fence, they pointed out, had been falling down since 1974.

“It is not falling down,” he told his granddaughter that morning, as the frost steamed off the pasture. “It is leaning with dignity.”

She was nine, and she held the can. At the far corner they came to the gate, which was older than the fence, hung on a hinge made from a plow part, and which had not shut properly in anyone’s memory. He lifted it, set it on its hook, and it hung open exactly as far as it always had.

“Why do you fix it if it never works?” she asked.

He considered this longer than a grown man should. “The gate was never for keeping things out,” he said. “It is for letting the right things in. Your grandmother came through this gate. So did the Johnsons’ cow, once, and that was the only one of the two I did not enjoy.”

They stood a while. A flock of juncos, newly come down from the north, worked the hedgerow in a gray-and-white scurry.

“Well,” said Mr. Pruitt, tapping the hinge with the hammer, “that ought to hold until spring.” The gate swung a quarter inch and settled. It had not held in fifty years, and it held that morning, perhaps out of politeness.

Until tomorrow, with good wishes —
