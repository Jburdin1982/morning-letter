Friday is the warmest of the stretch, sunny and about 74 after a dawn near 47, and Saturday stays in the low 70s under a mixing sky with only a slim chance of a shower. The change comes Sunday: showers are likely in the morning and a thunderstorm is possible through the day as a cold front crosses, the best rain chance we have seen in weeks, at better than one in two. The sun rises at 6:43 and sets at 4:52, and the day gives up another minute and a half of light. The moon is waxing gibbous, about two-thirds lit, already up in the eastern sky by mid-afternoon and riding high at dusk; it fattens each evening toward the full Beaver Moon around Tuesday, the 24th, two days before Thanksgiving.

Pecans are falling now across Northeast Arkansas, and a dry autumn has made for easy picking under the trees; the shells are hard and the meat sweet when the nuts rattle in the hull. The same dry spell is hard on anything planted this fall, and a slow, deep soaking for new trees, shrubs and evergreens before Sunday’s rain and the first hard cold of next week is the best insurance against winter burn. With lows near freezing Monday and Tuesday nights, tonight and the weekend are the time to bring in the last potted geraniums, drain and coil the hoses, and dig any sweet potatoes still in the ground.

In the woods the oaks are the last to hold their color, rusty reds and browns on the ridges, while the sweetgums have already dropped their prickly balls along every driveway. Overhead, the first long skeins of snow geese are coming down the Mississippi Flyway, and their high, broken chatter carries a long way on a still evening.

Weather lore, not a forecast: the sailors’ rhyme warns, “Mackerel sky and mares’ tails make tall ships carry low sails,” meaning that thin, high, rippled clouds often run a day or two ahead of a storm. Watch the western sky Saturday afternoon for exactly that sort of sign ahead of Sunday’s front.

Excavator tumbles into traffic during Jonesboro police chase

A Jonesboro man faces three felonies and five misdemeanors after an excavator slid off the trailer he was towing and landed in oncoming traffic on Matthews Avenue during a police chase Tuesday, Nov. 17. Police allege Jacob Lee Lawrence was speeding west on Johnson Avenue in a white Ford F-150 pulling a trailer carrying the excavator. They say he passed cars in the center turn lane and rear-ended another vehicle near Main Street as he merged back, then drove off. The other driver went to the hospital with neck pain.

Officers found the truck near Gee Street and Washington Avenue and turned on lights and siren, but police say the driver did not stop. According to the affidavit, he ran the stop sign at Meadowbrook Drive and Matthews Avenue and swung onto the sidewalk turning onto Matthews, which is when the excavator fell into the oncoming lane. The pickup dragged the trailer a short way until it broke loose, continued west, turned onto Ferrell Street and stopped near Courtney Cove, where officers arrested him.

The charges are fleeing, a Class C felony; two Class D felonies for leaving the scene of a crash, one with injury and one with $10,000 or more in damage; and misdemeanors for reckless driving, driving on a suspended or revoked license, inadequate insurance, careless driving and failure to transfer a title. Special Judge Barbara Halsey found probable cause Wednesday and set bond at $250,000. He is due in Craighead County Circuit Court Jan. 25. The charges are allegations; he is presumed innocent.

Gun allegedly drawn in dispute over duck-hunting land

A man is charged with two felonies after a quarrel over access to leased duck-hunting ground near Craighead County roads 213 and 209, outside Jonesboro, on Sunday, Nov. 15 — six days before Saturday's duck season opener. The man who leases the land told them he was there with a hunting group when Terry Alfred Moody drove up in a white pickup, said he would be closing gates on the road, and told the group they would have to park about two and a half miles away.

After an argument, the affidavit says, Moody pulled a revolver and pointed it at the leaseholder while three children sat in a side-by-side a few feet behind him; the man pushed the gun back into the truck. Moody is charged with aggravated assault and first-degree endangering the welfare of a minor, both Class D felonies. A milestone for a survivor of the August pedestrian crashes

Christy Eppes, one of several pedestrians struck in back-to-back crashes in Jonesboro on Aug. 13, has begun speaking again after months of setbacks. The crashes also claimed the life of Belinda Adams, 63. Since August, Mrs. Eppes has suffered strokes, a near-fatal lung infection and a severe colon complication, and at one point her family weighed surgery against hospice care.

Her husband, Marty Eppes, said a nurse at the long-term acute care hospital in Searcy where she is staying called to say she was suddenly forming sentences. He found her sitting up and alert; she said his name, gave her own name and birthdate, and lifted the left arm the strokes had weakened. "I knew she was back," he said. She still has a tracheostomy tube, is fed by tube or IV and is bedridden, but is preparing to begin physical therapy. He credits a first responder at the scene and the intensive-care staff at St. Bernards with saving her life.

Michael Coleman, accused of causing the crashes, faces several charges including first-degree homicide, which could carry a life sentence on conviction. He remains in the Craighead County Detention Center on a $5 million bond, with motion and plea dates set for Dec. 3 and Dec. 18. The charges against him have not been proven in court. Mr. Eppes asked neighbors to keep praying, saying his wife's recovery is "just getting started."

From the State

Arkansas justice faces Senate questions for federal appeals seat

Arkansas Supreme Court Justice Cody Hiland went before the U.S. Senate Judiciary Committee in Washington on Wednesday as President Donald Trump’s nominee for the 8th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals, the federal appeals court that hears cases from Arkansas and six other Midwestern states. Hiland, who has sat on the state’s high court since July 2023, is a former U.S. attorney for the Eastern District of Arkansas, a former local prosecutor and a former chairman of the Arkansas Republican Party. He would replace Judge Bobby Shepherd, who is taking senior (semi-retired) status. Trump named him last week after withdrawing his earlier pick for the seat, U.S. District Judge Lee Rudofsky, whose nomination had stalled without the backing of Arkansas’ two senators.

Asked by Democratic Sen. Richard Blumenthal who won the 2020 presidential election, Hiland said Joe Biden won the Electoral College and was certified the winner, but declined to say who won the popular vote, calling it a political question. Sen. Dick Durbin, the committee’s top Democrat, asked about the recent arrest of a former Arkansas Supreme Court staff director accused of tampering with bar exam scores from 2020 to 2025; Hiland said he learned of the investigation in May, had no role in it, and that officials are still working out “the universe of the problem.” He also walked back an August remark to law students that he had been “belligerent” in turning down a lower federal judgeship, saying “resolute” was the better word.

Republican Sens. Tom Cotton and John Boozman both praised him, and Attorney General Tim Griffin and 10 other state attorneys general wrote in support. Durbin voiced “deep concerns” and accused Republicans of rushing judges through before Democrats take control of the Senate in January. No committee vote date has been announced.

$261 million for town water and sewer systems, with millions for Northeast Arkansas

Gov. Sarah Huckabee Sanders announced Wednesday that the Arkansas Natural Resources Commission, the state board that finances water projects, has approved $261.1 million in grants and low-cost loans for 75 drinking water and wastewater projects serving more than 740,000 Arkansans. The money comes mostly through federal-state revolving loan funds, plus state grant and bond programs; some loans include “principal forgiveness,” meaning part need not be repaid. “We’re working hard to make sure every Arkansan … has the resources they need to live safely and thrive,” the governor said.

Northeast Arkansas shares in it. Black Oak in Craighead County gets a $1 million grant for sewer collection lines. Paragould Municipal Utilities gets an $8.5 million loan for wastewater plant upgrades, and the Lafe Rural Water Distribution District a $1.39 million loan. Rector in Clay County receives about $3 million for water system improvements, most of it forgivable. In Mississippi County, Burdette gets a $3.5 million grant toward a new water treatment plant, Keiser about $1.8 million for meters and plant upgrades, and Manila a $237,800 loan. Lepanto in Poinsett County receives a $2.36 million grant for a new treatment plant, the Cross County Rural Water Association $9.8 million, Cherry Valley $721,900, and Batesville a $17.5 million loan for its wastewater plant.

Arkansas again gets an F for premature births, as poll shows voters want action

Arkansas earned an F for its premature birth rate for the fourth straight year on the March of Dimes report card, an annual ranking of mother and baby health released Tuesday by the national nonprofit. Nearly 12% of Arkansas babies are born early, against 10.4% nationally, and the state ranked 51st of 52 (the states, Washington, D.C., and Puerto Rico) for infant deaths, at 8.3 per 1,000 births; only Mississippi did worse. Arkansas remains the only state that has not extended Medicaid coverage for new mothers from 60 days to a full year after birth, which the report urges and which Gov. Sanders has opposed. Her spokesman, Sam Dubke, pointed instead to the Healthy Moms, Healthy Babies Act, a 2025 law that speeds Medicaid for pregnant women and directs $45 million a year to maternal and infant health.

A poll released Wednesday by Heartland Forward, a Bentonville research group, found more than 90% of 500 likely Arkansas voters want the state to invest more in maternal and infant care, with majorities in both parties, the highest support of the 10 states surveyed. Some four in five worried about hospitals closing labor and delivery units; eight have closed since 2020, and only 22 of the state’s 75 counties still have one. A postpartum Medicaid bill passed the House in 2025 but died in a Senate committee. Lawmakers return for the 2027 session on Jan. 11.

Judge rejects poultry companies’ deal over Illinois River pollution

A federal judge in Tulsa, U.S. District Judge Gregory Frizzell, on Wednesday rejected a proposed settlement between Oklahoma and 11 poultry companies with operations in Northwest Arkansas in a pollution case that has run since 2005. Oklahoma sued, saying chicken litter spread as fertilizer by the companies’ contract growers was feeding algae in the Illinois River, which flows from Northwest Arkansas into eastern Oklahoma. Frizzell ruled against the companies in 2023 and last December ordered remedies both sides called unworkable, including a court-appointed overseer for 30 years with no cap on cleanup costs and tight limits on spreading litter that the companies called a near-ban.

The rejected deal would have had the companies pay almost $44 million for river restoration and legal costs, fund buffer strips to catch runoff and cut litter use. In his order, Frizzell said the companies had dragged out the case for years and suggested Oklahoma’s timing in settling reflected politics more than the merits. The companies have said they will go to the 10th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals in Denver to seek approval of the settlement. For now his penalty order stands, a worry for poultry growers across the watershed.

From the Nation

Jeffries chosen by House Democrats to be the next speaker

House Democrats on Wednesday nominated Rep. Hakeem Jeffries of New York, their current leader, to be speaker of the House when the new Congress opens in January. The vote in a closed-door caucus meeting was 251 to 11. The speaker presides over the House and sets which bills come to the floor; Democrats won control of the chamber in this month's midterm elections and are on track to hold more than 250 seats, comfortably above the 218 votes needed in the formal floor election.

No one ran against Mr. Jeffries, but a handful of incoming progressive members voted no, saying they wanted firm commitments on Medicare for All (a government-run health plan for everyone), immigration enforcement, campaign finance and Gaza. If elected in January, Mr. Jeffries would be the first Black speaker in the nation’s history.

The caucus also filled its other top posts: Rep. Katherine Clark of Massachusetts will be majority leader, the first woman in that job; Rep. Pete Aguilar of California will be whip, the lawmaker who counts and rounds up votes; Rep. Joe Neguse of Colorado won the assistant leader post; and Rep. Ted Lieu of California will chair the caucus. On the Senate side, Sen. Chuck Schumer of New York faces a challenge from Sen. Chris Van Hollen of Maryland for majority leader; Mr. Schumer says he has the votes for an early December election.

Officials tell Congress the string of outages was not an attack; Iran threats “dramatically increased”

The government’s top security officials told the House Homeland Security Committee on Wednesday that they have found no evidence that the recent run of technology failures, which included last Friday’s nationwide halt of air traffic, a network disruption at the National Security Agency on Monday and outages at several large banks, was the work of hackers or a foreign government. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said he was “98% confident” foreign adversaries were not behind them, though he added he had “absolutely not” seen so many outages in so short a span. Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton called them separate incidents tied to “maintenance and technical changes,” and FBI Deputy Director Christopher Raia said there was “no indication whatsoever” of malicious cyber activity.

The air traffic halt, only the second nationwide ground stop since Sept. 11, 2001, has been traced to work by the Federal Aviation Administration’s telecommunications contractor, L3Harris Technologies, which was replacing copper lines with fiber at a remote site when a router switch flooded other routers and overloaded the system. Rep. Dan Goldman, a New York Democrat, accused Mr. Clayton of dodging whether the NSA disruption came from the agency’s own software work.

Lawmakers also pressed the officials about reported Iranian plots, including possible use of a “dirty bomb” (a conventional explosive that scatters radioactive material) or chemical weapons against senior officials. Mr. Clayton said Iranian threats inside and outside the country have “dramatically increased”; Mr. Raia called much of the reporting “largely aspirational” but said it is taken seriously. Officials did not confirm any active plot, and the White House has said it has no evidence of a credible threat to the homeland. Separately, in a television interview from China on Wednesday, President Trump said there is “indication” Iran is exploring chemical and biological weapons, and that if an attack comes the U.S. will “wipe them out”; he did not offer evidence for the claim.

Senate Republicans unsure they can pass $350 billion in war money before Democrats take over

Senate Majority Leader John Thune, a South Dakota Republican, said Wednesday it is an “open question” whether Republicans have the votes to pass a roughly $350 billion Pentagon funding package on their own before the year ends. The money was requested by the Defense Department, and the war with Iran is a large part of the backdrop. Republicans are weighing “budget reconciliation,” a special process that lets certain spending bills pass the Senate with a simple party-line majority instead of the usual 60 votes.

Some Republicans worry that Democrats, who oppose the Iran war and take control of the Senate in January, would hold back defense funds once in charge. Deputy Defense Secretary Steve Feinberg made the case to Republican senators at a closed-door lunch on Wednesday, but Mr. Thune said senators still want the total confirmed and justified. Budget Committee Chairman Ron Johnson of Wisconsin said the process probably will not start this week. Republicans are also cool to Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy’s request for $50 billion to overhaul the air traffic control system. The Senate has about three weeks left in session before its winter break.

Record rain floods South Florida; storm may become Tropical Storm Kyle

Two days of torrential rain have flooded parts of South Florida and the Space Coast east of Orlando. Plantation, a suburb of Fort Lauderdale, recorded 16.4 inches, Hollywood 13.8 inches and Fort Lauderdale 12.2 inches; drivers abandoned cars in standing water, water entered homes, and some neighborhoods in Cocoa and Rockledge were cut off as the St. Johns River near Cocoa rose above its record level. No deaths have been reported.

Forecasters expect another 6 to 12 inches in places on Thursday and Friday. The National Hurricane Center, which tracks tropical weather, gives the system a 60% chance of becoming a tropical or subtropical storm this weekend as it moves north off the coast; if named, it would be Kyle. It is expected to bring nor’easter-like wind, beach erosion and coastal flooding from Daytona Beach to Charleston on Saturday, the Carolinas and Virginia on Sunday, and possibly the New England coast on Monday, though the exact track remains uncertain.

Trump, Xi and Putin meet together for the first time; talk of nuclear arms but no deal announced

President Trump, Chinese leader Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin held the first-ever three-way meeting of their countries’ leaders on Thursday in Shenzhen, China, on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit, a yearly gathering of Pacific Rim economies. After brief public remarks, the three met privately for about an hour and a half. No joint statement was given to reporters, and the White House released no account of the talks; Mr. Trump left for the airport soon afterward and is flying home.

In his opening remarks, Mr. Trump said the three nations are “uniquely positioned to discuss how to reduce the number of nuclear weapons on earth,” including keeping them from Iran, and said they were “really making history.” Mr. Xi, according to Chinese state media, said nuclear weapons should be “completely banned and completely destroyed” and that good relations among large countries are “a necessity”; China has long refused to join arms-limitation talks with the U.S. and Russia, and its arsenal of roughly 600 warheads is far smaller than theirs. Mr. Putin said the three could strengthen global stability and named energy and the war in Ukraine among the subjects.

The Kremlin later released a summary it said all three had agreed to: it says they reaffirmed a commitment to “strategic stability,” agreed there “can be no winners in a nuclear war,” backed reform of the United Nations, pledged to raise oil and gas output and criticized the International Criminal Court (the war-crimes court in The Hague), and that nuclear-risk talks will continue. The U.S. has not confirmed that account.

Before the three-way session, Mr. Trump met Mr. Putin for about 40 minutes, their first meeting since their Alaska talks in 2025; a Kremlin aide called it “a good meeting” focused largely on Ukraine, and Mr. Trump called Mr. Putin a friend. He also met Mr. Xi for about an hour; Mr. Trump said he would press China to buy more American farm goods, while Chinese state media said Mr. Xi raised Taiwan, the self-governed island China claims, and urged the U.S. to avoid “turbulence” there. World court finds Myanmar forces acted with genocidal intent against the Rohingya

The International Court of Justice, the United Nations’ highest court, on Thursday delivered its long-awaited judgment in the case the West African nation of The Gambia brought in 2019 accusing Myanmar of genocide against the Rohingya, a largely Muslim minority. Early reports of the ruling say the court found that Myanmar’s security forces killed thousands of Rohingya civilians, committed widespread sexual violence and extreme violence against children during a 2017 military crackdown, and that the evidence supports a reasonable inference that the forces acted with intent to destroy a substantial part of the group. The court’s president, Judge Yuji Iwasawa of Japan, said some 10,000 Rohingya were killed.

The court also found that Myanmar failed in its duty to prevent genocide. The crackdown drove more than 730,000 Rohingya into neighboring Bangladesh, and the UN says more than 1.3 million remain displaced, most in camps around Cox’s Bazar. The Gambia asked the court to order Myanmar to punish those responsible, pay reparations and allow the Rohingya to return safely; how the court ruled on those requests, and the judges’ vote, were not yet clear in early reports. Myanmar, now run by its military, has always denied genocide, saying it was responding to attacks on police posts by Rohingya militants. The court’s rulings are binding, but it has no means of enforcing them.

Denmark approves deal widening U.S. military role in Greenland

Denmark’s parliament voted 109 to 0 on Thursday to approve a security agreement with the United States over Greenland, the vast Arctic island that is a self-governing part of the Danish kingdom. Greenland’s own parliament approved it earlier this month without opposition. The deal, signed in New York in September after months of tension over President Trump’s push to bring Greenland under American control, expands a 1951 defense pact: it lets the U.S. modernize Pituffik Space Base, open new “defense areas” at Narsarsuaq and Mestersvig and add more later, while Greenland stays under Danish sovereignty.
