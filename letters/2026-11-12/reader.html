A thin young moon, only three days past new, sets early this evening in the southwest, so the nights are dark and the stars sharp after the front clears the air; first quarter comes around next Tuesday. The day is now ten hours and twenty minutes long, and we lose nearly two minutes of it every day.

This is the week the fields along the St. Francis and the Cache turn to stubble and water. Rice ground is being flooded for the ducks that will be welcome on the 21st, and the cotton pickers are working the last of the late fields. On Crowley’s Ridge the oaks have gone to rust and the sweetgums to wine, and a good wind will bring most of them down by Thanksgiving. Pecans are dropping in the yards; gather them before the squirrels do, and dry them a week or two in a shallow box before cracking.

In the garden, the front is the signal to finish: cut back the asparagus ferns, pull the frost-blackened peppers, and spread a few inches of chopped leaves over the bare beds. Collards, kale and turnip greens will shrug off a frost and taste the sweeter for it. Indoors, it is a fine week to check the furnace filter and the smoke-alarm batteries before the heater runs in earnest.

Weather lore, not a forecast: the old fishermen held that “when the wind is in the north, the skillful fisher goes not forth.” Behind tonight’s front, the lakes may well prove them right on Friday.

Of Local Interest

Park camping ban leaves the homeless few places to turn

Jonesboro’s new rule against camping in city parks is drawing attention to how few places the city’s homeless residents have to sleep as cold weather settles in. On Nov. 5 the City Council voted 10 to 0 for Ordinance 26-038, which forbids putting up tents or shelters, or bedding down overnight, in any city park or city-owned parking lot outside a designated camping area; Craighead Forest Park holds the designated campground. Police or code-enforcement officers may remove a camper’s belongings, and a first offense carries a fine of at least $100.

Dr. Charles Coleman, who has represented Ward 2 on the council for nearly 15 years, said the matter was raised months ago over safety concerns, including trouble around parked cars and people camping without permission. Maj. Jon-Phil Winter, who commands the Salvation Army on Cate Avenue, said he understands those concerns but called homelessness a complicated problem tied to addiction, health and money, and urged the community to help people out of it. The Salvation Army runs Jonesboro’s only emergency shelter: 16 beds, for men only, open nightly from 5 p.m. to 8 a.m. It cannot take women and children because it lacks the staff and money to do so safely.

Neighbors are stepping in. Karleigh Johns, who owns Hunter’s Loaded Lemonade, a food truck named for a late brother, is collecting jackets, gloves, blankets and rain ponchos through Dec. 5, to be handed out that day at the second annual Hunter’s Holiday Market at Gamble’s Shop Local Park, where more than 70 vendors and 10 food trucks have signed up.

Red kettles return Friday

The Salvation Army of Jonesboro begins its yearly Red Kettle Campaign on Friday, Nov. 13, with bell-ringers out through Christmas Eve and a goal of $100,000. The money pays for the charity’s year-round work, including that overnight shelter, community meals and a food pantry; Maj. Winter calls it the group’s biggest fundraiser of the year. Volunteers are needed at about 15 spots across Northeast Arkansas, among them Walmart, Kroger, Hobby Lobby and Belk, in four-hour shifts from 10 a.m. to 2 p.m. or 2 to 6 p.m.; children must ring with an adult. Cash, checks and digital payments are all accepted, and the Angel Tree Christmas program for children opened Thursday.

Cooksey’s Flower Shop closing after 74 years

Cooksey’s Flower Shop, which has made Jonesboro’s corsages, wedding bouquets and holiday arrangements since the early 1950s, is closing for good. The family says the decision came after Henry Cooksey’s wife was diagnosed with cancer, and Mr. Cooksey says he needs to be at her side. The shop was founded by the parents of the present generation and later run by Henry and Janelle Cooksey; it has already been closed for a while. A final sale is planned, with details still being worked out. In thanking the town, the family said its customers had been “as much friends as you have customers.”

Two seriously hurt in head-on crash on Nestle Road

Two people were seriously injured about 6:30 Wednesday morning in a head-on collision between a GMC Acadia and a Ford F-250 pickup on Nestle Road near the Camfil plant, according to Jonesboro Police spokeswoman Sally Smith. The pickup’s driver was flown to Regional One Health, the trauma hospital in Memphis, and the SUV’s driver was taken to a Jonesboro hospital. Nestle Road was closed for a time between C.W. Post Road and Interstate 555. Police have not released the drivers’ names or said what caused the crash.

A new playground built for every child

City officials cut the ribbon Tuesday on a rebuilt playground at University Heights Lions Club Park on North Bridge Street, designed so children with disabilities can play alongside everyone else. It has a paved walkway, artificial turf that wheelchairs can roll across, and a rope-climbing feature that lets children who cannot walk still reach the slides. Parks and Recreation Director Jim Stearns said the park had fallen into disrepair before work began in October 2025: “We want to make it inclusive where everybody has access regardless of their ability or disability.” Mayor Harold Copenhaver spoke as well; another inclusive park is planned on Disciple Drive.

Schoolchildren and seniors honor veterans

About 55 veterans came to the fifth annual Veterans Day ceremony at Jonesboro’s International Studies Magnet School on Wednesday. The speaker was Tim Doyle, a retired sergeant first class in the Army National Guard who served in Iraq in 2006 and 2007 and is the husband of Superintendent Misty Doyle; he told the students that “small things make a difference.” Fifth graders sang “Our Heroes,” senior Ava Fitzgerald sang the national anthem, and students pinned America 250 pins on veterans in their own families.

At South Wind Heights, an independent-living community, more than 80 residents and veterans shared a hamburger lunch and live music, and eight residents put on boxing gloves for friendly rounds with a coach; no one was hurt. “I don’t let anybody hold me back if I want to do something,” said resident Connie Latham. South Wind Heights is serving one free meal a day to veterans and their spouses through the end of November.

A-State leadership center turns 10 and asks for mentors

Arkansas State University’s Center for Leadership Excellence, founded on Nov. 10, 2016, by Kathy White Loyd and 28 other women to prepare students for careers and leadership, marked its tenth birthday this week. From the State

Former prison chief tells lawmakers the tools are already in hand

Joe Profiri, who formerly ran the Arkansas Department of Corrections and now advises Gov. Sarah Huckabee Sanders, told a joint meeting of the House and Senate Judiciary Committees on Tuesday that the state already has the laws it needs to ease prison crowding. “At this point it’s largely about executing,” he said, urging a shift “from punitive to rehabilitative.” It was his first appearance before legislators since 2025; he lost the corrections job after a public fight with the state Board of Corrections over adding prison beds without adding guards, and lawmakers had talked in September of subpoenaing him.

Profiri said the state has added about 1,500 beds, yet nearly 3,000 people sentenced to prison are again waiting in county jails for a bed. Marshall Clement of the Council of State Governments Justice Center, a nonprofit that advises states, told the committees that Arkansas sends a larger share of people to prison for breaking probation or parole rules than almost any other state, and suggested using the six community correction centers, lower-security residential sites, for the last months of some sentences. Lawmakers heard that the prison population could grow by about 5,000 over the next decade at a cost of roughly $2 billion. The governor’s proposed 3,000-bed prison in Franklin County remains on hold; more hearings are expected before the session opens in January.

University report grades Arkansas schools

Researchers at the University of Arkansas Department of Education Reform, led by department head Harry Anthony Patrinos, have released a book-length report, “The State of Education in Arkansas,” weighing the LEARNS Act, the governor’s 2023 overhaul of state school law, and the 2025 ACCESS Act. They found the state still trails the nation in educational attainment, and that fourth-grade reading and eighth-grade math slipped against other states on the national exam called the Nation’s Report Card. On the state’s own test, the share of students scoring proficient rose from 36.9 percent in 2025 to 42.2 percent this year.

The report urges five priorities: affordable, high-quality early childhood education; better pay and mentoring for teachers; public dashboards linking school and workforce data; more advanced math and career pathways; and more help with attendance and mental health. It praises the fast growth of Education Freedom Accounts, the state program that pays private-school and homeschool costs for 51,573 students this year, but calls for careful study of its effects on learning, public schools and the budget. State Rep. Jessie McGruder, a Marion Democrat and West Memphis teacher, said he worries about the program’s long-term cost.

Sultana museum reopens Saturday in Marion

The Sultana Disaster Museum in Marion, about an hour southeast of Jonesboro, reopens Saturday in the restored 1938 Marion High School gymnasium on Military Road, after a preview Friday for descendants of those aboard. The museum remembers the worst maritime disaster in American history: early on April 27, 1865, the overloaded steamboat Sultana, built for 376 people but carrying nearly 2,000 Union soldiers just freed from Confederate prison camps, exploded on the Mississippi above Memphis. The museum honors almost 1,200 dead; some counts run higher. The wreck lies under a soybean field on the Arkansas side.

The renovation began in March 2025 and cost about $11 million, said John Fogleman, president of the Sultana Historical Preservation Society; FedEx and Hartford Steam Boiler each gave $1 million. The museum opened in 2015 in an 800-square-foot room. “It is going to blow people away,” Mr. Fogleman said.

From the Nation

White House warns Democrats their investigations could be “criminality”

White House counsel Will Scharf, the president’s chief in-house lawyer, sent a letter Monday to Rep. Robert Garcia of California, the senior Democrat on the House Oversight Committee and its expected chairman when Democrats take control of Congress in January. The letter warns that threatening executive-branch officials, or using legal demands “designed merely to abuse or instill fear,” could amount to “blatant criminality.” Oversight of the executive branch is one of Congress’s ordinary powers.

Mr. Garcia replied that the letter suggests the president “has something to hide” and that Congress needs no “permission of the White House” to investigate. Democrats have said they plan to examine the growth of the Trump family’s wealth, the handling of the Jeffrey Epstein investigation, immigration enforcement, the White House ballroom project, and the president’s cryptocurrency and mineral dealings; the White House denies wrongdoing. Attorney General Todd Blanche first told CNN there “could be” a crime if Democratic rhetoric turned threatening, then said the Justice Department would work with Congress through ordinary subpoenas and letters.

At Arlington, Trump defends his war policy and his planned arch

President Trump laid a wreath at the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier at Arlington National Cemetery on Veterans Day with Vice President JD Vance, then spent much of his speech defending his use of force abroad, including the conflict with Iran and operations involving Venezuela, and praising Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, who drew applause. He also honored individual veterans, among them a 103-year-old veteran of the Second World War.

Mr. Trump said he would dedicate to veterans his planned 250-foot “triumphal arch,” topped by a gold statue of Liberty, on Memorial Circle between the Lincoln Memorial and the cemetery. A federal planning commission approved the design Nov. 5, but U.S. District Judge Tanya Chutkan has temporarily blocked construction while lawsuits proceed, brought by Vietnam veterans, the District of Columbia, Virginia and, this week, nine members of the Kennedy family, whose relatives are buried at Arlington. Critics say the arch would overshadow the memorial and the cemetery.

Immigration arrests go on at full pace

Federal immigration arrests have not slowed since last week’s election, even though President Trump said in a “60 Minutes” interview recorded Friday that his approach may have been “too tough” and that he would “love to do something on immigration” with Democrats. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin, the former Oklahoma senator, says his orders have not changed and deportations will continue through the holidays. Advocates report arrests in California, Michigan, Massachusetts, Florida and elsewhere; Immigration and Customs Enforcement has released no numbers. Cases drawing attention include the detention of 19-year-old twin soccer players at Wallace State Community College in Alabama, whose relatives say they have pending asylum cases and work permits, and of an 8-year-old Haitian boy in San Diego. Hard-line Republicans have warned the White House against softening.

Proposed food-stamp rule would drop millions by 2029

The Agriculture Department has proposed a rule that its own analysis says would remove about 6.3 million people from SNAP, the federal food-assistance program once called food stamps, by 2029, and end automatic free school meals for about 1.8 million children. The rule targets “broad-based categorical eligibility,” used by more than 40 states, which lets families with incomes a little over the normal limit qualify if they receive certain non-cash help such as child-care aid or job training; only families receiving cash welfare would still qualify that way. The administration says the change stops benefits to households with too much income and too many assets; Rep. Angie Craig of Minnesota, top Democrat on the House Agriculture Committee, called it “heartless and cruel.” A similar rule was proposed in 2019 and withdrawn under President Biden. The proposal is open for public comment.

From Abroad

Indonesia’s vice president resigns over his schooling

Gibran Rakabuming Raka, 39, vice president of Indonesia, the world’s fourth most populous country, resigned Thursday. He is the eldest son of former President Joko Widodo and was elected in 2024 as President Prabowo Subianto’s running mate. Last month Indonesia’s Constitutional Court and Supreme Court ruled that the education papers he filed before the election did not meet the legal requirement of a high-school diploma, and more than 120 lawmakers from eight parties began a process that could have led to impeachment; he stepped down hours before the House was to take it up.

President Prabowo said he accepted the resignation with a “heavy heart.” There will be no new election: the national legislature, the People’s Consultative Assembly, will choose a new vice president within 60 days from two candidates the president names. Analysts say the episode may end the alliance between Prabowo and Widodo, at a time of street protests over the economy, corruption and months of wildfires.

Congo’s Ebola outbreak passes 11,000 cases

The Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo has topped 11,000 cases, with 11,042 infections and 5,387 deaths, the Congolese government said Wednesday, making it the worst in that country’s history; only the 2014–2016 West African epidemic was larger. Declared in mid-May, it is caused by the Bundibugyo strain, which has no approved vaccine or treatment, though trials are under way. It has reached eight of Congo’s 26 provinces and spread to Uganda, Kenya and Nigeria. Health Minister Roger Kamba said most new infections are now in North Kivu, in the east, where fighting, bad roads and distrust of health workers slow the response. World Health Organization chief Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus condemned attacks that killed four health workers and said more than 1,000 people exposed to the virus are going untracked.

Drones send Baltic residents to shelters

Overnight Tuesday into Wednesday, Lithuania, Latvia and Estonia, the three small NATO countries on the Baltic Sea that border Russia, told residents to take shelter after unidentified drones crossed into their airspace, and NATO fighter jets were scrambled; the alerts lifted hours later, and no one was hurt. Lithuania’s prime minister said four drones were detected across the region. The alerts said the drones came from Russia or Belarus, during a night of Ukrainian drone attacks on Russia that set fire to the port at Ust-Luga. Russia accuses Ukraine of using NATO airspace as cover; Ukraine says Russian jamming sometimes knocks its drones off course, and the Baltic governments deny letting their airspace be used. One attempt was made to shoot down a drone over northern Lithuania; whether it was hit is unclear. A NATO commander said the alliance could turn its Baltic air patrol into a full air-defense mission.

World leaders meet for climate talks in Turkey, with many absent

More than 100 heads of state and government gathered Wednesday and Thursday in Antalya, on Turkey’s Mediterranean coast, for the leaders’ portion of COP31, the annual United Nations climate conference, which runs through Nov. 20. U.N. Secretary-General António Guterres noted that global emissions hit a record in 2025: “The brutal truth is that the climate crisis is outrunning us.” Turkey’s President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan said wealthy nations must do more to cut emissions and pay for the change. Many major leaders stayed away, among them President Trump, China’s Xi Jinping, India’s Narendra Modi, France’s Emmanuel Macron and Germany’s Friedrich Merz, and the European Union’s climate chief said “expectations are not necessarily high” for the final agreement.

Iran lists Argentina’s navy and air force as terrorists

Iran’s Foreign Ministry on Wednesday declared Argentina’s navy and air force “terrorist organizations,” in retaliation for Argentina’s decision in April to put Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, its most powerful military force, on Argentina’s own terrorist list. Argentina blames the Guard and its Lebanese ally Hezbollah for the 1994 bombing of the AMIA Jewish community center in Buenos Aires, which killed 85 people. Argentina’s foreign ministry said it “categorically rejects” Iran’s move. Earlier this month Iran took a similar step against Britain’s Royal Navy and Royal Air Force.

Afield & Afloat

Ratings are rule-of-thumb estimates from the forecast, the rut calendar and the sun, not measurements of animal or fish behavior.

Deer outlook: Fair

The rut is at its peak in north Arkansas, which is reason enough to be in the woods, but this morning is mild, near 48 to 50 at dawn, the barometer is steady, and the afternoon will be warm for November. Be settled by 6:06 a.m. and hunt until about 8:35, then sit again from about 3:00 p.m. to sunset at 4:57; in the peak of the rut, 10 a.m. to 2 p.m. is realistic too, with bucks cruising for does. Today’s tip: on a warm, light-wind day, does bed in shade and cover close to water, and bucks check them there; hang near a creek crossing or a thick bottom with the light breeze in your face, and save your best stand for tomorrow.

Tomorrow, Friday (estimate: Excellent): the front brings a dawn near 33, about 15 degrees colder than today, a rising barometer and a moderate breeze of 10 to 12 miles an hour. That combination in the middle of the rut is about as good as the calendar offers. Watch from about 6:07 to 8:37 a.m. and 2:56 to sunset at 4:56, and stay through midday if you can. Tomorrow’s tip: deer will be on their feet early after a cold, clear night; be in the stand well before first light, hunt the downwind side of a doe bedding area or a fresh scrape line, and keep scent down, since sound and smell carry far in cold, still air. It is also the last day to hunt archery-only before modern gun opens Saturday.

Fishing outlook: Good

At the Crowley’s Ridge lakes (Craighead Forest Lake, Lake Frierson), steady pressure and an afternoon in the low 60s make this the best fishing day of the week. Fish late morning through mid-afternoon as the shallows warm, then the last hour before sunset at 4:57. Today’s tip: fall bass are feeding up on shad; throw a shad-colored crankbait or a spinnerbait along the wind-blown banks and the mouths of coves, and look for crappie suspended around brush piles in 8 to 12 feet.

Tomorrow, Friday (estimate: Poor): the cold front drops the dawn temperature by about 15 degrees, and the afternoon reaches only the upper 40s, which usually puts fish off their feed for a day or two. If you go, the best chance is early to mid-afternoon, plus the last hour before sunset at 4:56. Tomorrow’s tip: slow everything down: a small jig or a live minnow fished vertically over deeper brush, and a jig-and-pig crawled along the bottom for bass, will catch more than anything moving fast.

Ducks open Saturday, Nov. 21, and run through Nov. 29, then Dec. 10–23 and Dec. 26 to Jan. 31; the AGFC warns that its magazine calendar and duck-stamp mailer wrongly listed the first segment as closing Dec. 1. The daily limit is six, with no more than four mallards (two hens). Geese follow the same segments. The AGFC has expanded its permit-only waterfowl hunts, including 715 acres of the St. Francis Sunken Lands in Poinsett County; applications open each week from 3 p.m. Thursday, beginning today, to midnight Sunday, at agfc.com. Quail (through Feb. 7), rabbit and squirrel (both through Feb. 28) are open.

Postscript

Yet one smile more, departing, distant sun!
   One mellow smile through the soft vapoury air,
Ere, o’er the frozen earth, the loud winds run,
   Or snows are sifted o’er the meadows bare.

— William Cullen Bryant, “November,” a sonnet (1824). A fitting word for the last warm afternoon before the frost.

Until tomorrow, with good wishes —
