Mid-November is pecan time: the nuts are dropping in old yards and along the fencerows, and a dry week is the best kind for gathering them before the squirrels and crows finish the job. Persimmons on the wild trees at the woods' edge have turned soft and orange-brown, and the old rule holds that a frost or two takes the pucker out of them. Over the flat Delta fields east of the Ridge, the harvested rice and bean ground lies open, and the first big flights of snow geese and ducks are coming down out of the north ahead of next Saturday's duck opener.

In the garden, there is still time to set out tulip and daffodil bulbs, which want the cold to come; plant them about three times as deep as the bulb is tall. Fallen oak and maple leaves are better shredded into the beds or the compost pile than bagged for the curb. On this date in 1851 an American publisher brought out Herman Melville's Moby-Dick, the whaling novel that sold poorly in its author's lifetime and is now counted among the great American books.

Of Local Interest

Brookland man gets 12 years in federal prison for selling methamphetamine

A 56-year-old Brookland man, Rickey Allen Brown, was sentenced Thursday to 12 years in federal prison for distributing methamphetamine and possessing it with intent to sell. U.S. District Judge D.P. Marshall Jr. also ordered five years of supervised release once he is out; there is no parole in the federal system. Brown had pleaded guilty on May 2, 2025, the same day prosecutors filed the charges against him.

According to the case, in December 2021 an informant working with investigators bought two bags of methamphetamine from Brown at his Brookland home for $700, and a federal drug lab found the drug was 100% pure. In January 2022, officers stopped Brown after watching him leave a suspected drug house and found two bags in his pocket and nearly 110 grams under the driver’s seat. His record includes earlier convictions for making methamphetamine, stalking, domestic battery, assault and terroristic threatening.

The FBI led the investigation with the Second Judicial Drug Task Force (a regional narcotics unit), the Craighead County Sheriff’s Office and Arkansas State Police.

Jonesboro changes how it posts agendas and records council votes

The City of Jonesboro is moving its public meeting records and its in-meeting voting system to a new company, CivicPlus. Agendas, minutes, documents and meeting videos for the City Council and its committees, the Metropolitan Area Planning Commission (the board that reviews zoning and development) and the Board of Zoning Adjustments have lived on a service called Legistar, which residents had called cumbersome and outdated.

City Clerk April Leggett said the city had looked for alternatives for several years because it was not getting the customer support it needed, and the old SHURE voting system in the council chambers was aging and could no longer be supported. Chief Administrative Officer Brian Richardson said the switch also helps the city meet disability-access rules for its website. Leggett called the change “budget neutral,” with the biggest cost, new microphones and tablets for members and speakers, paid from the information technology budget.

Documents for upcoming meetings are already being posted on the new portal, jonesboroar.portal.civicclerk.com, and moving the old records over should take one to two weeks. Until then, past records stay searchable on Legistar, whose contract runs through the end of 2026. Residents who need help can call the clerk’s office at 870-935-0305 or visit the first floor of the Municipal Center, 300 S. Church St., weekdays 8 a.m. to 5 p.m.

Single parents in Craighead County win college scholarships

The Arkansas Single Parent Scholarship Fund, a nonprofit that helps low-income single parents finish college or job training, has awarded $121,200 this fall to 88 single parents in Northeast Arkansas, including 46 from Craighead County. The awards of up to $1,600 cover costs that can stand in the way of a degree or trade certificate, and they come with mentoring, money-management lessons and career coaching.

A family endowment, the Ernest and Anna Ritter Family Endowment, paid for 25 of the scholarships, totaling $32,000, for students from Poinsett, Craighead and Mississippi counties. Mokebia Calicutt, a Craighead County mother attending Arkansas State University-Newport, said the help eased the strain of balancing classes, work and raising her son.

The fund has given more than $55 million to single parents across Arkansas since 1990. Program manager Carrie Allen can be reached at 870-345-4681.

From the State

Arkansas turns down a federal offer to delay its Medicaid work requirement

Arkansas will begin enforcing a work requirement for many adults on Medicaid on Jan. 1, even though federal officials said Friday that states may hold off on enforcement until June 30. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, the federal agency that oversees Medicaid, offered the six-month delay to give states time to “improve and refine their systems.” A spokesman for the Arkansas Department of Human Services said the state is “prepared to implement” the requirement on schedule.

The rule applies to ARHOME, the state’s Medicaid expansion program, which covered about 209,000 low-income Arkansans ages 19 to 64 as of Nov. 1. Many of them will have to log 80 hours a month of work, school or volunteering, a condition set by the federal tax-and-spending law Congress passed last year. Pregnant women, parents and caregivers of children under 14, the medically frail, veterans rated totally disabled and several other groups are exempt. In July, almost 64,000 enrollees were told they were “not compliant” during a trial run that carried no penalties.

Arkansas tried a similar requirement in 2018; a federal judge struck it down after thousands lost coverage. The state’s human services department says it will check records such as income, work hours and disability status to decide who complies. The Legislature, which convenes Jan. 11, will also have to settle on a long-term replacement for ARHOME, whose federal approval was extended only six months.

State Library Board reverses itself and reopens library rules for comment

The Arkansas State Library Board on Friday voted to send its proposed rules on “sexually explicit” library materials back out for public comment, hours after it had unanimously approved them. The rules would carry out Act 372 of 2023, a state law meant to keep minors from obtaining books and films with nudity or sexual content in public libraries that receive state aid. The law also makes knowingly giving a “harmful” item to a minor a misdemeanor punishable by up to a year in jail.

After librarians objected that the draft could sweep in educational works, the board voted 3-2 to exempt encyclopedias and fine art, which one member defined as certain art created before 1900. Under the draft, covered material would be placed in adult-only areas, children under 16 would need a parent’s permission to check it out, and libraries could offer puberty information to children over 11 with written parental consent. A federal appeals court in July lifted an order that had blocked the law. The new comment period’s dates were not announced; the rules would then go to the Legislature.

Texarkana weighs selling Millwood Lake water to North Texas

Texarkana and neighboring Southwest Arkansas towns are pursuing a 40-year deal to lease up to 110 million gallons a day of water rights from Millwood Lake, a reservoir on the Little River, to the North Texas Municipal Water District, a utility serving the Dallas area. About 70 residents questioned city and water officials at a meeting Thursday. A private company would build a roughly $2 billion, 72-inch pipeline, and the participating communities could earn more than $20 million a year.

Texarkana’s city manager said Arkansas’ water needs “will always come first,” noting the city uses about 7 million gallons a day and would keep about 40 million. Residents raised concerns about droughts and endangered species; officials said the deal includes provisions to cut or suspend transfers in low water, though no separate environmental impact study has been done. The Arkansas Natural Resources Commission, which oversees state water resources, is expected to take up the application at a hearing Nov. 18, and the Legislature would have the final say, possibly in March or April.

University of Arkansas review of Razorback athletics sets goals but no plan

A review of University of Arkansas athletics, ordered by the UA System Board of Trustees in September after the Razorback football team’s third losing season in four years, produced broad goals but no concrete plan when it was presented Friday. UA System President Jay Silveria, who leaves in January to become president of Purdue University, outlined five “pillars,” mainly raising revenue, improving name, image and likeness (NIL) pay opportunities for athletes, strengthening donor support and ranking in the top half of the Southeastern Conference in athletic spending.

The written report stated that “no action plans nor next steps were identified.” Board Chairman Randy Lawson said spending alone does not guarantee wins and urged university leaders to keep the effort going after Silveria departs.

From the Nation

Nationwide ground stop halts U.S. flights for hours; thousands canceled

The Federal Aviation Administration, which runs the nation’s air traffic control system, stopped all departing flights across the United States for more than four hours Friday afternoon after what it called a “temporary telecommunications outage.” The problem disrupted communications at the regional centers that guide planes between airports. The ground stop began in mid-afternoon and was lifted that evening; planes already in the air were allowed to land.

Cancellations approached 5,000 by Friday night, and delays were expected to spill into the weekend. At Little Rock’s airport, at least 20 departures were delayed or canceled, and some passengers waited hours aboard parked planes. The last comparable nationwide shutdown was in January 2023, when an FAA safety-messaging system failed.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said network switches and routers became overloaded with data and went down, but added, “We’re still working on the details of what went wrong today.” Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said there was no indication of a cyberattack and no link to this week’s Microsoft outage or to a new AI-based FAA planning tool, while not ruling anything out; he said officials are examining whether older equipment clashed with newer technology being installed. Sen. Tammy Duckworth, an Illinois Democrat, called for a full investigation.

United States reportedly loses its measles elimination status

The United States has lost the “measles eliminated” status it has held since 2000, according to reports Friday that an independent expert panel advising the Pan American Health Organization, the World Health Organization’s regional office for the Americas, made the determination. A country loses the status when the same strain of measles spreads continuously for more than a year. A Utah lab study found one strain caused more than 95% of that state’s cases from June 2025 to June 2026.

More than 5,400 measles cases have been reported nationally this year, the most since 1991, nearly all in people who were unvaccinated or whose status was unknown. Pennsylvania and Wisconsin each reported a new measles-related death Friday, bringing state-reported deaths this year to at least 10; the federal count is lower, after Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. asked the CDC not to count disputed deaths. Canada lost its status last year. Losing it carries no automatic penalty, but health experts say regaining it will be difficult as kindergarten vaccination rates have fallen to 92.4%. Supreme Court will decide whether Trump can shut off asylum at the border

The Supreme Court agreed Friday to decide whether President Trump had the power to suspend asylum at the southern border. On his first day back in office in January 2025, Trump issued a proclamation declaring an “invasion” at the border and barring migrants who cross between ports of entry from seeking asylum, the legal protection for people who fear persecution at home. A federal judge in Washington ruled in July 2025 that neither the Constitution nor immigration law gave him that authority, and a divided appeals court largely agreed in April.

The justices will also decide whether the lower court could protect all asylum seekers rather than only those who sued. The administration argues the policy is its main tool against illegal crossings and says border encounters have dropped 93% since it took effect; immigrant advocates say it violates laws passed by Congress. The policy remains largely in force while the case proceeds. Arguments are expected in the spring, with a ruling by early summer.

Washington widens its look at Nvidia after its chief executive resigns

Congress is moving to question Jensen Huang, who resigned Thursday as chief executive of Nvidia, the computer-chip maker at the center of the artificial intelligence boom, after reports that he had a relationship with a business executive whom U.S. officials reportedly suspect of spying for China. The woman worked for a Singapore company accused of smuggling restricted Nvidia chips into China. Chief Financial Officer Colette Kress is serving as interim chief executive.

Sen. Tim Scott of South Carolina, the Republican chairman of the Senate Banking Committee, on Thursday asked Huang to testify Dec. 3 and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick on Dec. 10, threatening subpoenas if they refuse. Lutnick, whose department enforces chip export limits, has been criticized over reports that it was slow to act against firms routing chips to China; a Commerce spokeswoman said he agreed to appear voluntarily, and the White House has backed him. Three House committee chairmen have also sought testimony from Huang and Nvidia’s top lawyer. Democrats, who take control of both chambers in January, say they will continue the inquiries.

From Abroad

Trump, Xi and Putin plan first three-way meeting next week in China

The White House said Friday that President Trump will meet China’s President Xi Jinping and Russia’s President Vladimir Putin together next week in Shenzhen, China, on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit, a yearly gathering of Pacific Rim leaders. It would be the first meeting of the three leaders together. A U.S. official said Trump wants to discuss cutting nuclear weapons and the war with Iran, but not the war in Ukraine.

The talks come after New START, the last treaty capping U.S. and Russian nuclear arsenals, expired in February, leaving no limits on either country’s weapons for the first time in more than 50 years; Trump wants any new deal to include China. A senior administration official said, “We do not expect to get to any kind of agreement.” Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov called the meeting “extremely significant,” while Ukraine’s President Volodymyr Zelensky has voiced concern that his country’s future could be discussed without Ukraine present.

Iran escalates in the Strait of Hormuz as Trump says no deal is needed

Iran said Saturday that more oil tankers struck naval mines and caught fire in the eastern Strait of Hormuz, the narrow passage linking the Persian Gulf to the open sea and a key route for the world’s oil. Iran says ships need its permission to pass and warned that any tanker using what it calls a restricted route “will be set on fire.” Iran’s claims could not be independently verified; the U.S. military says it has cleared the main shipping lanes and that the strait is open. A senior U.N. official said Friday that at least 25 sailors have been killed in and around the strait since the U.S.-Israeli war with Iran began in February, and shipping there is about 65% below prewar levels.

On Friday, the U.S. military said it shot down two Iranian attack drones headed for Bahrain, the island kingdom that hosts the U.S. Navy’s Fifth Fleet, and denied an Iranian Revolutionary Guard claim that it destroyed a U.S. radar there. Iran also told the International Atomic Energy Agency, the U.N. nuclear watchdog, that talks on inspections cannot proceed while it faces military threats.

The fighting follows President Trump’s announcement Thursday that “we don’t need a deal” with Iran, as he removed Vice President JD Vance from leading negotiations and assigned him to investigate fraud in Democratic-run states. Trump later said it would be a “defeat” to leave Iran’s current government in power, and officials would not say whether talks have stopped.

Europe names five nations to join Ukraine peace talks; Moscow noncommittal

European Union leaders meeting in Dublin, Ireland, decided Friday that Britain, France, Germany, Italy and Poland, together with EU officials, will represent Europe in the next round of U.S.-brokered talks to end Russia’s war in Ukraine. The step follows a visit to Moscow this week by U.S. envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, who said afterward that Putin was “willing to give it a try” with European participation. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky welcomed the move, saying it would give Europe “a voice.”

The Kremlin said no decision had been made; Russia has opposed direct European involvement because European countries arm Ukraine. European Council President Antonio Costa said Europe “must speak with one clear voice.” No date or location has been set for the next round. Zelensky also said a recently agreed prisoner exchange would be the largest of the war.

International Criminal Court drops inquiry into U.S. troops in Afghanistan

Judges at the International Criminal Court, the world’s permanent war-crimes tribunal in The Hague, have halted the investigation into alleged crimes by American troops and intelligence officers in Afghanistan, narrowing the case to the Taliban’s conduct since it retook power in 2021. The ruling, issued Thursday and made public Friday, cited “significant limitations” on prosecutors’ ability to gather evidence. The U.S. portion of the inquiry had been largely dormant since 2021.

The prosecutor’s office said it has already appealed. The United States is not a member of the court and has sanctioned 13 current and former court staff over this inquiry and over arrest warrants for Israeli officials. Secretary of State Marco Rubio has said Trump “will never allow” the court to prosecute Americans.

On the Field

Arkansas State football (4–5, 2–3 Sun Belt) kicks off at noon Central at Coastal Carolina (3–6, 2–3) on ESPN+, from Brooks Stadium in Conway, S.C. Both teams are trying to bounce back. The Red Wolves lost 19–17 to ULM last week, and the Chanticleers fell 21–8 to Old Dominion. Quarterback Trey Owens threw for 193 yards and ran for 67 against ULM. Coastal, a slight favorite of about a point and a half, will start redshirt freshman Deuce Bailey on its Senior Day and is trying to end a four-game losing streak at home. A-State needs two more wins to become bowl-eligible.

A-State men’s basketball (1–2) has lost two straight since opening with a 71–70 win over Toledo: 101–86 at Alabama and 82–65 at home to UT Martin on Tuesday. They host Dillard tonight at 7 at First National Bank Arena (ESPN+) and UAPB on Wednesday at 7, then head to Miami for the FIU Thanksgiving tournament Nov. 23–25.

Arkansas (3–6, 1–5 SEC) faces No. 17 South Carolina (6–3, 4–3) at 6:45 p.m. on SEC Network in Fayetteville. The Razorbacks are in the spoiler’s role and have lost 10 straight SEC home games. The Gamecocks have won four in a row, and quarterback LaNorris Sellers was upgraded to probable Thursday after playing on a sore ankle. It is first-year Hog coach Ryan Silverfield’s first meeting with Shane Beamer, and South Carolina’s offensive coordinator is former Arkansas OC Kendal Briles. Arkansas leads the series 14–10, and South Carolina is favored by about a touchdown.

Tennessee (7–2, 4–2 SEC), No. 8 in the CFP rankings, plays at Texas A&M (4–5, 1–5) at 2 p.m. on ESPN at Kyle Field. The Vols lead the nation with 39 sacks and allow 15.1 points a game. Running back DeSean Bishop has 1,066 rushing yards, 426 shy of the school’s single-season record. Tennessee is favored by about 2½ points over an Aggie team that has lost three straight SEC games, and LSU visits Knoxville next week.

Afield & Afloat

Ratings are rule-of-thumb estimates from the forecast, the rut calendar and the sun, not measurements of animal or fish behavior.

Deer outlook: Good

Opening morning of modern gun season comes with nearly everything in the hunter's favor: the rut is at or near its peak in north Arkansas, the dawn is below freezing, the barometer has climbed behind the front, and the wind is light. Best windows: from about 6:08 a.m., a half hour before sunrise, until about 8:40; again from 10 a.m. to 2 p.m., when rutting bucks cruise in daylight looking for does; and from 2:55 p.m. to the 4:55 sunset.

Today's tip: on a still, frosty dawn your scent sinks and creeps downhill, pooling in the hollows and creek bottoms, so sit above the trails you are watching rather than below them; once the sun warms the slopes by mid-morning the air turns and rises. With more than 300,000 hunters expected afield this weekend, wear the required blaze orange from the truck to the stand, and know what lies beyond your target before you squeeze.

Tomorrow, Sunday (estimate: Good). Another cold, near-calm dawn and steady high pressure, with the rut still in full swing; the same windows apply, from 6:09 a.m., 10 to 2, and 2:55 to sunset. Tomorrow's tip: after a day of opening-weekend gunfire, older deer slip into the thickest cover they know, cutovers, briar patches and creek-bottom cane, and bucks trailing does keep moving through midday; a patient hunter on the edge of that cover, staying put through lunch, often does better on Sunday than on Saturday.

Fishing outlook: Fair

Lake water is cooling fast after the frosty nights, and the bite is slow early, but the light breeze and a sunny afternoon near 53 should stir the fish in the warmer hours. Best times: late morning through mid-afternoon as the water warms, and the last hour before the 4:55 sunset.

Today's tip: at Craighead Forest Lake and Lake Frierson, crappie are drawing together on brush piles and around fishing piers and docks, often 8 to 15 feet down; fish a small minnow or a 1/16-ounce jig slowly, just above the cover. Bass will move to the sunny north banks by afternoon, where rocks and wood soak up warmth; a slow-rolled crankbait or a jig dragged along the bottom beats fast, flashy lures in cold water.

Tomorrow, Sunday (estimate: Fair). Another frosty start and light wind, with a slightly warmer afternoon near 57; the best hours again run from late morning to mid-afternoon and the last hour of light. Tomorrow's tip: catfish feed through the cool months in the deeper water near the dam and old creek channels; a piece of cut shad or chicken liver on the bottom, fished patiently through the warm part of the afternoon, is the surest bet for a Sunday stringer.

Modern gun season opens this morning in all four zones. In Zone 4, which takes in Craighead County, it runs only nine days, through next Sunday, Nov. 22, and allows just one antlerless deer by modern gun; in Zones 3, 6 and 8 it runs to Dec. 6. All four zones reopen Dec. 26–28, and Zones 3, 6 and 8 have a muzzleloader hunt Dec. 12–14. The season bag is four deer, no more than two of them bucks, and chronic wasting disease rules on carcass movement apply in Craighead County; check agfc.com for check-in rules and the zone's details before you go.

Ducks open a week from today, Saturday, Nov. 21, for a first segment through Nov. 29, followed by Dec. 10–23 and Dec. 26 through Jan. 31; the daily limit is six, with no more than four mallards, two of them hens. Canada geese run with the duck segments, two a day; white-fronted geese are two a day; snow, blue and Ross's geese, 20. The youth, veteran and active-military waterfowl hunt is Feb. 6–7. Quail are open through Feb. 7, six a day.

Postscript

Yet one smile more, departing, distant sun!
One mellow smile through the soft vapoury air,
Ere, o’er the frozen earth, the loud winds run,
Or snows are sifted o’er the meadows bare.
One smile on the brown hills and naked trees,
And the dark rocks whose summer wreaths are cast,
And the blue gentian flower, that, in the breeze,
Nods lonely, of her beauteous race the last.
Yet a few sunny days, in which the bee
Shall murmur by the hedge that skirts the way,
The cricket chirp upon the russet lea,
And man delight to linger in thy ray.
Yet one rich smile, and we will try to bear
The piercing winter frost, and winds, and darkened air.

— William Cullen Bryant, “November” (1824). A week of sunny days lies ahead before the first real chill of winter; the poet would tell us to linger in them.

Until tomorrow, with good wishes —
