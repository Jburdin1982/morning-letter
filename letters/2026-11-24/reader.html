The sun rises at 6:48 and sets at 4:50, for ten hours and two minutes of daylight, about a minute and 21 seconds less than Monday’s. The moon is full today, the November full moon that the old almanacs call the Beaver Moon, after the season when trappers set their lines before the ponds froze. Tonight’s clouds will likely hide it, but Thursday’s clearer sky should show a moon still nearly round, rising in the early evening over the stubble fields.

In field and garden: the oaks along Crowley’s Ridge are holding on to their brown leaves, as many young oaks do all winter, rattling in the north wind long after the maples and sweetgums have gone bare. Watch the feeders this week for dark-eyed juncos, the small gray “snowbirds” with white outer tail feathers that come down from the north for the winter and hop about under the bird feeders picking up dropped seed. In the kitchen, a frozen turkey thawing in the refrigerator needs about a day for every four to five pounds, so a big bird still in the freezer this morning will not be ready by Thursday that way; the safe shortcut is to keep it in its wrapper submerged in cold tap water, changed every half hour, allowing about 30 minutes a pound.

Weather lore, not a forecast: an old country saying runs, “Clear moon, frost soon.” Tonight’s full moon will be veiled in cloud and the low near 35, so the saying gets no test; but Wednesday night clears, and Thanksgiving morning is forecast near 32.

Of Local Interest

Two days before Thanksgiving, the news at home runs to a longer runway, the first ballots in the December runoffs and a good many hot meals.

A longer runway opens at the Jonesboro airport

Jonesboro Municipal Airport, the city’s airfield on Lindbergh Drive, holds a ribbon-cutting at 10:30 this morning to mark the end of work that strengthened its main runway and added 500 feet to it, along with its parallel taxiway. The runway now runs 6,700 feet instead of 6,200, and officials say the extra length will help the airport meet present and future demand; it was designed with faster aircraft such as business jets in mind.

The expansion began in 2017 and was carried out in phases as money for each piece was found. The main runway closed for construction in July, and the airport kept operating on its secondary runway in the meantime. Airport manager George Jackson invited the public to the ceremony, which will be held at FBO Building No. 1, 3901 Lindbergh Drive. (FBO stands for fixed-base operator, the hangar-and-fuel business that serves private pilots.)

Early voting opens today in the Dec. 1 runoffs

Early voting begins this morning in the runoff elections that follow this month’s general election, for races in which no candidate won a majority on Nov. 3. In Craighead County there are two: the Jonesboro City Council seat for Ward 5, Position 1, where incumbent L.J. Bryant faces Patti Lack, and the race for mayor of Lake City, between Daniel Haynes and Jackie Davis Jr.

Craighead County voters may cast early ballots from 8 a.m. to 5 p.m. today, Wednesday, Friday and next Monday, Nov. 30, at the Election Annex in Jonesboro or the Eastern Annex in Lake City; there is no early voting on Thanksgiving Day. Election Day is Tuesday, Dec. 1.

Runoffs are also on the ballot elsewhere in the region: for mayor in Pocahontas (Keith Futrell and Danny Swann), Wynne (Jennifer Hobbs and De’Andra L. Warren), Tuckerman (Roger Williams and Mark Warren), Gosnell (Richard Brown and Jason M. Taylor) and Cherry Valley (Brett Stephens and Christy Phillips), and for two Parkin City Council seats.

Finance committee to take up license rules and street paving

The Jonesboro City Council’s Finance and Administration Committee, the group of council members that reviews money and administrative matters before they go to the full council, meets at 4 p.m. today at the Municipal Center, 300 S. Church St. Its agenda, posted last week, is short.

The main item is a proposed ordinance that would add sections to the city code setting out standard guidance and what the city expects of businesses holding a city privilege license (the yearly license businesses need to operate in Jonesboro). The committee will also consider accepting the low bid from Atlas Asphalt, Inc. for milling and resurfacing selected city streets, work paid for with a Community Development Block Grant, the federal money cities receive to improve lower-income neighborhoods; the agenda does not list the bid amount or the streets. Five resolutions would place $275 municipal liens, claims the city files against a property to recover what it spent cleaning up or mowing it, on properties around town.

Items the committee approves go on to the full City Council. The committee’s next meeting is Tuesday, Dec. 8.

Hot meals, and hands to serve them, for Thanksgiving

The 30th Jonesboro Community Thanksgiving, the free holiday dinner held each year at St. Bernards Auditorium on East Washington Avenue, opens at 10 a.m. Thursday and serves until the food runs out. Organizer Barbara Sanders said the effort turns out about 2,500 meals a year, close to 2,000 of them delivered to homes and 200 to 300 eaten on site. Volunteers can sign up online through today, and the group says it can still use help.

It is open to everyone, first come, first served, with no sign-up or ID needed. The center served about 75 meals last year and is planning for more than 100. It also delivers in Cash and Egypt; delivery requests must be in by today, with volunteer Gayle Pennington at 870-761-4436.

Red Wolves athletes box up Thanksgiving dinners

Athletes from across Arkansas State University’s teams spent the week before Thanksgiving on a volunteer project they called One Pack Gives Back, run by the university’s Student Athlete Advisory Committee, the group that represents athletes’ views to the athletic department. Working with Volunteer A-State and its campus food pantry, they shopped for groceries at Bill’s Cost Plus Supermarket on Wednesday, assembled boxed meals on Thursday and Friday, and handed them out to selected families at the A-State Food Pantry on Saturday.

Jaelynn Blackwell, a track and field athlete who came to Jonesboro from Maryland, about 15 hours away, said the work helped her settle into Arkansas; teammate Alexis Waller, a triple jumper, said it helped her get to know athletes in other sports. Both are recruiting more athletes to the committee, and Blackwell urged everyone, not only athletes, to take up community service and to know about the campus food pantry.

From the State

Data center judge sees likely open-meetings violation

U.S. District Judge Lee Rudofsky said Monday that he was “concerned significantly” that a Pulaski County justice of the peace broke the state’s open-meetings law while pushing the county’s new data-center ordinance, a finding that would make the ordinance invalid. He spoke at the end of a daylong hearing in Little Rock on AVAIO Digital’s request for a preliminary injunction, a court order that would keep the county from enforcing the ordinance against its planned $6 billion data center near Wrightsville while the company’s lawsuit goes forward. The Quorum Court, the county’s elected governing board, passed the ordinance in October over the veto of County Judge Barry Hyde; it requires a Planning Board permit for “high-intensity digital infrastructure.”

The dispute centers on Justice of the Peace Julie Blackwood, who is a defendant along with the county and Judge Hyde. AVAIO says she called fellow members before the Aug. 25 meeting to line up votes, which would amount to an illegal private meeting; she testified that she asked only whether they would attend. The judge said her text messages pointed the other way and told her lawyer, John Adams, “It looks pretty bad for your client,” though he noted the Quorum Court could likely pass a similar ordinance again quickly. Company witnesses testified that the site plan approved in August was all the county permission the project needs, and County Judge-elect Wendell Griffen, who campaigned against data centers and takes office Jan. 1, testified that he drafted the ordinance himself, using artificial-intelligence tools to understand some concepts.

Friday’s proposed settlement between AVAIO and Judge Hyde, which the court refused to sign, is off the table, and the full lawsuit is months from trial. Judge Rudofsky said he would try to rule on the injunction soon; reports differ on whether he set this Friday or Dec. 4 as his goal.

Senator files bill to replace Arkansas’ Medicaid expansion

State Sen. Bryan King, a Green Forest Republican, has filed the first bill of the coming session that proposes a specific replacement for ARHOME, Arkansas’ version of Medicaid expansion, which uses federal and state Medicaid money to buy private insurance for about 209,000 low-income adults. Senate Bill 9 would direct Gov. Sarah Huckabee Sanders to seek federal permission to move those enrollees into traditional “fee-for-service” Medicaid, in which the state pays doctors and hospitals directly, starting Jan. 1, 2028, and would repeal ARHOME at the end of 2027.

The program, first known as the “private option,” has run under a federal waiver since 2014, but the Trump administration refused to renew it for five years and granted only a six-month extension; Centene Corp. is also leaving, which would leave Arkansas Blue Cross and Blue Shield as the only insurer. A state-hired actuary found in July that ARHOME costs almost twice as much as traditional Medicaid. Sen. King says the savings could help clear the waiting list of nearly 2,500 Arkansans with disabilities seeking home- and community-based services.

Bo Ryall, president of the Arkansas Hospital Association, warned that hospitals could lose hundreds of millions of dollars under a new model even if paid at Medicare rates. Sen. Joshua Bryant of Rogers, recently elected Senate president pro tempore, said more bills are likely and urged colleagues not to pick sides yet. The Legislature convenes Jan. 11.

Democrats file bills to raise the minimum wage to $15

Four Democratic lawmakers from East and central Arkansas have filed three bills to raise the state’s minimum wage, now $11 an hour, where voters set it in 2018. Rep. Lincoln Barnett, a former mayor of Hughes who represents parts of Crittenden and St. Francis counties, filed them with Rep.-elect Audrey Willis of Marion and Sens.-elect Jessie McGruder of Marion and Tara Shephard of Little Rock.

House Bill 1008 would raise the wage to $15 on Jan. 1, 2028; House Bill 1009 would raise it to $13.50 as soon as it takes effect and to $15 a year later. House Bill 1010 would leave the rate alone but end exemptions that let employers with fewer than four workers, and those hiring full-time students, apprentices or workers with disabilities, pay less than the minimum.

The bills come after Gov. Sanders recently told viewers of ABC’s “The View” that Arkansas’ minimum wage is $15, and alongside a separate proposed ballot measure, submitted to Attorney General Tim Griffin’s office this month, that would raise the wage $1 a year. None has had a hearing; the session opens Jan. 11.

“Jacksyn Act” would raise the age to buy fireworks

The same East Arkansas lawmakers, Rep. Lincoln Barnett and Sen.-elect Jessie McGruder, have filed two bills named for Jacksyn Smith, a 14-year-old from Hughes in St. Francis County who died after a firework exploded on July 4; his grandmother has said he was holding one that was “designed to be placed on the ground.” Arkansas now lets children as young as 12 buy fireworks.

House Bill 1006 would raise the buying age to 18 and require adult supervision for anyone under 18 setting them off. House Bill 1007, a middle course, would set the age at 16, require 16- and 17-year-olds to shop with an adult at least 21, and require supervision for anyone under 16. Both await the session that begins Jan. 11.

From the Nation

Nvidia rehires Jensen Huang as chief executive after Trump steps in

Nvidia, the California company that makes most of the chips that power artificial intelligence, reinstated co-founder Jensen Huang as president and chief executive on Monday, 11 days after he resigned and less than a week after the board said it did not intend to bring him back. Mr. Huang stepped down Nov. 12 after admitting extramarital affairs, including one with Alice Huang, a former executive of Megaspeed, a Singapore company; the Justice Department is examining whether she worked for Chinese intelligence and has reached no conclusion, and no one has been charged. The two are not related. Colette Kress, the finance chief who served as interim CEO, returns to her old job; the board’s chairman, Harvey Jones, said Mr. Huang “is the right person to lead NVIDIA,” and Mr. Huang said he would be “a better CEO the second time around.”

President Trump said in a phone interview with CNBC that he spoke to the board “directly and indirectly” and “convinced them,” adding, “I made the deal.” The board said only that it had “received input from senior levels of the United States government” and was confident the move “poses no national security risk.” CNBC, citing three people familiar with the board’s thinking, reported that a personal assurance from Mr. Trump that Mr. Huang had not broken national-security laws sealed the decision, along with the steep fall in the stock after he left. The shares jumped about a third on the news. The same day, the Commerce Department added Megaspeed and six related companies to its trade blacklist on suspicion of diverting Nvidia chips to China; Megaspeed says it acted lawfully.

Senate Democratic Leader Chuck Schumer of New York said Democrats would “wield their full power to investigate any wrongdoing by the Trump Administration” once they control Congress in January, and Sen. Ruben Gallego, an Arizona Democrat, accused the president of “selling out our national security to keep the stock market high.” Rep. Don Bacon, a retiring Nebraska Republican, said the company should have ignored the administration. Mr. Huang is still scheduled to be interviewed by three House committees next week.

Court clears the Army to detonate Iowa explosives Wednesday

The 8th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals in St. Louis on Monday lifted its pause on the Army’s plan to blow up about 9,500 pounds of degraded lead azide, a highly sensitive explosive used in detonators, at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant near Middletown, in southeastern Iowa. The court turned down two environmental groups, the Iowa Environmental Council and the Sierra Club, finding that “a controlled detonation with attendant safety measures better alleviates the risk of irreparable harm than the risk of an uncontrolled explosion”; the Army says the material, in 68 barrels found in March, has crystallized and could go off on its own in cold weather. The judges said the Environmental Protection Agency’s emergency permit included adequate safeguards against spreading lead dust, such as sand barriers around the bunker, weather limits and air monitoring.

Earlier Monday, before the ruling, President Trump wrote that he had ordered the Army to hold off until the court agreed and made it “fully subject” to the agreement of Iowa’s governor-elect, Democrat Rob Sand, whom he said he would hold responsible for any “death or destruction” if it did not go ahead. Mr. Sand, who takes office in January, said the decision rests with the federal officials running the job and asked that residents get the same briefing he received. Monday night the acting Army secretary, Adam Telle, said the “carefully controlled detonation” will happen Wednesday morning, based on forecast winds, and called it a “consensus decision” that Mr. Sand ultimately accepted. Lead is a nerve poison, and nearby residents have been told to stay indoors with windows shut during the blast.

Appeals court reopens Trump’s bid to erase his New York conviction

A three-judge panel of the 2nd U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals in New York on Monday revived President Trump’s effort to move his hush-money criminal case from state court to federal court, where he could ask to have the conviction thrown out on grounds of presidential immunity. A Manhattan jury convicted Mr. Trump in 2024 of 34 felony counts of falsifying business records to hide a $130,000 payment to the adult-film actress Stormy Daniels; he was given no punishment, and he denies wrongdoing.

The panel said the late U.S. District Judge Alvin Hellerstein, who died last month at 92, “erred in holding that removal is categorically unavailable in a state criminal case” after a conviction. The guilty verdict stands for now. The panel told a lower federal judge to rule promptly on the request and asked that no further proceedings take place in the state courts in the meantime, which puts on hold Mr. Trump’s separate appeal in New York’s Appellate Division, argued Nov. 5. A spokesman for Mr. Trump’s lawyers called the ruling “prompt and correct”; the Manhattan district attorney’s office declined to comment.

Trump orders prosecutors to seek death for child killers

President Trump signed a memorandum at the Justice Department on Monday directing federal prosecutors to seek the death penalty for first-degree murder of a child and for killings committed during a rape, child sexual exploitation, kidnapping or human trafficking, and to carry out existing federal death sentences “as quickly as possible.” “Once juries have spoken, capital punishment will be swift and certain,” he said. It adds to an order he signed on his first day back in office covering, among others, killers of police officers.

The move answers California Gov. From Abroad

Iran vows “all available means” against U.S. blockade as more tankers burn

Iran’s Foreign Ministry said Monday it will use “all available means” to break the U.S. Navy’s blockade of Iranian ports, which spokesman Esmaeil Baqaei called an act of military aggression; the United States has blockaded the ports since July to counter Iran’s own closure of the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow passage at the mouth of the Persian Gulf that carries a large share of the world’s oil. Iran’s navy said three oil tankers hit mines in the strait on Monday and two more caught fire after hitting mines east of it on Tuesday, and Britain’s maritime security agency, UKMTO, confirmed an attack Monday on a ship anchored off the United Arab Emirates, with a fire but no reported casualties. The navy said the strait “will never be reopened” until Iran’s demands are met, including lifting the blockade and sanctions and unfreezing its assets.

The U.S. Treasury on Monday sanctioned about two dozen people and companies in Tehran and Dubai it says run Iran’s “shadow banking” network, and Secretary of State Marco Rubio met Qatar’s prime minister, whose government has been carrying messages between the two sides; indirect talks are stalled. President Trump said Sunday that Iran’s drones and missiles had “done no damage” and that he wants a “permanent” deal keeping Iran from a nuclear weapon. U.S. officials are also investigating possible Iranian involvement in cyberattacks on as many as 50 ships carrying oil and other explosive cargo (single source: Wall Street Journal). In Israel, Gadi Eisenkot, the former army chief trying to form a new government, said he sees no immediate Iranian threat requiring action before then, while caretaker Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu warned against downplaying “grave threats.”

NATO sends a radar plane to Poland after Russian missiles land there

NATO will station an AWACS early-warning aircraft, a radar plane that watches the skies and directs allied fighters, over Poland after three Russian Kh-101 cruise missiles came down in the country’s southeast on Friday during a mass Russian strike on Ukraine, Polish Deputy Defense Minister Paweł Zalewski said Monday after an emergency meeting of NATO ambassadors in Brussels. He said every ambassador expressed “full solidarity” with Poland and that the plane will give Poland better “operational awareness” to respond to such threats. Poland, which borders Ukraine, did not invoke Article 4 of the NATO treaty, which calls for formal consultations among allies; its other requests for stronger air defenses on NATO’s eastern edge were put off to a meeting of alliance foreign ministers next week. NATO Secretary-General Mark Rutte has called the incident a “grave violation” of Polish airspace.

Belgium shut down by strike as budget fight threatens the government

Tens of thousands of people marched through Brussels on Monday during a national strike against spending cuts, with police counting about 50,000 and unions about 70,000; all flights at Belgian airports were grounded when air-traffic controllers walked out, and trains, buses and schools were disrupted. The protest is aimed at Prime Minister Bart De Wever, a Flemish nationalist, whose five-party coalition wants to save about 10 billion euros over several years, including cuts in health care and a sales-tax increase, to bring down a deficit above 5 percent of the economy, well over the European Union’s 3 percent limit.

Mr. De Wever offered his resignation Nov. 2 after budget talks broke down, but King Philippe refused it and told him to keep trying. Belgium already missed the EU’s Oct. 15 budget deadline, and Mr. De Wever has warned that his government cannot carry on without a deal; he is due to report to the king again next week.

Iranian singer flogged 74 times for performing without a head scarf

Parastoo Ahmadi, an Iranian singer, and four musicians who performed with her were each given 74 lashes on Monday at the prosecutor’s office in Qom, the country’s religious center, her lawyer, Mostafa Nili, said. They were punished for a December 2024 concert, filmed without an audience at a historic caravanserai and posted on YouTube, in which Ms. Ahmadi sang without a hijab, the head covering Iranian law requires women to wear in public. A Qom court sentenced them for violating public morality, and an appeals court upheld it; the sentence also bars them from performing and from leaving the country for two years. Human-rights groups condemned the flogging, and there was no immediate response from Iranian officials.

Saudi-led strikes in Yemen kill dozens, Houthis say

Yemen’s Houthi rebels, who are backed by Iran and hold the capital, Sanaa, said Monday that an air strike by the Saudi-led coalition on a telecommunications compound in the Red Sea port of Hodeidah killed 17 workers and wounded 11, and that a strike on buses and trucks in Lahj province left 25 people dead or missing. Saudi Arabia did not comment, and the tolls could not be independently confirmed. The coalition, which backs Yemen’s internationally recognized government, said it hit military sites in six provinces, part of a stepped-up campaign since Houthi missiles and drones were fired toward Riyadh, the Saudi capital, in recent days. Hodeidah handles most of Yemen’s commercial imports, and the government says the port remains open.

Afield & Afloat

Ratings are rule-of-thumb estimates from the forecast, the rut calendar and the sun, not measurements of animal or fish behavior.

Deer outlook: Excellent

Everything that moves deer is in place today: the rut is still in its peak window for north Arkansas, the dawn is near 33, the barometer climbed about a sixth of an inch over the last day behind the weekend’s front, and it begins a slow slide this afternoon as clouds thicken ahead of Wednesday’s weather, which tends to set deer feeding. Best windows: from 6:18 a.m. to about two hours after sunrise, from 10 a.m. to 2 p.m. while bucks are still cruising for does, and from 2:50 p.m. until sunset at 4:50.

Today’s tip: the wind holds out of the north-northeast all day, near 14 miles an hour by afternoon, so sit stands on the south and southwest sides of bedding cover and food plots, where the breeze carries your scent away from the deer, and walk in from the south. A steady wind like this also covers a little noise, a fair day to still-hunt slowly along a ridge into the wind. In Zone 4, around Jonesboro, the gun season is over, so this is a bow day there.

Tomorrow, Wednesday (estimate: Good). A raw, gray day with a dawn near 39, a lighter wind near 10 miles an hour, and the barometer down about a seventh of an inch from today; the rut keeps deer on their feet, though the chance of light precipitation rises toward 42 percent by midafternoon. Windows: 6:19 a.m. to about two hours after sunrise, 10 a.m. to 2 p.m., and 2:50 p.m. to sunset at 4:50.

Tomorrow’s tip: make the morning sit count and plan to stay put until midday, before the damp sets in; if it does turn wet late, deer often move right up to dark under a light drizzle, so a short evening sit near a trail into a food source can pay off. Pack a rain shell that does not rustle.

Fishing outlook: Fair

A slowly falling barometer under a gray sky ahead of Wednesday’s weather usually puts fish on a modest feed, and that is about what today offers on Craighead Forest Lake and Lake Frierson, though the cold mornings have been cooling the water steadily and the afternoon only reaches about 47. Best times: late morning through midafternoon, as the water warms a little, and the last hour before sunset at 4:50.

Today’s tip: crappie have been sliding toward deeper brush and drop-offs as the lakes cool, so start around 10 to 15 feet with a small jig or minnow under a slip bobber, fished slowly; bass will hold tight to cover on the wind-blown north and northeast banks. Fish the wind-sheltered side if you are after bream.

Tomorrow, Wednesday (estimate: Poor). The barometer drops quickly ahead of the weather, the afternoon stays in the upper 30s and the sky is solid gray, a combination that usually shuts fish down. If you go anyway, the best chance is late morning to early afternoon, before the precipitation chance climbs.

Tomorrow’s tip: slow everything down, a jig dragged along the bottom or a minnow held still over brush, or re-spool reels for the warm weekend ahead.
