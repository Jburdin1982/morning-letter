A cool, clear start, around 40 at first light and 42 by 6 o’clock, gives way to a fine, dry day: about 52 by 9, 65 at noon and a high near 68 by mid-afternoon under a mostly sunny sky, with no chance of rain and only a light breeze. After the 4:53 sunset it eases to about 59 by suppertime and 53 by 9 o’clock, and tonight bottoms out in the mid- to upper 40s, noticeably milder than the last few nights.

The warm spell builds from there: about 71 on Thursday and 72 on Friday, and still about 71 on Saturday with lows in the 50s. A front brings the week’s only real rain chance, about one in four, on Sunday, then much cooler air arrives: highs near 55 on Monday and 51 on Tuesday, with Monday morning’s low near 35. The ground is badly in need of more than that; the region has been in drought for well over a year.

The moon is just past first quarter, a little more than half lit, riding high in the south at dusk and setting around midnight; it grows each evening toward the full Beaver Moon around the 24th, two days before Thanksgiving. The day has shrunk to 10 hours and 11 minutes, a minute and 34 seconds shorter than yesterday. With the moon down after midnight, the small hours are dark enough to catch a stray meteor from the Leonid shower, which peaks each year around the 17th and 18th; look east after 2 a.m., and expect a handful an hour rather than a storm.

In the country, the combines have gone quiet and the talk has turned to water. The rice and bean fields stand in stubble, the wheat that went in on the drier ground is showing a faint green wash where it caught any moisture at all, and the sloughs and brakes along the Cache sit low ahead of Saturday’s duck opener. Pecans are falling in the old yard trees, and the mild days are good for gathering them before the crows and squirrels do. It is a fine week to set out garlic and pansies, rake the leaves onto the garden beds as a winter blanket, and give young trees and shrubs a deep soaking, since a dry plant goes into a hard freeze the worse for it.

Weather lore, not a forecast: the old saying holds that a warm November is the sign of a bad winter. This November is doing its part to test it, with the 70s on tap through Saturday.

Of Local Interest

Council adopts Jonesboro’s 20-year growth plan

The Jonesboro City Council on Tuesday night approved the city’s new Comprehensive Growth Plan and Master Street Plan, which together will guide where and how the city grows over the next 20 years. The plans were drafted by DPZ Co-Design, a consulting firm the city hired, using answers from a community survey, and went first to the Metropolitan Area Planning Commission on Oct. 27. They call for building the city around community and neighborhood hubs, making it easier to get around on foot and by bicycle as well as by car, and adding green spaces and parks.

Before the meeting, the council’s Public Safety Committee unanimously approved the city’s application for a federal Bulletproof Vest Partnership grant, which repays up to half the cost of 57 ballistic vest packages for the Police Department, $129,070 in all. The council also gave a first reading to an ordinance granting a private club permit, which allows a restaurant to serve alcohol, to Delta Catfish Crawfish, Grill and Seafood on South Caraway Road; it returns for another reading at the next meeting.

A-State names an interim football coach and begins a national search

Arkansas State University confirmed Tuesday that Butch Jones will not return as head football coach, effective immediately, and named Jalen Ortiz, the associate head coach who coaches the cornerbacks, interim head coach for the last two games, beginning Saturday at Louisiana Tech. Ortiz joined the staff in 2025 after stops at Utah State, Nevada, Oregon and Wyoming; of his players he said, “They got hit with some adversity.”

Athletic director Chris Pezman thanked Jones for his “many contributions” and said the university will conduct a national search for the program’s 32nd head coach. Jones, who called his time in Jonesboro “an honor and privilege,” leaves with a 30–43 record over six seasons, including three straight bowl trips from 2023 to 2025; this year’s team is 4–6.

Police move into their new headquarters on Highland Drive

The Jonesboro Police Department cut the ribbon Tuesday on its new station at 1709 East Highland Drive, the former Shelter Insurance building, and officers are already working there. The city paid $2.75 million for the building and its contents and $250,000 for upgrades, about $3 million in all, against at least $7 million for a new building, city leaders said. It has just over 14,000 square feet, about 30 offices and 51 parking spaces, and houses administration, criminal investigations, records, community outreach and D.A.R.E.; patrol, warrants, evidence, training and other units stay in the Caraway Road building.

Police services had been scattered across five buildings since a structural problem closed the Justice Complex in the summer of 2025. Chief Rick Elliott said the department is now “consolidated down to two buildings,” adding, “not the most ideal, but under the circumstances, we’ll take it.” A single station remains the goal, he said, but it is years away and depends on money.

Dry rivers greet duck hunters ahead of Saturday’s opener

Duck season opens Saturday, but some 15 months of drought have left rivers and wetlands across the region low; the Delta is about 20 inches behind on rain. The Cache River gauge near Sedgwick reads about 1.7 feet, 2.8 feet lower than six months ago, and there is no visible water in parts of the river at the Highway 228 bridge on the Lawrence–Greene county line. The Black River at Pocahontas is barely above gauge level, though still flowing.

Randy Zellers of the Arkansas Game and Fish Commission said fewer acres of rice, whose flooded fields once held water for ducks, make it worse, and the commission does not usually pump groundwater, which is reserved for cities and farms. Warm weather has also delayed the migration, and some ducks are flying past a dry Arkansas to Louisiana. “The best that we can hope for is to get some rain, get some water on the landscape,” he said; Sunday’s modest rain chance is the only one in the forecast.

From the State

Bill would send $2 million a year to each of Arkansas’ 75 counties

State Sen. Bryan King, a Green Forest Republican, has filed Senate Bill 6 for the 2027 session to create a “Bring It Home Fund” for county governments. The first $150 million the state collects each fiscal year from its gross receipts tax, the state sales tax, would go into the fund, and once it reached that sum the state treasurer would send $2 million to each of the 75 counties, Craighead included. The bill calls this a reallocation of existing revenue, not a new tax.

Counties would have to spend at least three-quarters of their share on roads and other infrastructure, water and sewer projects, equipment, law enforcement or county debt; the rest could also go to fire departments, qualifying local nonprofits or community projects. That sales-tax money now goes to state agencies and programs under the Revenue Stabilization Law, and the bill does not say what would cover the $150 million they would lose. It carries an emergency clause and a July 1, 2027, effective date; the Legislature convenes Jan. 11.

House members choose their committees with a Medicaid fight ahead

Members of the Arkansas House met at the Capitol on Tuesday to choose their committee seats for the 2027 session, picking in order of seniority, and many of the most senior chose the committees expected to decide the future of ARHOME, the state’s Medicaid expansion covering more than 200,000 low-income adults. Four of the 10 most senior picked the Public Health, Welfare and Labor Committee, and 12 of the top 30 chose Insurance and Commerce. Majority Leader Howard Beaty Jr., a Crossett Republican, said ARHOME legislation could go through either one. Some members also chose Insurance and Commerce to weigh in on the proposed repeal of the Generating Arkansas Jobs Act, the 2025 law letting utilities charge customers for new power plants while they are being built.

Federal officials have refused to extend the approval ARHOME runs under, which expires in July. Minority Leader Andrew Collins, a Democrat, said members want a hand in the Medicaid talks because of high health costs and trouble getting care. By House tradition, the newest member to draw the last seniority number, Republican Mike Bishop of Harrison, received a $1 coin. Two new lawmakers were sworn in this week: Kara Armas of Pea Ridge, a Republican filling a Benton County House seat until Jan. 11, when her husband, Brian Armas, begins the full term he won, and Sen. Harrell Wilson of Rison, a Republican representing southeast Arkansas through January 2029.

Some families must choose between two child care programs by July 1

The state Early Childhood Commission, a panel of child care providers and advocates that advises the state Office of Early Childhood, decided Tuesday that 243 Arkansas families now getting child care help from two programs at once must choose one by July 1. The families use Early Head Start, the federal program that usually covers infants and toddlers from morning to early afternoon, together with School Readiness Assistance, the state’s child care aid for low-income families, which covers the later afternoon. Commissioners had considered a Jan. 1 deadline.

Office director Ashelyn Abney said the change should free money for a waiting list of more than 3,500 families, and that she hopes all 243 keep receiving care. Commissioners worried that families giving up one program will have to find care for the hours it covered. Abney said 20 child care centers have closed since October 2025. Gov. Sarah Huckabee Sanders has proposed $20 million a year for early childhood education in next year’s budget, and the Legislature will decide how it is spent.

Four-day school weeks and lower grades

Nearly one in five Arkansas school districts now run some or all of their schools four days a week, and new figures show those districts are more likely than others to get low state grades. Of the 49 four-day districts, 3 received an A for 2025-26, 19 a B, 18 a C, 6 a D and 3 an F, so 18 percent landed at D or F, against about 10 percent of districts statewide; the grades rest mostly on ATLAS, the state’s yearly test in math, science and English.

Education Secretary Jacob Oliva has urged the three F-rated four-day districts, Blevins, Prescott and Strong-Huttig, to return to five days, and Blevins’ superintendent says her district will ask the state for permission to do so. The department says research links four-day weeks to weaker results, though districts set their own calendars, and some have thrived: Norfork, in north Arkansas, rose from a C to an A on a four-day schedule, which its superintendent credits with better attendance and less teacher turnover.

From the Nation

Peltola wins in Alaska, giving Democrats 56 Senate seats

Democrat Mary Peltola, a former Alaska congresswoman, has unseated Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan; the Associated Press called the race Tuesday night, the last Senate contest of the Nov. 3 midterms. With about 99 percent of ballots counted she had about 50.1 percent, enough to win outright without Alaska’s ranked-choice tally. The result is unofficial until certified, and Sullivan’s campaign has said it will not comment until every ballot is counted. Peltola, who is Yup’ik, will be the first Alaska Native in the Senate.

Her win gives Democrats a 56-44 majority when the new Congress meets in January. Democrats also won the House, where they are on track for more than 250 of 435 seats, and House Democrats meet today behind closed doors to choose their nominee for speaker. Rep. Hakeem Jeffries of New York, their leader in the minority since 2023, has no challenger; if the full House elects him on Jan. 3, he would be the first Black speaker.

Trump says the U.S. is “looking into” a report of Iranian attack planning

President Trump said Tuesday in Manila that his administration is “looking into” reports that Iran may be planning attacks in Washington, after CNN reported, citing seven officials, that U.S. intelligence shows Iran developing options to strike senior officials with a “dirty bomb,” an ordinary explosive packed with radioactive material, or a chemical weapon. The reporting describes planning, not an imminent attack, and rests on a single outlet; it has not been confirmed elsewhere. Trump warned Iran would “pay a price the likes of which no country has ever paid” if an attack occurred. A White House official said there are “no credible threats to the homeland,” and Iran called the report baseless.

Separately, a federal judge in Brooklyn sentenced Asif Merchant, a Pakistani man, to 27½ years in prison. Prosecutors said he plotted on behalf of Iran’s Revolutionary Guard, the elite branch of its military, to hire assassins to kill President Trump, Joe Biden and Nikki Haley; the “hit men” he paid were undercover FBI agents, and no attack took place.

Navy secretary nominee calls the Iran war “a strain on our Navy”

Hung Cao, the acting Navy secretary and President Trump’s choice for the permanent job, a Vietnamese refugee who became a Navy special operations officer, told the Senate Armed Services Committee at his confirmation hearing Tuesday that the eight-month war with Iran is “a strain on our Navy,” though he argued it has “set China back on their heels.” He agreed with an estimate cited by Sen. Elizabeth Warren that the naval blockade of Iranian shipping costs about $32.5 million a day and acknowledged the Navy has used expired munitions after recertifying them.

Senators of both parties pressed him on long deployments, food shortages aboard ships such as the carrier Abraham Lincoln, and suicide attempts among sailors. Chairman Roger Wicker, a Mississippi Republican, supports him, but because Cao left active duty in 2021, Congress must waive a rule requiring service secretaries to be out of uniform for seven years.

Nvidia beats forecasts, and its board says Huang will not return

Nvidia, the world’s leading maker of artificial-intelligence chips, reported $115.8 billion in quarterly revenue after the market closed Tuesday, more than double a year earlier and above Wall Street’s estimates, and forecast about $130 billion for the current quarter, assuming no data-center chip sales to China; its shares rose nearly 5 percent in after-hours trading. Board Chairman Harvey Jones said the company does not intend to reinstate co-founder Jensen Huang, who resigned as chief executive last week after reports of extramarital affairs, including one with a woman suspected of spying for China. Huang has said he would be open to returning, and President Trump has said he would support it. The board has hired a search firm to find a permanent chief, and Huang is due to testify before three congressional committees Dec. 1.

New questions about the FAA’s role in the El Dorado ground stop

New reports on last Friday’s nationwide flight shutdown, traced to a router installed by the contractor L3Harris at a Federal Aviation Administration radio site in El Dorado, point to the agency’s own oversight. Little Rock station KATV reported that internal FAA documents show a draft rule change letting upgrades like El Dorado’s be approved automatically as “maintenance notifications” without the earlier review with airlines, and The Washington Post reported that an FAA official supervised the contractor and signed off on the work; each is a single report not yet confirmed elsewhere. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy has blamed a faulty router or contractor error. Sen. Jack Reed, a Rhode Island Democrat, called for an independent inspector general investigation and public hearings.

In brief

A classified computer network used by the National Security Agency, the government’s main electronic intelligence agency, went down Tuesday during what it called routine maintenance; Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton said it was not a cyberattack, and officials see no link to the recent outages at the FAA, JPMorgan Chase and United Airlines. A strong Pacific storm, an “atmospheric river” of tropical moisture, is bringing up to 4 feet of snow to the Sierra Nevada of California and Nevada this week. And new federal rules for SNAP, the food-stamp program, now require adults up to age 65 to work, train or volunteer 80 hours a month to keep their benefits, under the tax-and-spending law signed last year.

From Abroad

Trump lands in China, where Xi greets him on the runway

President Trump landed early Wednesday in Shenzhen, the southern Chinese technology hub hosting this year’s Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit, a yearly meeting of 21 Pacific Rim economies, and Chinese leader Xi Jinping met him at the foot of Air Force One, a rare gesture returning Trump’s own airport welcome for Xi in September. Xi said he wants “a constructive, strategic and stable relationship.” Before Trump arrived, Xi held talks in Shenzhen with Russian President Vladimir Putin, their 50th meeting by Xi’s count.

On Thursday the three leaders are scheduled to hold three-way talks on the Iran war, Russia’s war in Ukraine and nuclear weapons, though China’s foreign ministry has said not to expect concrete results; Trump and Xi will also meet separately, with Washington hoping to extend a trade truce past January. In Manila, Trump announced “the biggest trade deal” yet with the Philippines, but Philippine officials spoke only of “an understanding” and said nothing was signed.

Iran strikes at U.S. bases in Bahrain and Kuwait after a U.S. raid

Iran’s Revolutionary Guard said Tuesday it launched drones at a U.S. radar at Sheikh Isa Air Base in Bahrain, home of the Navy’s Fifth Fleet, the third such attack in five days. U.S. Central Command, which runs American forces in the Middle East, said the drones were shot down without damage, and U.S. forces then struck a Revolutionary Guard missile site on Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz in what an official called self-defense. Early Wednesday Kuwait said its air defenses were fighting off “hostile” drones, which Iran said were aimed at Patriot missile batteries, and Bahrain intercepted another attack; no casualties were reported.

At sea, a ship of ADNOC, the United Arab Emirates’ national oil company, was struck in the strait overnight, leaving one crewman missing and 11 injured, according to a single report not yet confirmed elsewhere. Qatar, which carries messages between Washington and Tehran, warned that escalation poses an “extremely grave danger” to the chances of a deal, while President Trump says the U.S. blockade of Iran stays in place.

A Pentagon official’s criticism of Ukraine’s deep strikes angers allies

Elbridge Colby, the Pentagon’s top policy official, told allies at NATO headquarters in Brussels on Tuesday that Ukraine’s long-range strikes inside Russia have been a “spectacle” that has “not been effective in driving an end to the war,” though he credited its shorter-range drones with slowing Russian advances. German Defense Minister Boris Pistorius called criticizing strikes on Russian military targets “quite a stupid thing,” and Britain’s defense secretary, Wes Streeting, said it is not Britain’s place to tell Ukraine how to fight. The dispute follows this week’s Ukrainian attack on the Kerch Bridge linking Russia to occupied Crimea, where Ukrainian officials say a road section collapsed; the Kremlin says the bridge “will be restored.”

Saudi Arabia replaces its Yemen commander as the Houthis near Aden

Saudi King Salman has replaced the commander of the Saudi-led coalition fighting in Yemen after weeks of gains by the Houthis, the Iran-backed rebels who have held the capital, Sanaa, since 2014, handing command to Gen. Fayyad Al-Ruwaili, chief of the Saudi General Staff. A Yemeni security official said the rebels have reached the coast about 60 miles west of Aden, the southern port that serves as the seat of the internationally recognized government; the Houthis say they do not plan to attack the city, and analysts do not see an assault as imminent. The coalition said its strikes Tuesday hit 60 military targets, and on Wednesday Yemen’s parliament voted to label the Houthis a terrorist group.

Netanyahu loses a fight over Israel’s parliamentary speaker

Israel’s parliament, the Knesset, elected Matan Kahana, a former special forces soldier allied with opposition leader Gadi Eisenkot, a former army chief of staff, as its speaker on Tuesday by 66 votes to 52, defeating the candidate of Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s Likud party. The speaker controls the chamber’s schedule and can speed or stall bills, including Netanyahu’s changes to the courts. No camp won the 61 seats needed to govern in the Oct. 27 election, and Netanyahu leads a caretaker government while coalition talks go on; if no government forms, Israel faces another election early next year.

In brief

The United Nations climate summit, COP31, in Antalya, Turkey, enters its last days split over a draft deal due Friday, with the Arab Group and Russia rejecting language on moving away from fossil fuels and the European Union backing a goal of 35 percent electrification by 2035; any agreement needs the consent of nearly 200 countries. On Thursday the International Court of Justice, the U.N.’s top court, is scheduled to rule on whether Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya, a mostly Muslim minority.

Afield & Afloat

Ratings are rule-of-thumb estimates from the forecast, the rut calendar and the sun, not measurements of animal or fish behavior.

Deer outlook: Good

The rut is still in its peak window for north Arkansas, dawn is cool at about 38 to 40, the wind is light at about 6 mph, and the barometer, high at 30.38 inches, eases slowly through the day. Windows: about 6:12 to 8:40 a.m.; 10 a.m. to 2 p.m. is realistic with bucks cruising; and 2:53 p.m. to sunset at 4:53. Tip: with the afternoon climbing to the upper 60s, deer will bed in shade and on the cool north faces of the ridges by midday; hunt the morning hard and the last hour of light, and if you sit through midday, watch the thick edges between bedding cover and the nearest water, since dry woods send deer to drink.

Tomorrow, Thursday (estimate: Good). A much milder dawn near 50, nearly calm winds and the rut still on, though the afternoon reaches about 70. Windows: about 6:13 to 8:43 a.m., 10 a.m. to 2 p.m., and 2:52 p.m. to sunset at 4:52. Tomorrow’s tip: in still air your scent pools and drifts rather than carrying away, so play the thermals: sit low in the morning while the air sinks, then climb higher in the afternoon as it rises.

Fishing outlook: Good

A slow fall in pressure, an afternoon near 67 and a light chop make for a good day on Craighead Forest Lake and Lake Frierson. Best times: late morning through mid-afternoon as the sun warms the water, and the last hour before the 4:53 sunset. Tip: after the cold nights, the north banks that take the full afternoon sun warm first; work a small crankbait or a spinnerbait slowly along them for bass, and look for crappie suspended around brush piles and the fishing piers at 8 to 12 feet, where a small minnow under a slip cork does the work.

Tomorrow, Thursday (estimate: Good). Steady pressure easing slightly, a warm afternoon near 70 and very light wind. Best times: late morning to mid-afternoon and the last hour before sunset. Tomorrow’s tip: on a glassy, bright day the fish see you coming, so lengthen your casts, scale down to lighter line, and try bream and catfish around the deeper riprap with crickets or nightcrawlers.
