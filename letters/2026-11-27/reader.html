In field and woods: most of the hardwoods on Crowley’s Ridge are bare now, but the oaks, the white oaks especially, will hold their brown leaves into winter, rattling in every breeze; the old folks said an oak keeps its coat until the new buds push it off in spring. Out in the Delta the harvest is done, and many rice fields lie flooded behind their levees for the ducks; at dusk, look up for the long, wavering lines of geese and mallards working south along the Mississippi Flyway. Late fall and early winter, while the trees are dormant and the ground is still soft, is a good time to plant trees and shrubs, so a fruit tree or a redbud set in this week will have the whole winter to grow roots. On clear, cold nights like tonight, listen for the barred owl’s call from the creek bottoms, eight hoots that sound for all the world like “Who cooks for you? Who cooks for you-all?” which, the day after Thanksgiving, is a fair question.

Of Local Interest

Community Thanksgiving Meal marks 30 years, rain and all

Volunteers served the Jonesboro Community Thanksgiving Meal for about the 30th time Thursday at St. Bernards Auditorium on East Washington Avenue. Guests could eat there, take a plate home, or have one brought to the door, and most meals go out as home deliveries. Volunteers prepared about 2,500 plates, and organizers reported 1,960 meals given out by the end of the day. They set up and worked in cold, windy, misty weather after wet snow fell Wednesday night.

The meal started in the mid-1990s with a few local churches, among them First United Methodist Church, and by 1999 several congregations had joined to make it a single community event. Jim Grisham, who helped start it, was still there this year helping set tables. His daughter, Barbara Grisham Sanders, now runs it with a five-person committee and a good many telephone calls to area churches, and she said the same churches say yes every year. Juanita Huggins and Megan Thiesse, a special-education teacher, have each volunteered for more than 20 years. The meal is meant for families who cannot manage a traditional dinner, elderly and homebound neighbors, people without homes and anyone with no one to eat with. Richard Carvell, a longtime volunteer who died in September 2025, used to describe it as being for “the hungry, the homeless, or the lonely.” Anyone who wants to help next year can write to jonesborocommunitythanksgiving@gmail.com.

Runners, dispatchers and lineworkers work through a wet holiday

Cold rain did not keep runners home Thursday morning. More than 70 people started the Disabled American Veterans Turkey Trot at 7 a.m. on the Arkansas State University campus. The DAV is a veterans’ service organization, and the entry fees will go toward replacing two vans that carry disabled veterans to medical appointments in Little Rock and Memphis. Jenny Fain of Jonesboro ran it again in honor of her father, who died this year of complications from his service in the Vietnam War, and she called the race “a great way to start your holidays.” The free Gearhead Run Club trot at Craighead Forest Park collected food for the Food Bank of Northeast Arkansas, and Bennett Zipfel of Gearhead Outfitters ran it dressed head to toe as a turkey.

At the Craighead County emergency dispatch center, which answers 911 calls and sends out police, firefighters and ambulances, the holiday brought fewer calls than usual but a bigger share of real emergencies, and the weather brought more wrecks. Shift supervisor Jason Dyck said that by noon dispatchers had sent help to 86 calls across the county, which is about normal for a holiday. On an ordinary day the center takes 400 to 500 phone calls, which lead to roughly 180 to 240 calls for service. Four dispatchers and a trainee worked the shift.

The weather also cut power to some homes in the region. As of 2 p.m. Thursday, Clay County Electric had more than 270 customers out, Entergy more than 60 in Randolph and Crittenden counties, and Craighead Electric more than 40 across Greene, Lawrence and Poinsett counties. Christmas at the Park, the drive-through light show at Joe Mack Campbell Park, opened Thursday night as planned, but as a drive-through only, for $10 a vehicle, with Santa’s Village closed because of the weather. Director Adam Sartin said every one of the more than 400,000 lights stayed on. The park plans to open fully tonight if the weather allows.

Illinois man held on $200,000 bond in U.S. 67 drug case

A Springfield, Ill., man is charged with trafficking a controlled substance, a Class Y felony, which is the most serious class of felony in Arkansas, after a traffic stop Tuesday on northbound U.S. 67 in Craighead County near the Lawrence County line. According to a sworn statement filed in court, a deputy stopped a car with Texas license plates and no insurance on record. The driver, Antonio L. Higgins, 40, said it was a rental and that he was moving back to Illinois from Texas.

Higgins agreed to a search, the statement says, and the deputy found a black bag of wrapped packages that together weighed about 11 pounds and tested positive for methamphetamine and fentanyl. Higgins told the deputy he did not know what was in them. District Judge Barbara Halsey found probable cause for the charge Wednesday and set bond at $200,000, with an ankle monitor required if he is released. He is held at the Craighead County Detention Center and is due in court at 8:30 a.m. Dec. 16. The charge is an allegation; he has not been convicted.

From the State

Runoffs Tuesday in 32 counties, and Clarksville votes on $55 billion in data center bonds

Arkansas voters in 32 counties return to the polls Tuesday, Dec. 1, for runoff elections that will settle dozens of mayoral and city council races left undecided on Nov. 3. Under state law, a city or county race goes to a runoff when more than two candidates ran and no one won a majority; the top two finishers meet again. A 2025 law did away with the old rule that let a leader with at least 40 percent of the vote skip the runoff by finishing 20 points ahead of second place, which is one reason so many races are back on the ballot. There are no runoffs for state or federal offices.

Among the contests around the state: Jacksonville Mayor Jeff Elmore faces James Bolden III; Fort Smith chooses between Christina Catsavis and Daniel Williams for mayor; Helena-West Helena Mayor Joseph Whitfield, whom Gov. Sarah Huckabee Sanders appointed in October 2025, faces Raymond “Tank” Simes; Bella Vista Mayor John Flynn faces Wendy Hughes; and Ouachita County picks a county judge between Republican Michael Tyler and Democrat Rayford Wright. Stuttgart, Bald Knob and several other towns also have mayoral runoffs, as do several races in our own corner of the state.

The most unusual question is in Clarksville, the Johnson County seat in the Arkansas River Valley, where voters will decide whether to uphold two city ordinances authorizing up to $55 billion in industrial development bonds for a data center campus planned by the company Serverfarm: up to $50 billion for equipment and improvements and up to $5 billion for land. The company says it, not the city or taxpayers, would repay the bonds; a simple majority decides each measure. Registered voters may vote Tuesday even if they skipped the November election. Early voting ends Monday, Nov. 30, and polls are open 7:30 a.m. to 7:30 p.m. on Election Day; sample ballots and polling places are at voterview.ar-nova.org.

Attorney general turns back proposed $15 minimum wage amendment

Arkansas Attorney General Tim Griffin, the state’s chief lawyer, has rejected the wording of a citizen-proposed ballot measure that would raise the state minimum wage from $11 an hour to $15 by 2032, one dollar a year, and raise the cash wage for tipped workers from $2.63 to $6.25 an hour. Before backers of an initiative can gather signatures, state law requires the attorney general to certify that its ballot title fairly and impartially summarizes it for voters; this one, filed by Arkansas resident Makenzie Hall under the title “Arkansas Fair Wage and Tipped Employee Act of 2028,” fell short, Griffin’s opinion said.

The opinion found the title did not adequately explain the measure or make clear whether it would repeal, amend or add to the existing Arkansas Minimum Wage Act. It also flagged technical problems: the required enacting clause sits at the end rather than the beginning, the measure calls its title a “common name” instead of the “popular name” the law requires, and one section uses inconsistent wage terms. Griffin wrote that the list was not complete and that the opinion takes no position on whether raising the wage is a good idea.

The sponsor may revise the proposal and resubmit it, and the 2028 date in the title points to the next general election ballot. The fight over wages is also running through the Legislature, where several Democratic lawmakers have filed minimum wage bills ahead of the January session.

Data center judge closed courtroom for secret testimony; ruling still pending

New details have emerged from the Nov. 23 federal court hearing in the fight between AVAIO Digital Partners, the company building a large data center campus in southeast Pulaski County, and the county itself. U.S. District Judge Lee Rudofsky briefly closed the Little Rock courtroom so that two AVAIO executives, project manager Tom Nesel and partner Kevin Murphy, and Jake Nabholz, head of Nabholz Construction, could testify out of public view. What they said under seal is not known; in open court they steered clear of details about the company’s long-term power arrangements with Entergy Arkansas, its possible tenants and future expansion.

AVAIO is asking the court to throw out a county ordinance, passed by the Quorum Court in October over outgoing County Judge Barry Hyde’s veto, that requires large data centers to obtain a new permit and to undergo fresh review if they grow in size, electric demand or water use. In open testimony, Murphy said AVAIO began looking at Arkansas around April 2023 for its power, land, fiber lines and state sales-tax breaks, signed a power agreement with Entergy in December 2025 and counts “hyperscalers” such as Amazon, Microsoft and Oracle among its customers. The project was announced in January at 150 megawatts, with room to grow to a gigawatt. The county’s lawyer argued the ordinance does not even apply, since AVAIO’s site plan was approved in August, and that later phases are only “hypothetical.”

Judge Rudofsky, who earlier refused to sign a proposed settlement between the two sides, said he intends to rule soon on AVAIO’s request to block the ordinance while the case goes on. Separately, the Arkansas Public Service Commission, which regulates utilities, has yet to rule on the AVAIO-Entergy power deal or on Entergy’s request to build the transmission lines the campus would need.

Farmers told to sign up for federal safety-net programs on paper if need be

A cyberattack on the U.S. Department of Agriculture that began more than a week ago is still causing trouble for Arkansas farmers with a Dec. 11 deadline looming. The attack knocked out USDA computer systems, including Farm Service Agency systems farmers use to enroll in Price Loss Coverage and Agriculture Risk Coverage, two federal programs that pay row-crop farmers when prices or revenues drop sharply. USDA Under Secretary Richard Fordyce said systems were shut down in response to the threat and that no personal information was lost, but farmers have had little word since, and it is unclear whether online enrollment works.

Tyler Oxner, senior economist at the Arkansas Farm Bureau, said the USDA has indicated it will not extend the Dec. 11 deadline. Those payments matter: Arkansas row-crop farmers lost an average of $243 an acre last year to low prices and rising costs, and many use the checks to pay down loans whose interest keeps running. Oxner urged farmers to visit their county Farm Service Agency office, where paper forms are available, and said the Farm Bureau will speak up for anyone who misses the deadline because of the outage.

Six Arkansas hospitals traded inpatient beds for emergency-only status. Results are mixed

Arkansas has six “rural emergency hospitals,” a federal Medicare category created in 2020 that lets small rural hospitals of 50 beds or fewer stop admitting overnight patients in exchange for a monthly federal payment of more than $3 million a year and somewhat higher Medicare rates for outpatient care. Only Mississippi, Oklahoma and Texas have more. The Arkansas hospitals are in Pocahontas, DeWitt, Eureka Springs, Helena-West Helena, Osceola and Camden.

Administrators describe a lifeline with costs. Ouachita County Medical Center in Camden, which made the switch in July and is in Chapter 11 bankruptcy, has made payroll with the federal money but has seen emergency visits fall and has shed about a third of its staff; chief executive Glenda Harper said the hospital has not “decided for sure that it was the best idea for us,” since seriously ill patients may be sent as far as Fort Smith when Little Rock beds are full. In Pocahontas, St. Bernards Five Rivers Medical Center, the first in the state to convert in 2023, says inpatient care had been only about 5 percent of its business, and it has since bought an MRI machine that has more than doubled monthly scans; its administrator, Randy Barymon, said the one thing the staff would really like back is “swing beds” for patients recovering after a hospital stay. Leaders in Helena and DeWitt said they could hardly stay open without the money.

A national critic, Harold Miller of the Center for Healthcare Quality and Payment Reform, calls it a myth that inpatient care is what sinks rural hospitals, and Mercy decided against converting its four western Arkansas hospitals. Eureka Springs Hospital says it may switch back. In Washington, U.S. Sen. John Boozman has signed on to a bipartisan bill that would loosen the program, including allowing swing beds.

State puts $8.5 million into reading tutoring grants for young students

Families of Arkansas kindergarten through third-grade students who are struggling to read can again apply for state grants of up to $1,500 for tutoring, after Education Secretary Jacob Oliva announced in a memo this week that the state will put $8.5 million into the program this year. The grants were created by the LEARNS Act, the broad 2023 education law pushed by Gov. Sarah Huckabee Sanders, and can pay for in-person or online tutoring or reading software from state-approved providers.

Students must attend a public or charter school and either fall below state reading standards, be judged at risk of reading trouble, or have been excused from the new rule that third graders must read at grade level to move to fourth grade. Money goes first come, first served, with priority for students who were held back a year and then for the lowest-scoring readers; school districts must notify eligible families.

The state says the push is paying off: just under 43 percent of third graders were proficient in English on last spring’s state test, up from about 36 percent the two years before. Last school year was the first under the third-grade reading requirement, though the department says 85 percent of students who missed the standard received a “good-cause” exemption.

Organ donation group sues attorney general over sweeping records demand

Southern Legacy of Life, the nonprofit that coordinates organ donation for much of Arkansas, has sued Attorney General Tim Griffin’s office in Pulaski County Circuit Court to stop a broad demand for its records. Griffin’s office served the group on Oct. 21 with a 15-page “civil investigative demand,” a kind of investigative subpoena, alleging violations of the state’s deceptive trade practices law and asking for staffing details, hospital partnerships, financial audits and records of every donor referral, including the reason for each unsuccessful transplant.

The nonprofit argues that it does not engage in the kind of business that law covers, that the state had no written complaint as the law requires, and that the demand reaches nearly every document it holds and threatens the privacy of donors and recipients; the state says no protected health information would be disclosed. A spokesman for Griffin said the office “fundamentally disagrees” and will make its case in court. The two have clashed before: in August 2025 a federal judge blocked a state law, Act 861, that would have let certain people undo a donor’s organ gift after death, in a suit the group brought. No hearing date has been set.

From the Nation

Hundreds of thousands still in the dark after the Thanksgiving storm

The powerful coastal storm that soaked the East Coast on Thanksgiving left nearly a million homes and businesses without electricity at its height, and hundreds of thousands were still waiting for power on Friday morning, chiefly in Pennsylvania, New Jersey and Maryland. Pennsylvania had the most, with about 152,600 customers out early Friday; New Jersey and Maryland each had well over 100,000 out Thursday night. New Jersey Gov. Mikie Sherrill declared a statewide emergency, and New York Gov. Kathy Hochul declared one for New York City, Long Island and Westchester County. Wind gusts reached 56 mph at LaGuardia Airport in New York, and coastal flooding swamped streets in Avalon, Ocean City and Margate City on the New Jersey shore; an Avalon town official called it some of the worst flooding the town has seen, though “not quite Hurricane Sandy.”

The storm also disrupted water and travel. The Washington Suburban Sanitary Commission, which supplies 1.9 million people in the Maryland suburbs of Washington, asked every customer to conserve water because outages threatened its two filtration plants. More than 2,500 flights were delayed and about 460 canceled on Thursday, the Chesapeake Bay Bridge-Tunnel closed for several hours, and seawater breached the dunes on North Carolina’s Hatteras Island. On the storm’s cold back side, St. Louis measured 7.3 inches of snow and Ste. Genevieve, Missouri, 14 inches; the roof of an empty ice arena in Wentzville, Missouri, collapsed early Thursday.

In New York, the 100th Macy’s Thanksgiving Day Parade, postponed for the first time in its history, remains set for Saturday morning at 8:30 Eastern (7:30 Central), when the weather is expected to be dry with light winds. Some visiting performers have had to scramble: the 500-member marching band from Pearland High School in Texas raised $350,000 so it could stay two extra nights. Coastal flood warnings on Long Island were to run through Friday afternoon before conditions improve.

Trump calls revised growth figures “fake” and demands an inquiry

President Trump used his Thanksgiving Day posts on Truth Social, his social media site, to accuse the Commerce Department of deliberately understating the nation’s economic growth before the Nov. 3 midterm elections, in which Democrats won control of both houses of Congress. On Wednesday the department’s Bureau of Economic Analysis, which keeps the official national accounts, revised its estimate of growth from July through September to a 4.3 percent yearly pace, up from 3.4 percent. Mr. Trump called the first figure a “massive, and purposeful, error,” asked “Why wasn’t the correct number issued before the Election?” and demanded an “immediate investigation.” He offered no evidence. The bureau revises its figures on a fixed schedule set in advance, as it always has, and the department did not respond during the holiday. Mr. Trump fired the head of the Bureau of Labor Statistics in August 2025 after a weak jobs report he called “rigged.”

The same holiday message attacked other targets. Mr. Trump thanked “the 2% of our Somalian population that have a job” and said “the others should be thrown out, immediately,” a reference to the large Somali community in Minnesota; told deported immigrants, “We miss you dearly, but you will never be back”; and criticized opponents of his White House renovation. Later in the day, speaking by phone from his Mar-a-Lago estate in Palm Beach, Florida, with first lady Melania Trump, he thanked troops serving overseas and told them “we have victory at hand” in the war with Iran (see From Abroad). He also paid tribute to Palm Beach County sheriff’s detective Alan Smith, a bomb-squad technician killed early Thanksgiving morning when a wrong-way driver struck his vehicle on Interstate 95.

Arkansas joins 42 states fighting the sale of millions of private AI chats

A bipartisan group of 42 state attorneys general, Arkansas among them, has asked a federal bankruptcy court in Delaware to stop Character.AI, a company whose app lets people hold long conversations with made-up “characters” powered by artificial intelligence, from auctioning off its users’ private chats. Character.AI filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in October, and its sale, set for Dec. 1, would include the chat histories of more than 21 million U.S. accounts, about 2.1 million of them belonging to minors, along with other personal data.

The attorneys general argue that the buyer could ignore the company’s privacy policy, resell the data and disregard users’ requests to delete it, even though the company had told users their information would not be sold. They also point out that the only bidder disclosed so far, Smirks & Pangs Acquisition LLC, was registered in Delaware on Nov. 13, the day before Character.AI picked it as the opening bid, and that its plans for the data are unknown. The states asked the court to reject the sale as written or at least to keep children’s chats out of it; California Attorney General Rob Bonta called the sale illegal. Character.AI has said users’ data “continues to be protected under our Privacy Policy.” The court has not yet ruled. The company has also faced lawsuits over child safety, including one over the suicide of a 14-year-old Florida boy.

Credit agency warns it may downgrade Nvidia over its board

Regulators clear SpaceX for up to a million satellites; astronomers object

The Federal Communications Commission, which licenses the use of the airwaves and of satellites serving the United States, has given conditional approval to SpaceX, Elon Musk’s rocket company, to put up to one million satellites into orbit to serve as data centers in space, part of a plan the company calls Starmind. Ratings are rule-of-thumb estimates from the forecast, the rut calendar and the sun, not measurements of animal or fish behavior.

Deer outlook: Fair

The late rut is still on, with older bucks checking the last few does, and a cool dawn near 35 helps. Against that, the barometer is holding nearly steady and there is no weather coming to push deer to feed, so movement should be ordinary rather than lively. Best windows: from 6:21 a.m. to about two hours after sunrise at 6:51, and from 2:49 p.m. until sunset at 4:49.

Today’s tip: the woods will be quiet while much of the county is at the stores, and Thursday’s soaking left the leaves damp and soft underfoot, ideal for slipping into a stand without a sound. With a northwest breeze this morning, approach from the south or east so the wind carries your scent away from where the deer will be, and sit the downwind edge of a thicket or a bottleneck between bedding cover and food. Around Jonesboro, in Zone 4, it is bows only until the Dec. 26 hunt; in Zones 3, 6 and 8 the rifles are out through Dec. 6.

Tomorrow, Saturday (estimate: Good). A clear, cool dawn near 39 and a barometer that begins to slide ahead of Sunday’s front should keep deer on their feet longer. Windows: 6:22 a.m. to about two hours after sunrise, and 2:49 p.m. to sunset at 4:49.

Tomorrow’s tip: deer often feed hard the afternoon before a front, so Saturday evening is the sit to plan for. Choose a stand on the edge of a cut field, a food plot or an acorn flat, settle in by two o’clock, and stay until legal light ends; the last half hour is when a cautious buck is most likely to step out.

Fishing outlook: Fair

The barometer is steady after Thursday’s storm, which fish generally like, and the overcast keeps the light soft, but the water is cold after two raw days and the breeze is brisk this morning, so expect a slow bite on Craighead Forest Lake and Lake Frierson. Best times: late morning through midafternoon, as the water warms a little, and the last hour before sunset at 4:49.

Today’s tip: slow everything down. Crappie hold tight to brush piles and standing timber in 10 to 15 feet of water now; a small jig or a minnow fished straight down, barely moving, is the ticket. Bass will sit near the deepest cover they can find; drag a jig or a Texas-rigged plastic worm along the bottom, pausing often.

Tomorrow, Saturday (estimate: Good). A slowly falling barometer ahead of Sunday’s front, a light chop from a 10-mile-an-hour breeze, overcast skies and an afternoon near 59 add up to the best day on the water this week. Best times: late morning through midafternoon, and the last hour before sunset at 4:49.

Tomorrow’s tip: the warm afternoon will draw baitfish and bass toward the shallower, north-facing banks that catch the most sun and the wind-blown shorelines where the chop stirs up food. A slow-rolled spinnerbait or a medium-running crankbait worked parallel to those banks is a good way to find them; bream and catfish should bite on worms in the same warm water.
