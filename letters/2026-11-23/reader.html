The front that came through Sunday has left a crisp, honest November morning behind it: about 38 degrees at six o’clock under a clear sky, with a bank of cloud drifting over around nine, when it is near 41. The sun wins out by noon, and the afternoon is bright and dry, about 47 at midday and 49 or 50 by three, with a steady breeze of 12 or 13 miles an hour that makes it seem cooler than the thermometer says. There is no rain in the forecast at all today. After sunset the air cools quickly, to about 42 by six and 38 by nine.

Tuesday brings the coldest dawn of the season so far, near 31, and a gray day under thick cloud that tops out about 45. Wednesday, the great travel day, is raw and damp, with clouds all day, a high near 43 and about a three-in-ten chance of rain, mostly light. Thanksgiving Day itself looks kind: a cloudy start near 40 giving way to sunshine and about 51 in the afternoon. Friday is sunny and near 55, and the weekend warms to about 63 on Saturday and 67 on Sunday, fine weather for the ball games and the drive home.

The sun rises at 6:47 and sets at 4:50, giving ten hours and three minutes of daylight, about a minute and 23 seconds less than Sunday’s. The moon is 95 percent lit and will be full around Tuesday, the 24th, the last full moon before winter begins; with clear skies tonight it will light the yards almost like a lantern. On this date in 1804, Franklin Pierce, the 14th president, was born in Hillsborough, New Hampshire.

In field and garden: Tuesday’s dawn near 31 may be the first true freeze of the season for many yards around Jonesboro and Brookland, so this afternoon is the time to bring in potted ferns and geraniums, unhook garden hoses and wrap any outdoor faucets and well pumps that sit in the open. Pecans are falling in the yards now, and a walk under the trees this afternoon should fill a bucket for the Thursday pie; let them dry in a cool place a week or two before cracking if you can wait. In the kitchen, if your turkey is still frozen, the old rule from the Department of Agriculture is about one day in the refrigerator for every four to five pounds, so a big bird should go in the refrigerator today. In the woods the oaks are dropping the last of their leaves, and squirrels are busy burying acorns where the fallen leaves will cover them.

Weather lore, not a forecast: an old country saying holds, “If there’s ice in November that will bear a duck, there’ll be nothing after but sludge and muck.” Tuesday’s freeze will not make ice that thick, so by the old reckoning the winter has not yet shown its hand.

Of Local Interest

It was a quiet weekend at home, as the weekend before Thanksgiving usually is, and most of the news is of the holiday sort.

Schools out, trash days shifted for Thanksgiving week

Jonesboro Public Schools are out all week, today through Friday, for the Thanksgiving holiday. City trash pickup in Jonesboro runs as usual Monday through Wednesday, but the city’s Sanitation Department is closed Thursday and Friday; under the city’s 2026 collection calendar, the Thursday route will be picked up next Monday, Nov. 30, and the Friday route on Tuesday, Dec. 1. Check the city’s calendar if your days fall later in the week.

Brookland city offices close Thursday. The Brookland Planning Commission, which reviews building and zoning requests for the city, next meets Tuesday, Dec. 1, at 7 p.m.; items for its agenda are due by noon Wednesday. In Jonesboro, the City Council’s Finance and Administration Committee, which reviews spending and budget matters before they go to the full council, meets Tuesday at 4 p.m.

NEA Baptist lights its tree and gathers gifts for children

NEA Baptist Memorial Hospital, the Jonesboro hospital on East Johnson Avenue, held its annual Christmas tree lighting in the hospital lobby Sunday evening, with carols, refreshments and visitors dressed as Santa and as Elsa from the film Frozen to greet the children. Admission was free, and guests were asked to bring new socks and underwear for Children’s Homes, a faith-based ministry that provides foster care and residential care for children. Ty Jones, the hospital’s marketing director, said the evening is a way to “give back to the community,” and that it also lets patients and their families come out of their rooms and enjoy the season during a hard time.

A dry year, but the Christmas trees held up

Months of drought have left rivers across Northeast Arkansas low or dry in places, but Crow’s Nest Christmas Tree Farm, on Crowley’s Ridge in Greene County, expects a normal season. Owner Kimberly Dale, who began growing trees about ten years ago, said her Virginia pines mostly came through, apart from a few stressed trees and some early yellowing, and that rain in the coming weeks would help. Farms that depend on irrigation may have a harder year, she said, and hers plans to irrigate in future dry years if it must. The farm’s last day of store hours this season is Sunday, Dec. 6.

Winter markets keep a paycheck coming for small farmers

Shoppers filled the Judd Hill Christmas Market in Jonesboro on Saturday, and for the local growers who sell there, the holiday markets bring in money after the regular farmers market season ends. “The winter markets definitely help because I don’t really have anywhere to set up anymore now,” said Andrew Allen, who manages Sunshine Farms; he said the markets also let him meet customers and show them where their food comes from. The Judd Hill Farmers Market has one more winter market this year, Saturday, Dec. 19, from 9 a.m. to 2 p.m. at 3350 Aggie Road.

From the State

Data center hearing resumes this morning in federal court

A federal hearing over AVAIO Digital’s planned $6 billion data center near Wrightsville, in southern Pulaski County, resumes at 8:30 a.m. today in Little Rock before U.S. District Judge Lee Rudofsky. The company is asking for a preliminary injunction, a court order that would keep Pulaski County’s new data-center ordinance from being enforced while its lawsuit goes forward. The Quorum Court, the county’s elected governing board, passed the ordinance in October over the veto of County Judge Barry Hyde.

On Friday the judge refused to sign an agreement that AVAIO and lawyers for Judge Hyde drafted during a recess, which would have declared that the ordinance “does not apply” to the company’s site plan; he said it “all looks a little too perfect” and that he would not sign anything binding future county officials. The two sides may still settle on their own; otherwise he will rule on the injunction. County Judge-elect Wendell Griffen, who takes office Jan. 1, has said he expects to enforce the ordinance within weeks.

Supreme Court ruling revives old child sex-abuse lawsuits

Lawsuits against a former Pulaski County pediatrician and the owners of a former youth home are moving forward again after the Arkansas Supreme Court ruled 3-2 this month that the Legislature could revive expired claims of childhood sexual abuse. The 2021 Justice for Vulnerable Victims of Sexual Abuse Act opened a two-year “look-back window” for such suits, and the Nov. 5 ruling upheld it, overturning some 180 years of state precedent; Chief Justice Karen Baker and Justice Shawn Womack dissented.

In one case, four plaintiffs accuse former pediatrician and camp counselor James Nesmith of abusing them between 1995 and 2007; it returns to Pulaski County Circuit Court, where their lawyers plan to ask for a trial date. In the other, 16 plaintiffs sued the owners of the former Lord’s Ranch in Warm Springs, Ted and Shirley Suhl, and a former counselor; a motion asks a federal judge to reinstate plaintiffs he had dismissed, and the defendants must answer by Dec. 2. The accusations are allegations and have not been proved in court. Because the window has closed, few new suits are expected.

Bar exam scandal leaves a question mark on some lawyers’ records

Some lawyers who passed the Arkansas bar exam during the years under investigation are finding an asterisk on their files with the National Conference of Bar Examiners, the national group that keeps applicants’ exam records; what the mark means has not been explained, and it does not by itself show that anyone’s score was changed. Nancie Givens, who ran the Arkansas Supreme Court office that gives the exam until she resigned in February, is accused by prosecutors of altering results for at least 64 people between July 2020 and July 2025, moving points between applicants. She faces 65 felony counts of tampering with a public record and has pleaded not guilty.

Six of the seven justices have said that most applicants who were told they passed did earn passing scores and that affected examinees will be told once their status is confirmed. No motive has been given, and authorities have not said whether any applicant knew of the changes.

A look back at the election: gains, but no top office

An analysis published this morning of the Nov. 3 election finds that Black candidates made gains in Arkansas but did not win a federal or top statewide office; Arkansas remains the only former Confederate state never to have elected a Black member of Congress. In the 2nd Congressional District, which covers central Arkansas, Democrat Chris Jones, a pastor and nuclear engineer, lost to Republican Rep. French Hill by less than a percentage point, the closest a Democrat has come to a federal win in the state in 16 years. State Sen. Fred Love, the Democratic nominee for governor, lost to Gov. Sarah Huckabee Sanders by double digits.

Wendell Griffen became the first Black person elected Pulaski County judge, and Democrats gained 10 seats in the state House, enough to end the Republicans’ three-fourths supermajority, the 75 votes needed to pass most budget bills, when the Legislature meets in January.

At Little Rock’s airport, federal security screeners expect about 47,600 passengers between Nov. 20 and Dec. 1, with the Sunday after Thanksgiving the busiest day. From the Nation

Army says it will detonate Iowa explosives Wednesday despite a court pause

The Army says it will carry out a controlled detonation of degraded lead azide, a highly sensitive explosive used in detonators, at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant near Middletown, in southeastern Iowa, on Wednesday, even though a federal appeals court has paused the permit that allowed it. In a filing late Saturday with the 8th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals in St. Louis, the Justice Department said the Army would rely on an emergency exception in federal environmental law that does not depend on the permit, because “the Army must perform a controlled detonation to protect the public from the more significant threat from an uncontrolled explosion.” The material, in 68 barrels found in March, has crystallized, and officials say it could go off on its own once temperatures fall below 30 degrees.

Two environmental groups, the Iowa Environmental Council and the Sierra Club, have asked the court to stop it, questioning whether safer methods were fully considered; their lawyers say the government is trying to get around the court. Residents nearby have been told to stay indoors with windows shut, bring pets and livestock inside and clean outdoor surfaces afterward, because the blast could scatter lead-bearing dust, and some of Middletown’s roughly 400 residents have been advised to leave. The court had not ruled on the groups’ request as of the latest reports.

Report ties woman in Nvidia scandal to Chinese spy agency; Rep. Crawford presses for answers

The New York Times reported Sunday that Alice Huang, the woman at the center of the scandal that led Nvidia, the leading maker of artificial-intelligence chips, to remove co-founder Jensen Huang as chief executive, was in contact with an official of China’s Ministry of State Security, its main spy agency, from spring 2025 until this fall, while the two were romantically involved; ABC News said its own sources confirmed the account. The two are not related. It is not known whether she passed any inside information to China; she has denied similar allegations before, and no charges have been filed against anyone. She formerly ran Megaspeed, a Singapore company federal investigators have examined since 2024 over claims it helped Nvidia chips reach China in violation of U.S. export rules.

On Fox News Sunday, Rep. Rick Crawford, the Jonesboro Republican who represents Arkansas’ 1st District and chairs the House Intelligence Committee, said he and the committee’s senior Democrat, Rep. Jim Himes of Connecticut, wrote to Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick nine days ago asking whether advanced chips reached China, and “we haven’t heard back.” He called a subpoena “premature” and said Nvidia’s leaders are expected at a closed-door congressional interview Dec. 1. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said on CBS that “we do need answers” from the company about whether it had “a culture of compliance.”

Rep. Virginia Foxx concedes North Carolina seat after recount

Rep. Virginia Foxx, a Republican who has represented North Carolina’s 5th Congressional District for 11 terms, conceded to Democrat Chuck Hubbard on Sunday after a recount left him ahead by 330 votes; the machine recount finished Saturday gained her a single vote. Foxx said she called Mr. Hubbard to congratulate him, and he thanked her for “decades of service.” It was North Carolina’s last undecided House race from the Nov. 3 election, and it adds one more seat to the Democrats’ incoming House majority.

Senate Democrats spar over leadership; officials split on the outages

Sen. Chris Van Hollen of Maryland, who is challenging Sen. Chuck Schumer of New York to be majority leader when Democrats take control of the Senate in January, said on NBC’s “Meet the Press” that his support is “north of 15” senators and that “momentum is in our direction”; Mr. Schumer says he has the votes to stay on, and published counts differ widely ahead of the secret-ballot vote on Dec. 1. Mr. Van Hollen called the filibuster, the rule that requires 60 votes to pass most bills, “an anti-democratic provision,” and President Trump wrote that it would be “very hard for me to work with Chuck Schumer” as majority leader.

On the same Sunday programs, officials differed on whether the recent string of government and corporate computer outages and hacks, including the breach of the FBI’s national crime database, are connected. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said, “We are not under systemic attack,” and former Homeland Security Secretary Jeh Johnson said the incidents are not “the product of one common bad actor,” while Republican Sen. John Kennedy of Louisiana said he had “no idea.” Congress must pass a spending bill by Dec. 11 to avoid a government shutdown.

From Abroad

Climate talks end without a deal for the first time since 2009

The United Nations climate summit known as COP31, in Antalya, Turkey, ended Sunday without adopting its main agreement, after the European Union and others refused a final text that did not commit to moving away from fossil fuels. “I understand that there is no agreement on this text. For that reason, these decisions have not been adopted,” said the summit’s president, Turkish Environment Minister Murat Kurum, who said he would not offer a new draft. Every one of the nearly 200 countries must agree, so a single objection can block a deal; the talks ran about two days past their scheduled end, the longest such summit on record, and it is the first annual climate summit to close without an overall deal since Copenhagen in 2009.

The EU, Britain, Norway, Panama and island nations such as the Marshall Islands, Tuvalu and Vanuatu objected because the text only “recalled” the 2023 Dubai pledge to transition away from fossil fuels, while Russia said it could accept no fossil-fuel wording at all and Saudi Arabia and other oil producers resisted stronger language. Speaking for the EU, Ireland’s climate minister, Darragh O’Brien, said the talks had been “hijacked by some oil states”; U.N. Secretary-General António Guterres said he was “deeply disappointed.” The United States sent no delegation for the second year in a row.

The failed text would have acknowledged for the first time that warming is on course to pass 1.5 degrees Celsius (2.7 degrees Fahrenheit) and called for at least tripling aid to help poor countries adapt by 2035. More than 90 countries separately signed a voluntary pledge to expand the use of electricity. The open questions go to midyear talks in Bonn, Germany, in June and to next year’s summit in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia; earlier agreements remain in force.

Russian drones kill at least 15 around Kyiv

A Russian drone struck a five-story apartment building overnight Saturday in Dymer, a town about 28 miles north of Kyiv, Ukraine’s capital, killing at least 11 people, regional officials said; a 7-year-old boy was pulled out alive after nearly 14 hours under the rubble. On Sunday evening a drone hit a supermarket in Vyshneve, a suburb southwest of the city, while shoppers were inside, killing three and injuring 15, and a strike on a home in the Bila Tserkva district killed one more. The Kyiv regional governor, Tymur Tkachenko, declared today a day of mourning.

President Volodymyr Zelensky called the attacks “constant Russian terror” and said Russia had fired some 2,700 long-range drones, 1,460 glide bombs and 165 missiles at Ukraine over the past week. A Ukrainian assault regiment said a counteroffensive had retaken 14 villages, about 79 square miles, in northern Kharkiv province near the Russian border; Russia disputes it (unconfirmed). Russian President Vladimir Putin said Thursday that Russia would not negotiate after Ukraine’s strikes on the bridge to occupied Crimea.

Russian warship sunk at a Libyan port by sea drones

The Merkury, a small Russian navy warship known as a corvette, was hit at dawn Sunday by sea drones, unmanned explosive boats, while docked in Benghazi, the main city of eastern Libya, setting off an explosion and fire, Libyan officials said; satellite images showed it capsized with only its bow above water. The eastern Libyan government, a rival to the U.N.-recognized government in Tripoli, said the ship had stopped to refuel under a cooperation agreement with Russia and that at least four other drones were shot down. Casualty reports vary and have not been confirmed. That evening a sea drone also hit a Malta-flagged cargo ship off Benghazi, injuring crew members who were rescued.

No one has claimed the attack, and Russia had not commented in first reports. The AP, citing unnamed sources, reported that the drones were launched from a Ukrainian-run base in western Libya (single source: AP; not independently confirmed). Eastern Libya’s prime minister, Osama Hammad, has asked the U.N. for an international investigation. It came a day after drones set a Russian cargo ship ablaze in the Gulf of Finland.

Trump says he is inclined to resume strikes on Iran after New Year if talks fail

President Trump told Fox News, in remarks reported Sunday, that he is inclined to resume major military strikes on Iran after the new year if negotiations, held indirectly with Qatar as go-between, do not produce a permanent agreement keeping Iran from nuclear weapons; he called Iran’s negotiators “professional tap dancers.” No decision has been announced, and Iran’s deputy foreign minister, Kazem Gharibabadi, said the window for diplomacy remains open but Iran is ready for any scenario.

Fighting around the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow passage that carries much of the world’s oil, went on: a British military agency that tracks shipping said two tankers leaving the strait were hit by “unknown projectiles” off Oman on Saturday, and Iran’s navy said tankers struck mines in a “forbidden” zone. Iran’s Revolutionary Guard claimed it hit two U.S. Patriot air-defense radars in Bahrain, home of the U.S. Navy’s Fifth Fleet (unconfirmed by the U.S.); Bahrain said a civilian industrial site caught fire and two residents were wounded.

Settler arson in the West Bank as Israeli coalition talks stall

Israeli settlers set fire to Palestinian cars and sprayed graffiti such as “Revenge” and “Death to Arabs” in several villages near Ramallah, in the occupied West Bank, early Sunday, residents said; no one was hurt, police opened an investigation and the military condemned the attacks. Gadi Eisenkot, the former army chief who is trying to form Israel’s next government after the Oct. 27 election, called it “Jewish terrorism in every sense of the word.”

Later Sunday, negotiators for Mr. Eisenkot’s party, Yashar, and Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s Likud met for more than four hours on a possible unity government without a breakthrough, and agreed to meet again; a main sticking point is Likud’s demand that the far-right National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir be included. Neither camp holds a majority of the 120-seat parliament, and Mr. Netanyahu remains caretaker prime minister; if no government is formed, Israel would vote again next year.

Afield & Afloat

Ratings are rule-of-thumb estimates from the forecast, the rut calendar and the sun, not measurements of animal or fish behavior.

Deer outlook: Excellent

Everything lines up this morning: the rut is still in its peak window for north Arkansas, the dawn is cold, near 38 and some 18 degrees colder than Sunday’s, and the barometer is climbing steadily behind the front. The one drawback is the wind, about 12 miles an hour in the morning and 11 by evening. Best windows: from about 6:17 a.m. to roughly two hours after sunrise at 6:47, and from 2:50 p.m. until sunset at 4:50; in the rut, 10 a.m. to 2 p.m. is realistic for bucks cruising.

Today’s tip: the first cold, bright morning after a front is the one to take off work for, if you can. With modern gun closed in Zone 4, bowhunters should set up on the downwind edge of a doe bedding thicket, where a cruising buck will scent-check it, and stay put through midday; in Zones 3, 6 and 8 a rifle hunter on a long field edge can watch for a buck trailing does well after the sun is up.

Tomorrow, Tuesday (estimate: Excellent). An even colder dawn, near 31, with the barometer at its highest of the week and starting to ease under a thickening gray sky ahead of Wednesday’s damp weather; the wind about 13 miles an hour in the morning and 11 or 12 by evening. Windows: about 6:18 a.m. to two hours after sunrise at 6:48, and 2:50 p.m. to sunset at 4:50, with 10 a.m. to 2 p.m. still worth a sit.

Tomorrow’s tip: deer often feed hard ahead of a change in the weather, so the last two hours of daylight Tuesday may be the best evening of the week; hunt the food: a cut bean field, a winter wheat planting or a white-oak flat still dropping acorns. Frost on the ground makes every step loud, so get to the stand early and let the woods settle.

Fishing outlook: Fair

The cold snap has only just arrived, and fish on Craighead Forest Lake and Lake Frierson tend to sulk for a day after a sharp drop like Sunday’s; the bright sun, an afternoon near 50 and a light chop from the breeze help some. Best times: late morning through mid-afternoon as the water warms, and the last hour before sunset at 4:50.

Today’s tip: look for the warmest water you can find: a north-bank pocket or a rocky shoreline that has had the sun on it since noon. A slowly worked jig or a small suspending jerkbait, paused for several seconds between twitches, suits cold-front bass, and crappie should be tight to brush in 12 to 18 feet of water.

Tomorrow, Tuesday (estimate: Fair). A cold, cloudy day near 45, with the barometer starting a slow fall ahead of Wednesday’s rain, which often wakes fish up for a feed. Best times: late morning through mid-afternoon, plus the last hour before sunset at 4:50.

Tomorrow’s tip: overcast skies let fish roam a little shallower; try a small crappie jig or minnow under a slip float over brush near the creek channel, and for catfish, cut shad or chicken liver fished on the bottom off the dam or a deep point.

Deer. Zone 4, which includes Craighead County, closed its modern gun season Sunday evening; Zone 4 hunters now have archery through Feb. 28 and the statewide Christmas holiday modern gun hunt, Dec. 26-28, and Zone 4 has no muzzleloader (Alternative Firearms) season. In Zones 3, 6 and 8, modern gun runs through Sunday, Dec. 6, followed by the Dec. 12-14 Alternative Firearms weekend and the same holiday hunt. The limit in every zone is four deer, no more than two antlered bucks; in Zone 4 only one antlerless deer may be taken by modern gun, while Zones 3, 6 and 8 allow three antlerless by gun and muzzleloader combined. The youth modern gun weekend returns Jan. 2-3.

Ducks and geese. The first duck segment is open through Sunday, Nov. 29, then closes until the second, Dec. 10-23, and the third, Dec. 26 through Jan. 31. The daily limit is six ducks, with no more than four mallards (two hens), three wood ducks, three pintails, two redheads, two canvasbacks, two black ducks, one mottled duck and one scaup; Canada geese two a day, white-fronted geese two, and snow, blue and Ross’s geese 20. On wildlife management areas, shooting ends at noon. Thanksgiving morning in the blind is an old Arkansas custom; mind the low water, and check the weekly water report on agfc.com before you go.

Small game. Quail season runs through Feb. 7 (six a day), rabbit through Feb. 28 (eight a day) and squirrel through Feb. 28 (12 a day, dogs allowed).

Not thankfull, when it pleaseth me;
