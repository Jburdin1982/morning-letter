The moon, three-quarters lit and still waxing, climbs the eastern sky through the afternoon and will be full around Tuesday, Nov. 24, the moon the old almanacs call the Beaver Moon, for the season when trappers set their lines before the ponds froze.

These last warm days are borrowed time, and the wise household spends them on the chores the cold will make miserable. With a freeze forecast for Wednesday morning, it is the weekend to drain and coil garden hoses, wrap the outdoor spigots, bring in the potted geraniums and ferns, and pick the last green tomatoes to ripen on a windowsill. Turnip and mustard greens and the collards will shrug off the frost and taste the sweeter for it.

In the yard and the woods, pecans are dropping under the old trees along the farm lanes, and the persimmons have turned soft and sugary on the bare limbs, a feast for possums, raccoons and the deer that slip out at dusk. The dry fall has left the ditches and sloughs low, so Sunday’s rain will be welcome on the land as well as at the duck holes.

Weather lore, not a forecast: an old saying holds, “When the wind is in the south, the rain is in its mouth,” for a steady south wind carries warm, moist Gulf air ahead of an approaching front. This weekend’s balmy southerly breezes and Sunday’s showers fit the rhyme nicely.

Of Local Interest

Former deputy prosecutor sues Hagood, alleging she was fired over her pregnancy

A former deputy prosecutor in the Second Judicial District, the prosecuting attorney’s office that handles criminal cases in Craighead, Mississippi and four other Northeast Arkansas counties, has sued her former boss in federal court, alleging she was fired days before her maternity leave was to begin. Presley Phillips Noel filed the suit Oct. 14 in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Arkansas against Prosecuting Attorney Sonia Hagood and Chief Deputy Prosecutor Jessica Thomason; the case came to public notice this week.

According to the complaint, Ms. Noel joined the office in January 2023, had complications during her pregnancy, submitted her maternity leave paperwork on Jan. 8 of this year and was fired on Jan. 9, two months before her due date. She also says that a 2025 transfer to Mississippi County raised her workload from two dockets to six and added more than two hours of daily driving. As she describes the office’s answer to her federal discrimination complaint, it pointed to a poor May 2025 evaluation and claims of insubordination; she calls that evaluation false and notes she received a $32,000 merit raise two months after it.

The suit claims violations of the Family and Medical Leave Act, the Pregnancy Discrimination Act, the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act and the Constitution’s equal protection guarantee, and seeks more than $75,000 in damages, reinstatement or front pay, and a jury trial. These are allegations only; Ms. Hagood did not respond to a request for comment, and no answer had been filed in court as of Thursday. U.S. District Judge James M. Moody Jr. stepped aside from the case, which is being reassigned to another judge.

City Water & Light trades wooden poles for steel

City Water & Light, Jonesboro’s city-owned electric, water and sewer utility, is replacing wooden power poles across town with steel ones, and most are already in the ground. Slade Mitchell, the utility’s energy, marketing and purchasing director, said the poles were bought at “pennies on the dollar” from a federal surplus that had been intended for hurricane relief in Puerto Rico; no dollar figure was given.

The standard 70-foot steel poles are rated to withstand hurricanes and are expected to last about 50 years or more, against roughly 30 for wood, which the utility says should mean steadier power as winter comes on. Work remains in a few places, including a stretch of Matthews Avenue, and crews expect to finish over the coming months alongside other projects.

Fire marshal’s office offers Thanksgiving kitchen advice

Thanksgiving is the worst day of the year for home cooking fires, and Jason Wills, division chief in the Jonesboro Fire Marshal’s Office, is asking families to take care. The National Fire Protection Association estimates fire departments answered 1,139 home cooking fires on Thanksgiving Day in 2024, more than three times the daily average.

Chief Wills advises staying in the kitchen while food cooks, keeping children back from the stove and never putting water on a grease fire; smother it with the pot’s lid instead, and keep that lid close at hand. Those frying a turkey should thaw it fully, not overfill the pot with oil and set up at least 10 feet from the house; grills belong outdoors, never in a garage or on a wooden deck. The Jonesboro Fire Department will install smoke detectors free of charge, and in a fire, get out and call 911.

From the State

Lawsuit takes aim at Arkansas’ ban on buying soda and candy with SNAP

A federal class-action lawsuit filed Wednesday seeks to overturn the U.S. Department of Agriculture waivers that let Arkansas and 17 other states bar food-stamp shoppers from buying soda, candy and similar items. SNAP, the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, is the federal grocery benefit for low-income households. Arkansas’ waiver, sought by Gov. Sarah Huckabee Sanders and approved by the USDA, took effect July 1 and forbids SNAP purchases of soda (diet and zero-calorie included), candy, energy drinks and certain prepared desserts.

The suit was brought by the National Center for Law and Economic Justice, a New York-based advocacy group for low-income people. It argues that the USDA overstepped its legal authority by letting states redefine what counts as eligible food, skipped required procedures before granting the waivers, and left both shoppers and grocers confused by a patchwork of state rules. It also says some recipients rely on the banned items for chronic health conditions, and that stores risk penalties for an honest mistake at the register. “A backdoor method to restrict access to SNAP,” the center’s Katharine Deabler-Meadows called the waivers.

The governor’s office says the plan returns SNAP to its purpose of nutrition and fits the federal “Make America Healthy Again” campaign; the USDA has defended the waivers as giving states room to encourage healthier choices. The filing changes nothing for now: Arkansas’ restrictions stay in force unless a court orders otherwise. Shoppers unsure of an item can check it with the state Department of Human Services’ SNAP Companion App, which scans barcodes.

Exceptional drought now covers more than half of Arkansas

More than 55 percent of Arkansas is now in “exceptional” drought, the most severe category on the U.S. Drought Monitor, the weekly federal map of dry conditions, according to Thursday’s update. Every part of the state is in some stage of drought heading into Thanksgiving week, and a dozen or more counties in north-central Arkansas remain in moderate to severe drought despite scattered showers.

Jeff Hood, a National Weather Service meteorologist in Little Rock, said this summer was among the hottest on record and that the state saw some of its five driest months on record; in places the rainfall shortfall stretches back more than a year. Lakes across the state sit below their normal levels, and Lake Maumelle, the main water supply for Little Rock and North Little Rock, is expected to fall 13.5 feet below full by the end of December without a good soaking.

County judges have ordered burn bans across much of south and Central Arkansas, including Pulaski County, and wildfire danger is rated high there. With most crops harvested, the state Department of Agriculture reminds farmers that crop-residue burning is allowed only when field edges have been properly disked. The outlook offers little help: highs in the 70s this weekend, a cold front next week with several freezing nights, and little or no rain through much of the week.

Report: Most new mothers who lose Medicaid at 60 days have no documented insurance

A new state report, set for review this morning by the Arkansas Legislative Council (the panel of lawmakers that oversees state government between sessions), shows how many new mothers lose health coverage two months after giving birth. Arkansas pays for pregnancy care through Medicaid, the joint federal-state health program for low-income people, but ends that coverage 60 days after delivery. It is the only state that has not lengthened the period to 12 months.

According to the Department of Human Services report, 5,039 women reached the end of postpartum coverage between July 1 and Sept. 30. About 62 percent stayed eligible for some other form of Medicaid, but 1,902 lost coverage, and only 129 of those, roughly 7 percent, were recorded as having other insurance afterward. The department says that figure undercounts, since it reflects only women who reported new coverage on their own. Thirty-one of the mothers who lost coverage were teenagers.

Christin Harper of Arkansas Advocates for Children and Families, a nonprofit policy group, called for “extending pregnancy Medicaid to 12 months.” Gov. Sanders has opposed the change as “duplicative” of other coverage; her office pointed to the Healthy Moms, Healthy Babies Act, which directs $45 million a year to maternal and infant health. The issue is likely to return when the Legislature convenes its regular session Jan. 11.

Griffin announces six arrests in year-long massage-parlor trafficking probe

Arkansas Attorney General Tim Griffin’s office announced Thursday that a year-long investigation into illicit massage businesses in Northwest Arkansas has produced six arrests, an effort his office described as the largest criminal operation it has ever carried out. Investigators believe the businesses were part of networks that lured women from China with promises of jobs in the United States, then forced them into commercial sex work.

The inquiry began in October 2025. On Oct. 6 of this year, the attorney general’s Special Investigations Division and 16 other federal, state and local agencies served search warrants at four businesses in Rogers, Springdale and Fayetteville and at a Springdale home, making five arrests. The sixth suspect was arrested Tuesday in California by the U.S. Marshals Service.

All six are charged with engaging in a continuing criminal gang, organization or enterprise; laundering criminal proceeds; and second-degree promoting prostitution. The charges are allegations, and none has been convicted. Another suspect remains at large and is believed to have fled to China by way of Mexico. Three women were offered help during the October raids, and two accepted services; the investigation continues.

Walmart raises its outlook, but shares slip

Walmart, the Bentonville-based retailer that is Arkansas’ largest company, reported Thursday that sales rose 5.2 percent to $188.8 billion in the quarter ended Oct. 31, while net income fell 19.5 percent to about $4.9 billion. Adjusted earnings of 65 cents a share edged past Wall Street’s 64-cent forecast. Online sales grew 17 percent worldwide, and its U.S. stores saw sales at established locations climb 2.9 percent; higher diesel prices added roughly $2 billion in costs.

Chief Executive John Furner said the company was “growing our core business and gaining market share.” Walmart raised its full-year forecast, now expecting sales growth of 4.9 to 5.2 percent and earnings of $2.84 to $2.89 a share. Investors were unmoved: the stock fell more than 2 percent Thursday to $124.67, though it remains up nearly 24 percent this year.

From the Nation

Anthropic’s record stock debut lands on a hard day for markets

Anthropic, the San Francisco artificial-intelligence company that makes the Claude family of AI models, began trading on the Nasdaq stock exchange on Thursday under the ticker ANT. It is the largest initial public offering (a company’s first sale of shares to the public) on record. The shares were priced at $83 the night before, above the $70-to-$78 range the company had marketed, and the offering was enlarged to 980 million shares because of demand. Underwriters said the shares were as much as seven times oversubscribed, and about a fifth were set aside for ordinary investors.

The price valued Anthropic at about $1.5 trillion. Fortune put the money raised at about $79 billion, and more if underwriters use an option to sell extra shares, which tops the roughly $75 billion SpaceX raised in its June debut. The stock opened at $111.50, up 34 percent, then faded through the afternoon to close at $97.17, still 17 percent above the offering price. It did so on a bad day for the wider market: the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell about 1,100 points, and all three major indexes closed more than 2 percent lower, with technology shares weighed down by worries over Nvidia’s sales to China.

The company’s filings show revenue of $33.2 billion for the nine months through September, up from $2.5 billion a year earlier. Fortune reported a net loss of about $104 billion over the same period, mostly from the way the company accounted for convertible notes. The prospectus also warned that, without safeguards, the technology could pose “catastrophic or existential risks to humanity.” Anthropic is organized as a public benefit corporation, a type of company that must weigh a stated public mission alongside profit, and its chief executive, Dario Amodei, and six other co-founders keep majority voting control through special classes of stock.

White House cyber chief out after a week of outages

Sean Cairncross, the national cyber director, is leaving the Trump administration, the White House confirmed Thursday. His office coordinates cybersecurity policy across the federal government, and he had led it since August 2025. His exit follows a week of technology failures, including last Friday’s nationwide halt to commercial flights, an outage of a key system at the National Security Agency, slowed bank transactions and email disruptions.

Accounts of why he is going differ. A source told CNN that President Trump “fully supported firing” Mr. Cairncross because he “lacked the strong cyber credentials to perform the job,” while Fox News reported that he did not wish to serve two more years, and Axios described the parting as by mutual agreement. Kirsten Davies, the Defense Department’s chief information officer, will serve as acting director; a permanent pick needs Senate confirmation.

Administration officials and Democrats in Congress have said the outages do not appear to be cyberattacks, but no full public explanation has been given for several of them. Mr. Trump has called them unconnected “individual events.”

Senate passes bill to speed permits for energy projects

The Senate voted 62 to 33 on Thursday to pass the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, a 500-page measure meant to shorten the federal permitting process that can delay power plants, transmission lines, mines and data centers for years. The bill was a compromise worked out with the White House after years of on-and-off talks. Fifteen Democrats joined nearly all Republicans in support; 31 Democrats and two Republicans, Sens. Mike Rounds of South Dakota and Josh Hawley of Missouri, voted no.

Supporters, including Republican Sens. Shelley Moore Capito of West Virginia and John Barrasso of Wyoming, say it will make it easier to build both renewable and fossil-fuel projects and new high-voltage lines by streamlining environmental and court reviews and limiting lawsuits. Opponents, including Democratic Sens. Jeff Merkley and Ron Wyden of Oregon and groups such as the Sierra Club, say it would weaken environmental protections, favor fossil fuels and ease approval of power-hungry data centers. One provision would require large new data centers to pay for the grid upgrades built to serve them.

The bill now goes to the House, where its fate is uncertain. A spokesman for House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries said it “falls short in several areas,” and some House Republicans want changes to the transmission sections. President Trump has said he would sign it.

Army delays blast of unstable explosives in Iowa

The Army has put off until Monday its plan to blow up about five tons of degraded lead azide, a highly sensitive explosive used to set off larger charges, at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, a town of about 360 people in southeast Iowa near the Mississippi River. The detonation had been set for noon Friday. Acting Army Secretary Adam Telle announced the delay Thursday evening, saying “public safety is the Army’s top priority,” and that Monday is the latest safe moment before a forecast cold snap, since cold raises the risk the material could explode on its own.

The 68 barrels were stored for more than 20 years, and an inspection this year found that water keeping the explosive stable had partly evaporated. The Army says moving them is too dangerous. Officials expect the blast to send a plume of “some lead-containing particles” downwind, possibly into northwest Illinois; lead is a toxin that is especially harmful to young children.

Lawmakers of both parties in Iowa and Illinois criticized the short notice, since many communities as far as 100 miles away learned of the risk only on Sunday, and Gov. Kim Reynolds asked for the delay. Environmental groups led by the Sierra Club’s Iowa chapter have asked a federal appeals court to block the Environmental Protection Agency’s emergency permit. Residents are advised to stay indoors with windows shut, bring in animals and feed, and wet-clean outdoor surfaces afterward.

California subpoenas Nvidia over former chief’s ties to a suspected spy

California Attorney General Rob Bonta issued an investigative subpoena to Nvidia, the Santa Clara company whose chips power most artificial-intelligence systems, his office said Thursday. He is examining whether actions by Nvidia or its former chief executive, Jensen Huang, let China obtain advanced chips or confidential information in violation of U.S. export limits, and whether the company misled investors about that risk. “Companies cannot mislead their investors,” Mr. Bonta said.

Mr. Huang resigned last week after admitting to extramarital affairs, one reportedly with Alice Huang (no relation), a former executive of Megaspeed, a Singapore company under investigation for allegedly diverting restricted Nvidia chips to China. Reports have described her as a suspected Chinese spy, but U.S. Attorney General Todd Blanche has said neither the Justice Department nor the FBI has confirmed that, and no public evidence shows Nvidia technology was improperly passed on. Nvidia has opened its own inquiry and says it has “no reason to believe” its secrets or national security were compromised. Its shares have fallen about 22 percent since Mr. Huang admitted the affair.

Record flooding in Florida as a storm eyes the East Coast

The St. Johns River near Cocoa, on Florida’s Space Coast, rose above its all-time record on Thursday, passing a mark set in October 1953, after another 6 to 8 inches of rain fell overnight on already soaked Brevard County. Rescue crews, including the Florida National Guard, pulled people from flooded homes in Cocoa, Palm Bay residents near a canal were ordered to evacuate, and Brevard schools are closed until after Thanksgiving. The river was expected to crest around 18.2 feet on Friday, with a flood watch into Saturday evening.

The low-pressure system behind the rain is expected to move north off the Southeast coast this weekend. The National Hurricane Center gives it a medium chance of briefly becoming a tropical or subtropical storm, which would be named Kyle. Either way, forecasters expect strong winds, dangerous surf and coastal flooding from Florida to the mid-Atlantic into early next week, just as early Thanksgiving travel begins.

From Abroad

Russia pounds Ukraine; a Russian missile comes down in Poland

Russia launched one of the largest air attacks of the war overnight into Friday, firing more than 110 missiles and over 400 attack drones at Ukraine, according to Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy. In Kyiv, local authorities said at least three people were killed and nine injured; a ballistic missile damaged the Podilskyi Bridge over the Dnipro River, and the Darnytskyi and Southern bridges were also hit, and parts of five city districts lost power. The strikes came hours after President Vladimir Putin, speaking in China, vowed to answer three Ukrainian attacks this week on the Kerch Bridge, the road-and-rail link between Russia and occupied Crimea.

During the barrage, a Russian Kh-101 cruise missile crashed in a field near the village of Nowy Lipowiec in eastern Poland, a NATO member, Polish Defense Minister Władysław Kosiniak-Kamysz said. Polish authorities are investigating two other sites in the southeast, which they believe were a second missile and a jet-powered drone. No one was hurt and no important infrastructure was damaged. Mr. Kosiniak-Kamysz called it “yet another provocation by Russia,” noting that the countries backing Ukraine, known as the Coalition of the Willing, were holding exercises nearby that day, though Warsaw has not said Poland was the intended target. Prime Minister Donald Tusk called in ministers, military and intelligence chiefs, and the Russian embassy did not respond to requests for comment.

The attack followed a Thursday meeting in Berlin where Mr. Zelenskyy, leaders of more than 40 partner countries and President Trump’s envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner discussed peace efforts. Mr. Trump phoned in from Air Force One and, by several accounts, told skeptical Europeans that Mr. Putin wants to end the war.

Shenzhen “peace declaration” draws doubts at home and abroad

The joint statement that came out of Thursday’s first-ever meeting of President Trump, China’s leader Xi Jinping and Russia’s Vladimir Putin, held in Shenzhen during the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit, turned out to be four short pledges. The three agreed that nuclear war cannot be won and that nuclear risks should be reduced, that oil supplies should increase, that the United Nations needs unspecified reforms, and that state officials should be shielded from what they called “jurisdiction abuse” by the International Criminal Court, the war-crimes court in The Hague that has an arrest warrant out for Mr. Putin.

The text says nothing about Russia’s war in Ukraine, the U.S. war with Iran or Taiwan, and sets no date for nuclear arms talks. Mr. Trump rated the meeting “12 out of 10,” and Mr. Xi called it “positive and fruitful.” Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer called the trip a “historical embarrassment,” and CBS News and the Associated Press described the outcome as more symbol than substance.

Mr. Trump also left China without the meeting with North Korea’s Kim Jong Un he had floated, blaming “too many meetings” and saying “we’ll have that meeting at some point.” The same day, Rafael Grossi, head of the International Atomic Energy Agency, the U.N. nuclear watchdog, told its board that North Korea has finished the outside of a new extension to its uranium-enrichment plant at Yongbyon, its main nuclear site, calling the expansion “extremely worrying.”

Israeli tanks cross the Gaza truce line in a rare raid

Israeli tanks pushed west of the “Yellow Line,” the boundary that has divided Israeli-held and Palestinian-held Gaza since the U.S.-brokered ceasefire of October 2025, in a raid into the Nuseirat refugee camp in central Gaza that began Wednesday. The military said it was destroying a Hamas tunnel that threatened its troops and announced Thursday that the operation had ended and the main north-south road had reopened. It was the first time since the truce that Israel publicly announced a raid that deep past the line.

Gaza’s Hamas-run government media office said about 6,000 people fled the area; some returned Thursday while others stayed away, citing gunfire. Hamas accused Israel of trying to wreck the ceasefire, and Defense Minister Israel Katz said the army is ready to take more land. Gaza health officials say at least 1,600 Palestinians have been killed since the truce began, and the Israeli military says five of its soldiers have been killed in that time.

Climate talks near their end with fossil-fuel language dropped

The United Nations climate summit known as COP31, in Antalya, Turkey, reached its scheduled final day on Friday with a slimmed-down draft that leaves out any explicit mention of moving away from fossil fuels. The new nine-page text, half the length of the earlier draft, also drops a proposed target of $120 billion a year by 2035 to help poorer countries adapt to climate change, though it keeps an earlier pledge to triple such aid. It retains a goal of raising electricity’s share of world energy use from about 21 percent to 35 percent by 2035.

The summit’s Turkish president, Murat Kurum, called the text the “Antalya Declaration,” and Australia’s Chris Bowen, who is leading the negotiations, called it the best basis for agreement. Campaigners called it a “backward step,” and Panama has vowed to block any outcome that does not meet its demands, including a cap on oil production. U.N. talks often run past their deadline, and this one may stretch into the weekend. The United States did not take part directly for the second year in a row.

Somalia expels the United Arab Emirates’ ambassador

Somalia’s government on Thursday gave the ambassador of the United Arab Emirates, the Gulf oil state, 72 hours to leave and recalled its own envoy from Abu Dhabi. Information Minister Abdifatah Kasim Mohamud accused the Emirates of violating Somalia’s sovereignty in what he called an “open invasion,” without describing a specific new act.

Somali President Hassan Sheikh Mohamud said this week that the Emirates had signed port deals at Berbera, Bosaso and Kismayo directly with regional governments that oppose the national government in Mogadishu, and had helped set up forces outside the national army’s command. Somalia canceled all its agreements with the Emirates in January. The Emirati foreign ministry condemned the expulsion as “escalatory and hostile” and said it would harm relations.

Afield & Afloat

Ratings are rule-of-thumb estimates from the forecast, the rut calendar and the sun, not measurements of animal or fish behavior.

Deer outlook: Good

The rut is still inside its peak window for north Arkansas, the wind is nearly calm at dawn, about 4 to 5 mph, and the barometer, high near 30.29 inches at first light, eases down through the day, which often gets deer on their feet ahead of a change. The one drawback is heat: a 46-degree dawn becomes a 73-degree afternoon, and mature deer bed early on warm days. Best windows: from 6:14 a.m. to about two hours after sunrise, and the last two hours of light, 2:52 to 4:52 p.m.; with the rut on, 10 a.m. to 2 p.m. is also realistic for a buck cruising between doe groups.

Today’s tip: on a warm, light-wind day, scent drifts and pools rather than blowing away, so set up where the thermals carry it off your trail: low in the bottoms early, when cool air slides downhill, and on the ridges in the afternoon, when the warming air rises.

Tomorrow, Saturday (estimate: Good). Much the same: a dawn in the upper 40s, an afternoon near 73 and light wind around 5 to 7 mph, with the barometer falling another notch toward Sunday’s front. Windows from 6:15 a.m. to about two hours after sunrise, 10 a.m. to 2 p.m. for cruising bucks, and 2:51 to 4:51 p.m. Tomorrow’s tip: Saturday and Sunday are the last two days of modern gun in Zone 4, and Sunday looks wet, so make Saturday count; sit a pinch point near water, since deer moving in the heat will drink at midday, and the drought has narrowed where they can.

Fishing outlook: Good

A warm, sunny afternoon near 73, a light chop and a slow fall in the barometer make for a good day on Craighead Forest Lake and Lake Frierson. Best times: late morning through mid-afternoon as the sun warms the shallows, and the last hour before the 4:52 sunset.

Today’s tip: fall crappie gather on brush piles and around the fishing piers in 8 to 12 feet of water; a small minnow under a slip bobber, set just above the brush, keeps the bait in the strike zone without hanging up. Bass will chase shad toward the backs of coves in the warm afternoon, so a shad-colored crankbait or spinnerbait is worth a few casts there.

Tomorrow, Saturday (estimate: Good). Another warm afternoon near 74 with a light breeze, and the pressure dropping ahead of Sunday’s front, which often sets fish feeding. Best times: late morning to mid-afternoon and the last hour before the 4:51 sunset. Tomorrow’s tip: the day before a front is a day to cover water; try a jig or a soft-plastic craw along the riprap and points for bass, and get your fishing in before Sunday’s rain and the colder week that follows chill the shallows.
