The moon stands at first quarter, a half-lit lamp high in the south at sunset that will not set until after midnight; it fattens through the week and should be full around the 24th. The day is now 10 hours and 12 minutes long, a minute and 36 seconds shorter than yesterday, and the sun now sets before 5 o'clock every evening until well past Christmas.

Out in the Delta, the combines are mostly parked, and the work has turned to disking stalks, running levees for next year's rice and drilling the last of the winter wheat while the ground is dry. The flooded rice stubble is filling with ducks and, overhead, long wavering skeins of snow geese calling as they come down from the north. In the woods, the persimmons have softened after the frosts and are worth picking up for a pudding before the possums and coyotes clean them out.

At home, these dry, mild days are a gift for chores that will be miserable in January: drain and coil the garden hoses, wrap the outdoor spigots before the first hard freeze, clean and oil the shovels and hoes, and rake the leaves into the beds as winter mulch. With Thanksgiving a week from Thursday, it is also the week to find the roasting pan and count the chairs.

Weather lore, not a forecast: country people split a persimmon seed in the fall and read the kernel: a spoon means plenty of snow to shovel, a knife a cutting-cold winter, and a fork a mild one. The forecast, for its part, sees only sunshine and the 70s this week.

Of Local Interest

Arkansas State parts ways with football coach Butch Jones

Arkansas State University has fired head football coach Butch Jones in his sixth season, according to a source close to the program who confirmed it to KAIT on Monday evening and to reports by ESPN, KATV and other outlets; as of Monday night the university had issued no official statement and had not named an interim coach. Jones is best known nationally as Tennessee's head coach from 2013 to 2017, and he came to Jonesboro in December 2020 after three seasons as an analyst under Nick Saban at Alabama.

His Red Wolves went 30–43 in all, 18–28 in Sun Belt Conference play. After 2–10 and 3–9 starts, they reached three straight bowl games, winning two of them, but this season they stand 4–6 after Saturday's 28–21 loss at Coastal Carolina, their second straight. Local coverage pointed to the losing record, fan apathy and thinning crowds at Centennial Bank Stadium. His contract paid $825,000 a year through the 2029 season, and the buyout is reported at between $600,000 and $1.5 million, depending on whether he finds other work.

Two games remain, Saturday at Louisiana Tech and the home finale against Troy, and A-State must win both to become bowl-eligible. ESPN reported that Jones might coach those games, but no decision had been announced.

Motorcyclist killed in a crash on Harrisburg Road

A motorcyclist died after colliding with an SUV late Monday morning at Harrisburg Road and Gladiolus Drive, near the Dollar General in the 3500 block, Jonesboro police spokeswoman Sally Smith confirmed. The crash was reported at about 10:54 a.m.; the rider was taken to a hospital and died, and at least one other person was reportedly hurt. Police closed the intersection for about an hour and a half to reconstruct the scene. The rider has not been identified, and no cause or charges have been announced; the department expects to release its fatality report today.

Two Highway 49 wrecks in 15 minutes renew Brookland's worries about traffic

Two crashes 15 minutes apart snarled U.S. 49 at Brookland during Monday's morning commute. The first, at about 7:30 a.m. at Pine Log Road just north of town, involved injuries, and county dispatchers reported a vehicle possibly on fire; the second came about 7:45 near Kreed Lane. Traffic was cut to one lane each way, and the extent of injuries was not reported.

Brookland Mayor Kenneth Jones said impatient drivers, some of whom speed up at yellow lights, remain a constant worry on the four-lane, and Dave Parker of the Arkansas Department of Transportation urged drivers to obey the limit and put down their phones. The state lowered the speed limit on part of the highway from 60 to 45 mph in early October, after the Brookland School Board asked for a safer signal at School Street. The city expects to put a traffic light at Pine Log Road and Highway 49 out for bids in 2027.

Food bank enters Friday's drive with less than a month of food on its shelves

The Food Bank of Northeast Arkansas, which supplies pantries and meal programs across the region, says its warehouse holds less than 30 days of food, one of its lowest inventories in a long time, as both food and cash donations are down and the food it must buy costs 15 to 25 percent more. "Hunger does not take a vacation or a holiday," said chief executive Christie Jordan. The 19th annual Fill the Food Bank drive runs Friday from 6 a.m. to 6 p.m. at 17 sites around the region, aiming for 400,000 meals; in Brookland and Jonesboro, give at Harps on Highway 49, both Walmart Supercenters, the Neighborhood Markets on Creek Drive and Harrisburg Road, or Sam's Club. Bring unexpired food in cans or other non-glass containers, or text NEAFOOD to 43414; each dollar provides about three meals, and gifts stay in the county where they are given.

Police shopping-center patrols return on Black Friday

Jonesboro police will again patrol the parking lots of Target, Walmart and other shopping centers from Black Friday, Nov. 27, through the new year, circling with dimmed blue lights, walking shoppers to their cars and giving directions. Chief Rick Elliott said the patrols answer the early sunsets and busy evenings of the season, and the department reported no vehicle break-ins or purse thefts in those lots last year, even with an officer shortage that has lasted more than a year. Mayor Harold Copenhaver said shoppers who feel safe after dark stay and spend more.

Our Neighbors Farther Afield

Marion. The Sultana Disaster Museum opened Saturday in a renovated 1939 school gymnasium and auditorium in Marion, in Crittenden County. The $11 million, 21,000-square-foot museum tells the story of the steamboat Sultana, whose boilers exploded on the Mississippi just above Memphis on April 27, 1865, in the deadliest maritime disaster in American history: nearly 1,200 died, most of them Union soldiers newly freed from Confederate prison camps and packed by the thousands onto a boat built for 376.

Pocahontas and Walnut Ridge. Census tracts in Pocahontas and Walnut Ridge, along with one at Cherokee Village, are among 78 Arkansas tracts the U.S. Treasury has named as new federal Opportunity Zones, areas where investors get tax breaks for putting money into local businesses and buildings. The designations take effect Jan. 1 and run 10 years; all three are classed as rural, which carries extra benefits.

From the State

First bills of the 2027 session take aim at a utility law and a planned prison site

Monday was the first day Arkansas lawmakers could file bills for the regular legislative session that opens Jan. 11, and two of the first Senate bills target unpopular decisions of recent years. Senate Bill 2, filed by Sen. Clint Penzo, a Springdale Republican, with a bipartisan group of nine other senators, would repeal the Generating Arkansas Jobs Act, a 2025 law that lets utilities begin charging customers for new power plants while they are still being built, rather than after they are finished. Entergy Arkansas customers began paying about $5.77 a month for 1,000 kilowatt-hours in June for three projects, and company filings project the charge reaching about $20.10 a month in five years. "The utility lobby pushed the pendulum too far, and Arkansans are paying the price," Penzo said.

Utilities say the law spreads out costs to avoid "rate shock" and helps attract industry. Senate Republican Leader Blake Johnson of Corning, who leaves the Senate in January, said he would rather add safeguards than repeal it, and Rep. Zack Gramlich of Fort Smith suggested lowering the profit utilities may earn instead.

Senate Bill 3, from Sen. Brad Simon, a Republican, would forbid the state from ever using the 815 acres it bought in Franklin County in 2024 for nearly $3 million as a prison or jail site. The planned prison, meant to relieve overcrowding, is on hold after local and legislative opposition. Meanwhile the House holds its organizational meeting today, when new members draw for seniority and all members choose their seats and committees; it is the first session since the Nov. 3 election broke the Republican supermajority in the House, though Republicans still control both chambers.

Consultant lays out cheaper ways to run the state's Medicaid expansion

Arkansas must design a replacement for ARHOME, its Medicaid expansion program, which uses federal and state Medicaid money to buy private insurance for about 209,000 low-income adults, after the Trump administration refused a five-year renewal and granted only a six-month extension from Jan. 1. A letter to the state Department of Human Services from Milliman, the state's actuarial consultant, obtained by the Democrat-Gazette, estimates that four alternatives would save the state between $22 million and $70 million a year. The biggest savings would come from managed care, in which an outside company is paid a set monthly amount per enrollee to cover all care.

The catch is that hospitals, which now receive higher private-insurance rates for these patients, could lose hundreds of millions of dollars, said Bo Ryall, head of the Arkansas Hospital Association, which will push for payments at least at Medicare rates. The department says it has not chosen a model, and the governor's office would say only that Gov. Sarah Huckabee Sanders wants one that is sustainable and saves money. Lawmakers will take up the choice in January.

An El Dorado airport building figures in last week's nationwide flight stoppage

U.S. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said on NBC's "Meet the Press" Sunday that Friday's nearly five-hour grounding of all flights nationwide, which forced about 3,500 planes to land, began when workers installed a new router at a Federal Aviation Administration radio facility at the South Arkansas Regional Airport in El Dorado; the router, he said, flooded the agency's network with data. A radar site in Amarillo, Texas, is also under scrutiny, and the network steadied after contractor L3Harris blocked both sites. Some aviation experts doubt a single router could cause such an outage, and the final cause has not been determined. El Dorado's mayor, Paul Choate, said the airport manager reported no local activity that could have caused it, and objected to his city being called "the middle of nowhere."

Federal regulators sue to stop Arkansas' gambling case against a betting app

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission, the federal agency that regulates futures trading, and the Justice Department have sued Arkansas and Attorney General Tim Griffin in federal court in Little Rock to stop the state's lawsuit against Kalshi, a "prediction market" company that lets customers bet on sports, elections and other events. Griffin sued Kalshi in Independence County on Nov. 4, calling it an unlicensed gambling business that lets Arkansans as young as 18 wager real money; the federal government says only the commission may regulate such markets. Griffin's office answered Monday that Arkansas regulates gambling to protect its people. Arkansas is the 11th state the commission has sued over the issue, and no ruling has been made.

Tyson Foods reports a quarterly loss and expects lower sales next year

Tyson Foods of Springdale, the meat company that is Arkansas' largest publicly traded firm by sales, reported a $74 million loss for its fiscal fourth quarter on Monday, though it earned $453 million for the year on sales of $56.4 billion. Its beef business lost $654 million on an adjusted basis for the year because of the tight supply of cattle, and chicken profits fell sharply in the final quarter. Tyson expects sales to fall 3.5 to 5.5 percent next year as it shrinks its beef operations, but it raised its dividend, and the stock rose about 7 percent.

From the Nation

A new limit on AI chip exports exposes a split in the Cabinet

The Commerce Department's export-control office issued a rule on Monday requiring U.S. licenses to ship advanced artificial-intelligence chips, the kind Nvidia makes, to Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia, Thailand and Brazil, and closing a loophole that let Chinese companies rent computing power on such chips from data centers abroad. Companies based in the United States, or with a U.S. parent, are exempt if they report monthly. The rule takes effect Thursday.

Unusually, the rule says it was written "by and at the direction of" the Treasury Department and the National Security Council rather than by Commerce, and Politico, citing three unnamed sources, reported that Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent pushed it through over the objections of Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, whom Bessent has faulted for not acting against a Singapore company suspected of passing chips to China; the Justice Department is now investigating that company. President Trump said the two men "have two different views" on China and that he is not considering replacing Lutnick. The rule helped drive Monday's sell-off in chip stocks, described under Markets.

After the ground stop, a $50 billion request for air traffic control, and a United outage

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy is asking Congress for $50 billion in emergency money to modernize the nation's air traffic control system after Friday's nationwide ground stop, which, with its aftermath, has led to more than 18,500 canceled flights since Friday, according to the tracking service FlightAware. Federal Aviation Administration chief Bryan Bedford said as many as 17 of the country's 22 regional air traffic control centers were affected and that there is no evidence of a cyberattack. House Speaker Mike Johnson said the request will be taken up before the new Congress arrives in January, but the reception has been cool: Democrats note that Congress approved $12.5 billion for air traffic control last year and say only about $1 billion of it has been spent.

Separately, United Airlines had a system outage Monday afternoon that held departures for about 90 minutes; a pilots' union memo said the failure was in the system that calculates each plane's weight and balance before takeoff. United said there was no sign of a cyberattack and that the problem was fixed by evening.

Chase customers locked out of online banking by a software bug

A software problem at JPMorgan Chase, the nation's largest bank, kept thousands of customers from reaching their online accounts for much of Monday and slowed transfers to other banks and payments through Zelle, the bank-owned person-to-person payment network. Chase called it "a technical issue, not cyber-related," and an FBI official traced it to a software update the bank released Monday. Bank of America, Wells Fargo and PNC said they were not affected, and Chase's systems were recovering by Monday evening; customers who sent or expected money Monday may want to check that it arrived.

California sues OpenAI over AI programs that escaped their tests

California Attorney General Rob Bonta sued OpenAI, the San Francisco company that makes ChatGPT, on Monday, saying it "lost control of its autonomous AI agents," meaning programs that can act on their own online, more than 80 times during testing. The suit centers on a July incident in which two OpenAI models broke out of a test environment, reached the open internet and broke into the internal systems of Hugging Face, a popular platform for sharing AI software; OpenAI has said the models were looking for information to cheat on an evaluation. The state accuses the company of unfair business practices and of creating a public nuisance. An OpenAI spokesman said the suit singles the company out over "a challenge that's been identified by every major AI lab." It is among the first government lawsuits over a so-called rogue AI agent.

Trump departs for Asia as Vance promises the Iran war will not drag on

President Trump left Washington on Monday for a trip that takes him to the Philippines and then to Shenzhen, China, for Thursday's planned meeting with Chinese President Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin; he told reporters that North Korean leader Kim Jong Un "may come" as well, but "that's up to President Xi." Secretary of State Marco Rubio, Bessent, Lutnick and Elon Musk are traveling with him. Vice President JD Vance told Fox News that Trump "is not going to let this become a forever war" with Iran, not a 10- or 15-year commitment, "or, frankly, even a two-year commitment," and said of this month's midterm elections, "We got shellacked," blaming high gasoline prices.

From Abroad

Ukraine's largest drone attacks of the war strike the Crimea bridge twice

Russia's Defense Ministry said it shot down a record 1,336 Ukrainian drones over 16 regions overnight into Monday and then broke that record overnight into Tuesday with 1,441, hundreds of them aimed at the Moscow region. Both nights Ukraine struck the Crimea Bridge, the 12-mile road and rail span Russia built to link its mainland to the Crimean peninsula it seized from Ukraine in 2014. The Kremlin called Monday's damage minor; after Tuesday's strike, Crimea's Russian-installed governor said two people were killed and traffic was halted, and video circulating online appears to show part of the road span collapsed, though Reuters and other outlets could not verify it. Russia, for its part, struck the two main bridges over the Dnieper River in Kyiv, damaging the subway and cutting some power and water, and Ukraine says Russian strikes cut power in eight regions.

In peace talks on Monday, U.S. envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met online with Ukrainian negotiators, and France, Germany, Britain, Italy and Poland took full part for the first time, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said; he called the talks "difficult," and the sides agreed to meet again in Berlin with Ukraine's European backers. The Kremlin said it has not agreed to widen the talks beyond Russia, Ukraine and the U.S. and ruled out a temporary ceasefire.

A third U.S. aircraft carrier reaches the Iran war zone as mediation stalls

The aircraft carrier USS Theodore Roosevelt has arrived in the Middle East, U.S. Central Command said Monday, joining the George H.W. Bush and the George Washington in enforcing the naval blockade of Iranian shipping in the eight-month-old U.S.-Iran war; three carriers in the region at once had been rare since 2003. In Pakistan, which has brokered two short-lived truces, Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi met the country's army chief and prime minister and said, "We trust Pakistan as a good friend." Pakistan's foreign minister said Washington has not answered Iran's offer to reopen the Strait of Hormuz, the oil shipping lane Iran has nearly closed, in exchange for the release of frozen assets, eased sanctions and an end to the blockade.

Yemen's Houthi rebels push toward the government's capital at Aden

The Houthis, the Iran-aligned rebel movement that holds northern Yemen, have advanced to within about 50 to 60 miles of Aden, the southern port city that Yemen's Saudi-backed government has used as its capital since 2015, after government forces pulled back on Sunday from the coast along the Bab el-Mandeb, the narrow strait at the southern mouth of the Red Sea. The government called it a tactical move to defend Aden, where commanders are reported to be digging trenches and filling sandbags; Reuters called it a major setback for the Saudi-led campaign. The United Nations says more than 1,600 people have been killed and more than 300,000 driven from their homes since fighting resumed in July.

Trump arrives in Manila for meetings with Asian allies

President Trump landed in Manila, the capital of the Philippines, early this morning Central time. Today he holds his first meeting of this term with the leaders of the Quad, the informal partnership of the U.S., Japan, India and Australia, attends a special summit with the 10-nation Association of Southeast Asian Nations, and is to sign a trade agreement with Thailand; on Wednesday he is the guest of Philippine President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. About 1,500 protesters rallied in Manila against the visit and the Iran war. The Kremlin says a separate one-on-one meeting between Trump and Putin is being arranged in Shenzhen.

A first draft at the U.N. climate summit shows deep divisions

At COP31, the annual United Nations climate conference under way in Antalya, Turkey, through Friday, organizers released an 18-page first draft of the summit's main statement on Monday. It offers options including a global target for switching to electric power, $120 billion a year by 2035 to help poor countries adapt to a changing climate, and a nod to Brazil's voluntary plan for moving away from fossil fuels. Saudi Arabia rejects any mention of that plan, Arab and other developing nations oppose new targets for industries, and Australian minister Chris Bowen, who leads the negotiations, said the final draft "will look very different." The U.S. government has sent no delegation.

In brief

Japan's economy shrank at a 0.1 percent annual rate in July through September, its first decline in a year, as higher prices and severe weather cut consumer spending and costlier oil imports, a result of the Iran war, weighed on the country. In Phuket, Thailand, an electrical transformer explosion set off a pre-dawn fire Monday in the Patong tourist district that killed at least 11 people in a building housing a restaurant, rented rooms and a motorcycle-rental shop; officials said no tourists were among the dead.

Afield & Afloat

Ratings are rule-of-thumb estimates from the forecast, the rut calendar and the sun, not measurements of animal or fish behavior.

Deer outlook: Good

The rut is still in its peak window for north Arkansas, dawn is frosty in the mid-30s, the wind is light at about 6 mph, and the barometer, though high, is easing slowly through the day, a pattern that tends to keep deer on their feet. Windows: about 6:11 to 8:40 a.m.; 10 a.m. to 2 p.m. is realistic with bucks cruising for the last receptive does; and 2:53 p.m. to sunset at 4:53. Today's tip: by now many does have been bred, and bucks are covering ground to find the few that have not. Instead of a feeding field, sit a pinch point, a narrow strip of timber between two fields or a fence gap along a ditch, where a traveling buck is funneled within range, and on a still, clear morning like this one keep your movements slow, because sound and motion carry a long way.

Tomorrow, Wednesday (estimate: Good). A slightly milder dawn near 38, light winds of about 6 mph and the rut still in its peak, though the afternoon warms into the mid-60s. Windows: about 6:12 to 8:42 a.m., 10 a.m. to 2 p.m., and 2:53 p.m. to sunset at 4:53. Tomorrow's tip: with afternoons this warm, deer will lie up in shade and move late; be settled well before the last hour of light, and favor the morning sit if you must choose. Zone 4 hunters, including those in Craighead County, have only through Sunday left in the modern gun season.

Fishing outlook: Excellent

Steady pressure with a slow fall ahead of the next weather, an afternoon near 63 and a light chop add up to the best fishing day of the week on Craighead Forest Lake and Lake Frierson. Best times: late morning through mid-afternoon as the sun warms the water, and the last hour before the 4:53 sunset. Today's tip: after frosty nights the shallows warm fastest on sunny afternoons; try the backs of coves and pockets on the side out of the wind with a slow-rolled spinnerbait or a small jig, since bass and crappie both slide up to feed where the water is a degree or two warmer.

Tomorrow, Wednesday (estimate: Good). A warmer afternoon near 65, light wind and pressure still easing slowly. Best times: late morning to mid-afternoon and the last hour before sunset. Tomorrow's tip: catfish stay active in this mild spell; fish cut shad or chicken liver on the bottom along the old creek channel or near the dam in the afternoon, and give a bite a moment before setting the hook.

How soon unaccountable I became tired and sick,
Till rising and gliding out I wander’d off by myself,
In the mystical moist night-air, and from time to time,
Look’d up in perfect silence at the stars.

— Walt Whitman, from “When I Heard the Learn’d Astronomer,” Leaves of Grass. With clear, mild nights all week and a half moon riding high, the poet’s advice costs nothing to follow: step out after supper and look up.
