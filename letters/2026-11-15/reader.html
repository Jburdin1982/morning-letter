The day is now 10 hours and 15 minutes long, a minute and 39 seconds shorter than Saturday's, with sunrise at 6:39 and sunset at 4:55. We lose about a minute and a half of daylight each day for another three weeks or so, and then the shortening slows toward the solstice.

Two hard frosts have finished the summer garden. Pull the blackened tomato and pepper vines now, before they mat down, and feed the clean stalks to the compost pile; if garlic and onion sets are not in yet, a dry week like this one is the last easy chance. Fallen oak and maple leaves make good free mulch over strawberry beds and around shrubs, and a mower run over them first will keep them from blowing away.

In the woods the color is at its last stand: the sweetgums are wine-dark, the hickories gone to bare yellow sticks, and the white oaks hold their leaves longest, rust-brown and rattling. With leaves down, squirrel nests stand out in the treetops like black bundles, and the deer trails along the field edges are easy to read. It is a fine Sunday afternoon for a slow walk and a thermos of coffee.

Of Local Interest

Hundreds brave the cold for Jonesboro's Veterans Day parade, marking the nation's 250th year

Hundreds of people lined South Main Street in downtown Jonesboro on Saturday morning for the annual Veterans Day parade, put on by the Craighead County Veterans' Monument Foundation, the volunteer group that keeps up the veterans' memorial at the courthouse. This year the parade also marked 250 years since the founding of the United States. Several dozen floats and marchers passed beneath a large American flag strung between two fire trucks, and a ceremony followed in front of the Craighead County Courthouse.

For the first time the parade had two grand marshals, Doyle Yarbrough and Marie George. Mrs. George was among the early waves of enlisted women in the Navy and served with naval intelligence in Washington, D.C. Danny Honnoll, president of the Craighead County Historical Society, was the guest speaker, and Will Silas called the parade along the route.

Rich Norris, the foundation's president, told the crowd that the nation's 250th birthday is bound up with the sacrifices of its servicemen and women, and described the flag as standing for "America as a concept." He said his own father, a World War II veteran, was part of the reason he joined the military.

Among those in the front row were two young friends from a local Junior ROTC program, the military-led leadership class offered in high schools. When the ceremony's speaker asked who in the crowd hoped to serve, both raised their hands; one has decided on the Marines, and the other hopes to join the Army or the Navy.

Brookland band takes third in the state; Northeast Arkansas bands sweep the top spots

The Brookland High School marching band placed third in Class 5A at the state marching contest of the Arkansas School Band and Orchestra Association, the body that runs school band competitions, held at War Memorial Stadium in Little Rock. Brookland scored 85.3, behind two Greene County bands: Paragould, which won with 87.8, and Greene County Tech, second with 87.0. Paragould's director, Carlos Serna, noted that the 5A title has stayed with Paragould or Greene County Tech every one of the 11 times the contest has been held since 2016.

In Class 3A, the Rivercrest band from Mississippi County won first place for the fourth year running with a score of 87.7, and Piggott was second with 84.5. In all, five Northeast Arkansas bands brought home medals. Rivercrest's director, Sebastien Isom, credited after-school and early Saturday rehearsals and the habit area band directors have of watching one another's bands and offering advice.

Federal judge to hear Lord's Ranch abuse case in Jonesboro on Monday

A federal judge will hold a hearing in Jonesboro on Monday in the long-running lawsuits over the Lord's Ranch, a former children's treatment home near Warm Springs in Randolph County. Dozens of former residents allege they suffered sexual, physical and mental abuse there years ago; these are claims in civil suits and have not been proven in court. The ranch, run by Ted and Shirley Suhl, closed in 2016 after Mr. Suhl was convicted in a federal case involving bribery of a state official; President Trump later commuted his prison sentence.

U.S. District Judge D. P. Marshall Jr. has combined four lawsuits against those accused of responsibility for the abuse into a single case. Monday's hearing will take up arguments over that step and over whether the former residents may obtain more detailed financial information about the defendants. Lawyers for the Suhls are fighting subpoenas, or court orders for records, sent to 17 banks and other financial institutions that hold accounts for the couple and their nonprofit corporations.

Seventeen of the plaintiffs are relying on Arkansas's Delayed Discovery Act, a law allowing suits within three years of when a victim discovers the effects of abuse, after earlier courts had turned some claims away as too old. Two former employees, Emmett Presley and Alonza Jiles, are also named individually; Mr. Jiles, who now sits on the state Board of Corrections, denies knowing of any abuse.

A-State hosts young entrepreneurs this week, after a weekend of art and conversation

About 300 third- through sixth-graders from some 20 schools, including several Jonesboro magnet schools, Nettleton, Valley View, Riverside and Brookland Middle School, will gather at Arkansas State University on Thursday for a Small Business Leadership Conference. The event, from 9 a.m. to 2 p.m. on the third floor of the Reng Student Union, is put on by A-State's Scarlet to Black Program, which teaches young people about money and business. Each team has invented a toy and prepared a business plan, a print advertisement, a commercial and an "elevator pitch," a short sales speech, for a panel of judges.

Dr. Philip Tew, an associate professor of finance who directs the program, has led a version of the event since 2016; this is its 11th year, timed to National Entrepreneurship Month. Assistant director Melanie Ricker said the aim is hands-on learning that builds teamwork and an early interest in financial independence.

On Saturday, graduate students in an expressive arts and counseling class turned the ASU Museum into a free community day of watercolor and face painting, coffee and conversation, with more than 10 vendors. Visitors wrote a way of coping with stress on a sticker for their coffee cups. "The whole purpose is, we want to give people a chance to be heard in whatever way they need to be heard," said Bailey Green, a counseling student training to become a play therapist.

From the State

Lawmakers start sizing up Sanders' $6.96 billion budget as the state's surplus forecast grows

Arkansas legislators are only beginning to work through Gov. Sarah Huckabee Sanders' proposed $6.96 billion general revenue budget, and several said this week that it is too early to promise how big an income tax cut the state can afford. General revenue is the state's main pot of tax money for day-to-day spending. The plan covers fiscal 2028, the state budget year that runs from July 1, 2027, to June 30, 2028, and lawmakers will take it up when the regular legislative session opens Jan. 11.

The backdrop is a brighter revenue picture. State Finance Secretary Jim Hudson raised the expected surplus for the current budget year to $581.7 million, up from $389.6 million, and projects about $449 million left over in fiscal 2028 after the governor's spending plan, before any new tax cuts. The budget would add $150 million in state money for Medicaid, the health coverage program for low-income and disabled people, including services for 2,675 people on a waiting list for in-home support. It would also add $70 million for Education Freedom Accounts, the state's voucher-style program that pays for private school and home school costs.

Reactions split along familiar lines. House Speaker Brian Evans, a Cabot Republican, called a cut in the top income tax rate from 3.7% to 3.5% the "bare minimum" and said the voucher program is "hitting a plateau." Sen. Clarke Tucker, a Little Rock Democrat, said he is concerned about the voucher program's growth and about another tax cut aimed at top earners, though he praised new money for pre-kindergarten. Sen. Jimmy Hickey, a Texarkana Republican who co-chairs the Joint Budget Committee, said he wants to study the plan further before commenting.

Politics will matter more this time. Democrats gained 10 House seats in the Nov. 3 election, giving them 30 of 100, and because some spending bills need a three-fourths vote, Republicans will now need a handful of Democratic votes to pass many of them. The governor's office said Sanders will lay out her tax-cut plans closer to the session.

Federal crop report trims Arkansas corn and soybean yields but still points to records

The U.S. Department of Agriculture lowered its estimates for Arkansas corn, soybean and peanut yields in its November crop report, though it still expects record corn and soybean yields for the state. The November report is the department's last monthly adjustment before its final annual crop summary, due Jan. 12.

Corn was cut to 190 bushels an acre from 193, soybeans to 56 bushels from 59, and peanuts to 4,600 pounds an acre from 4,700. Rice went the other way, rising to 7,550 pounds an acre, and cotton held steady at 1,384 pounds of lint an acre. Jeremy Ross, a soybean specialist with the University of Arkansas System Division of Agriculture, said he doubts the soybean figure and expects it to be lowered again, noting that Arkansas growers counted far fewer soybean pods this fall than last year.

Harvest is nearly finished, with soybeans 96% and cotton 97% harvested, both ahead of normal. Winter wheat is behind, slowed by dry weather, and wheat prices have fallen about $2 a bushel since early September because of large crops around the world.

Arkansas shoppers may qualify for refunds in Family Dollar overcharging settlement

Family Dollar, the discount store chain, has agreed to a $63 million settlement with Arkansas and 17 other states plus the Federal Trade Commission, the federal consumer-protection agency, over charges that it rang up customers at prices higher than those posted on shelves, in ads and online. Arkansas Attorney General Tim Griffin announced the agreement Friday.

The deal sets aside $40 million for refunds nationwide and $20 million for a 15% discount on eligible merchandise. Anyone who bought something at a Family Dollar store from Jan. 1, 2023, through Oct. 1, 2026, may be eligible. The state of Arkansas itself will receive $95,321.

Family Dollar also must stop misrepresenting its prices and set up a pricing-accuracy program checked each year by an outside reviewer. A claim form is to be posted at familydollar.com within 14 days after a federal court approves the settlement; no claim deadline has been announced yet.

Benton County infant found in 1977 exhumed in hopes DNA can finally name him

The Benton County Sheriff's Office has exhumed the remains of an infant boy found in the woods nearly 50 years ago, hoping modern DNA testing can identify him and his parents. Teenagers playing in the woods found the baby's body on April 16, 1977, near a plastic bag and the placenta, and an investigator at the time believed someone had tried to hide the body. The child was buried in Bentonville Cemetery under a marker reading "Baby Boy Doe."

The remains were exhumed Oct. 8 and sent to the Arkansas State Crime Laboratory in Little Rock. When the lab finishes, the evidence will go to Othram Inc., a private company that uses DNA and family-tree research to identify unknown people, and may also be checked against the FBI's national DNA database. The sheriff's office says the case remains an open criminal investigation and that it also hopes to give the child a properly marked grave. Anyone with information is asked to call the Benton County Sheriff’s Office.

From the Nation

Router work at a remote site, possibly in Arkansas, blamed for Friday's nationwide flight shutdown

Federal officials say a newly installed router, a device that directs traffic on a computer network, set off Friday's nationwide halt of departing flights. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said the contractor L3Harris Technologies was doing routine work to switch telephone lines from copper wire to fiber at a remote site of the Federal Aviation Administration, the agency that runs the nation's air traffic control. The new router flooded the system with more than 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds, knocking out radar feeds and some radio contact with planes at 17 of the agency's 22 regional control centers.

The work was reportedly being done at a facility in El Dorado, in south Arkansas, though Duffy did not name the site and officials have also mentioned equipment in Texas; that location has not been officially confirmed. Duffy said investigators have not decided whether the fault lay with the equipment or with how it was installed, and officials say there is no evidence of a cyberattack. L3Harris said it is cooperating with the investigation.

The ground stop lasted a little more than four hours Friday and led to well over 9,000 cancellations that day and thousands more Saturday. Duffy said Saturday the system was "back at full operations," and FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford said schedules should be back to normal by the end of Sunday. Duffy has raised his request to Congress for air traffic control upgrades to $50 billion, and Bedford warned the aging system "will continue to break until we replace it."

Separately, a UPS cargo plane and a Copa Airlines passenger jet bumped while taxiing at Miami International Airport about 9:15 p.m. Saturday. Two people were treated at the scene for minor injuries, and the FAA is investigating.

Boebert ousted in Colorado as Democrats near a 56th Senate seat in Alaska

Vote counting from the Nov. 3 midterm elections is wrapping up with more Republican losses. In Colorado, Rep. Lauren Boebert, a three-term Republican known as a close ally of President Trump, lost her seat Friday to Democrat Eileen Laubacher, a retired Navy rear admiral, in a western Colorado district Republicans carried by more than 18 points in 2024. Boebert conceded in a video, saying of her party, "We collectively failed."

In Alaska, Democrat Mary Peltola, a former congresswoman, leads Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan by more than 10,000 votes with about 96% counted, roughly 49.8% to 46.1%. Because Alaska uses ranked-choice voting, in which voters rank candidates and the last-place finishers' votes are redistributed, Peltola must top 50% of first-choice votes to win outright. The state has until Wednesday, Nov. 18, to finish counting, with a ranked-choice tally to follow if needed.

Democrats already hold 55 Senate seats for the new Congress that begins in January, so a Peltola win would make it 56. Peltola would also be the first Alaska Native elected to the Senate. Alaska voters also chose Democrat Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins as governor, according to a projection by Decision Desk HQ, an election-results service.

Trump heads to Asia this week for a first-ever three-way meeting with Xi and Putin

President Trump leaves Washington on Monday for Asia, stopping first in the Philippines for a meeting of Southeast Asian nations before going to Shenzhen, China, for a summit of Pacific Rim economies. The White House announced Friday, and the Kremlin, Russia's seat of government, confirmed Saturday, that Trump, Chinese President Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin will meet together Thursday, Nov. 19. It would be the first such three-way meeting; China had not publicly confirmed it as of Saturday.

The White House said Trump wants to talk about reducing nuclear weapons and the war with Iran. A senior U.S. official said the war in Ukraine is not expected to be on the agenda because it is being handled in separate talks. Trump will also hold a lunch and meeting with Xi, their fourth since he returned to office.

On Iran, people familiar with Trump's thinking said he has told advisers he may hold off on new attacks if Iran and its allies in Yemen avoid fresh provocations, while keeping the U.S. naval blockade of Iranian ports in place.

SpaceX catches its Starship spacecraft with tower arms for the first time

SpaceX, the private rocket company led by Elon Musk, on Saturday caught the upper stage of its giant Starship rocket with the mechanical arms of its launch tower at Starbase in South Texas. The 171-foot spacecraft had spent more than 22 hours in orbit, circling Earth 14 times and releasing Starlink internet satellites, before flying back to the tower. It is the first time a spacecraft returning from orbit has been caught this way.

The 15th Starship test flight did not go entirely to plan. SpaceX hoped to catch both halves of the rocket, but the Super Heavy booster's automatic safety system canceled its catch Friday, and the booster came down and exploded in the Gulf of Mexico.

The catch matters because reusing rockets quickly is the key to cutting launch costs, and NASA is counting on a version of Starship to land astronauts on the moon, a mission now planned for 2028. Musk says the company hopes eventually to fly Starship weekly.

From Abroad

Iran fires drones at Bahrain and says tankers struck mines near the Strait of Hormuz

Bahrain, a small Gulf island kingdom that hosts the U.S. Navy's Fifth Fleet, said its air defenses shot down several Iranian drones early Sunday after sirens sent residents to shelter. Iran's Revolutionary Guard, the elite branch of its military, said it launched six drones at a U.S. air-defense radar at a helicopter base in Bahrain in response to what it called hostile U.S. actions. Neither the United States nor Bahrain confirmed any damage.

It was the second such claim in three days. On Friday the Revolutionary Guard said it destroyed a U.S. early-warning radar in Bahrain; the U.S. military's Central Command denied that, saying it shot down two drones and suffered no damage. On Saturday the Guard's navy said two oil tankers caught fire after hitting sea mines near the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow channel that carries much of the world's oil, claiming the ships had switched off their tracking systems and used an unauthorized route. The ships were not named and the claim has not been independently confirmed; the U.S. military has said it cleared the main shipping lanes of mines.

The U.S.-Iran war began with U.S. and Israeli strikes on Feb. 28 and has settled into a tense, uneven lull. Iran has sent the United States, through a Qatari mediator, seven conditions for ending the war, including lifting the U.S. blockade and sanctions, and is waiting for a reply.

Houthi rebels gain ground over a second key shipping lane as Yemeni forces pull back

Forces fighting for Yemen's internationally recognized government said Sunday they are pulling back from an airport and from the area around the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, the narrow southern entrance to the Red Sea. The move could leave the Houthis, an Iran-aligned rebel movement that controls Yemen's capital and much of the north, in control of high ground overlooking one of the world's busiest shipping routes.

The southern forces said they were shifting troops to protect Aden, the southern port city where the government is based, and the surrounding provinces, with backing from the Saudi-led coalition that supports the government. Government forces had retaken the area only last month after a Houthi push in September.

The pullback matters because ships are already avoiding the Strait of Hormuz on the other side of the Arabian Peninsula, and Houthi control near Bab el-Mandeb would give the rebels another way to threaten world trade. The Saudi-led coalition also said Sunday it shot down Houthi missiles aimed at the Saudi capital, Riyadh.

Zelensky says U.S. envoys asked whether Ukraine would give up Donbas in exchange for NATO membership

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky said American negotiators raised, for the first time, the idea of Ukraine withdrawing from the Donbas, the eastern industrial region Russia has been fighting to seize, in return for joining NATO, the Western military alliance. Zelensky said U.S. envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, the president's son-in-law, put the question to him at a meeting in Ireland on Thursday after their talks with Putin in Moscow. There has been no public comment from U.S. officials on the idea.

Zelensky said Ukraine is not ready to give up territory, but that if a full package of NATO and European Union security guarantees, a strong army and rebuilding money were confirmed, he would put the question to Ukrainians in a national vote, saying he has "no right" to decide it alone. Putin has long called Ukraine's NATO membership unacceptable.

Zelensky also said Russia and Ukraine have agreed on a prisoner exchange of more than 1,000 people, with final numbers still being worked out. European leaders have agreed that Britain, France, Germany, Italy and Poland will represent Europe at the next round of peace talks.

Netanyahu presses rival Eisenkot for a unity government; Eisenkot calls it a trick

Israeli caretaker Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu on Saturday renewed his call for a "national unity" government with Gadi Eisenkot, the former army chief whose bloc won more support in the recent election and who has been asked to form a new government. Netanyahu argued that only a broad government, without the Arab parties, could confront Iran, which he called a threat that must be ended "once and for all."

Eisenkot dismissed the appeal as "tricks," saying Netanyahu "knows he lost the election," and noted that negotiators from his Yashar party and Netanyahu's Likud party were already scheduled to meet. Eisenkot, endorsed by 67 of the 120 members of parliament, has 28 days, with a possible 14-day extension, to form a government; he has floated a minority government that could take office if Arab and ultra-Orthodox parties abstain. Israel's parliament, the Knesset, votes Tuesday on a new speaker, an early test of the two camps' strength.

France sees a fifth straight Saturday of nationwide protests

Protesters marched across France on Saturday for the fifth weekend in a row over the cost of living, school conditions and a proposed law that would give police officers more legal protection when they use their weapons. The Interior Ministry counted about 106,000 marchers nationwide, including 24,000 in Paris, the smallest turnout in recent weeks; student unions put the figure at 200,000.

Police detained 219 people nationwide, and there were clashes and barricades in cities including Lyon and Bordeaux. Supporters of the police bill, including Marine Le Pen's far-right party, say officers should not automatically be suspected of excessive force; opponents call it a "license to kill." Unions rejected a government offer to raise the minimum wage by 0.6% and have called another day of strikes for Tuesday.

On the Field

Arkansas State football lost 28–21 at Coastal Carolina on Saturday in Conway, S.C., as five turnovers undid an otherwise even game; the Red Wolves were outgained by a single yard, 271 to 270. Quarterback Trey Owens completed 23 of 40 passes for 217 yards with an interception and ran for a touchdown, and Chauncy Cobb and Timothy Carter each added a rushing score. Linebacker Joe Drew made a career-high 14 tackles. A-State falls to 4–6, 2–4 in the Sun Belt, and plays at Louisiana Tech on Saturday, Nov. 21, at 6 p.m. on ESPN+.

A-State men’s basketball bounced back with a 99–65 win over Dillard on Saturday night at First National Bank Arena. Jordan Pyke led with 25 points on 11-of-15 shooting and 11 rebounds, Chaz Watson added 19 points and four steals, and Tameron Ferguson had nine assists; the Red Wolves outrebounded Dillard 59–34 and forced 22 turnovers. A-State is 2–2 and hosts Arkansas-Pine Bluff on Wednesday at 7 p.m. on ESPN+. A-State baseball is in its offseason; its 2027 schedule, with 22 home games, is out and season tickets are on sale.

Arkansas upset ranked South Carolina 34–6 on Saturday night in Fayetteville, ending a 10-game SEC home losing streak by cashing in four Gamecock turnovers. KJ Jackson completed 15 of 24 passes for 188 yards and touchdowns to Chris Marshall and Antonio Jordan, and defensive back Joker Johnson had two interceptions, one returned 63 yards for a touchdown, plus a forced fumble. The Razorbacks are 4–6, 2–5 in the SEC, and play at Texas on Saturday at 2:30 p.m. Central; the network, ABC or ESPN, is still to be set.

Tennessee, No. 8 in the playoff rankings, won 22–7 at Texas A&M behind its defense and kicker Cooper Ranvier, whose five field goals tied a school single-game record. Trailing 7–3 in the second quarter, the Vols scored the last 19 points, including Faizon Brandon’s 5-yard touchdown pass to DaSaahn Brame, and held the Aggies to 179 yards. Tennessee is 8–2, 5–2 in the SEC, and hosts LSU on Saturday at 2:30 p.m. Central on ABC or ESPN.

Afield & Afloat

Ratings are rule-of-thumb estimates from the forecast, the rut calendar and the sun, not measurements of animal or fish behavior.

Deer outlook: Good

The second morning of modern gun season is another good one on paper: the rut is still near its peak in north Arkansas, dawn is a frosty 30 degrees, the barometer has kept climbing behind last week's front, and the breeze is light, around 6 mph. Best windows: from about 6:09 a.m., a half hour before sunrise, until about 8:40; again from 10 a.m. to 2 p.m., when rutting bucks cruise in daylight looking for does; and the last two hours before the 4:55 sunset, from about 2:55.

Today's tip: after a busy opening day, older bucks slip into the thickest cover they know: cutover, briar thickets, cane and willow flats. Rather than returning to an open food plot, set up on the edge of that cover or on a pinch point between two thickets, and stay put through the midday; hunters getting up to leave at 9 often push deer past the ones who stay. Wear your hunter orange.

Tomorrow, Monday (estimate: Good). Another frosty dawn near 32 under a clear sky, with almost no wind (about 3 mph) and high pressure. Windows: about 6:10 to 8:40 a.m., 10 a.m. to 2 p.m., and from 2:54 until the 4:54 sunset.

Tomorrow's tip: on a near-calm morning your scent pools and drifts slowly downhill and along creek bottoms, so sit above a bottom rather than in it, and walk in by a route the deer will not cross. With most hunters back at work, a weekday sit in Zone 4 is one of the best chances left before its modern gun season closes next Sunday.

Fishing outlook: Fair

A cold start and a gray, cloudy afternoon near 55 keep the bite on the slow side, though a light breeze helps. Best times: late morning through mid-afternoon as the water warms, and the last hour before the 4:55 sunset.

Today's tip: at Craighead Forest Lake and Lake Frierson, crappie are gathering on brush piles and standing timber in about 10 to 15 feet of water as the lakes cool. Fish a small jig or minnow slowly, just above the brush, and stay on a spot once you catch one. Nearby at Lake Charles, the water dipped to about 60 degrees this week and catfish were biting well on cut bait, a good sign for a channel cat or two on the Ridge lakes too.

Tomorrow, Monday (estimate: Good). Full sun, a warmer afternoon near 57, light wind and a slowly falling barometer late in the day add up to the better fishing day of the two. Best times: late morning through mid-afternoon, and the last hour before the 4:54 sunset.

Tomorrow's tip: bass will move up onto the shallow, sunny north banks (the ones facing south) as the afternoon warms them. Work a crankbait or a slow jig along rock, riprap and wood in 2 to 6 feet, and slow down when the water still feels cold.

All four zones reopen Dec. 26–28, and Zones 3, 6 and 8 have their alternative firearms (muzzleloader) hunt Dec. 12–14; Zone 4 has none. The season bag is four deer, no more than two of them bucks. Archery stays open through Feb. 28, and the second youth gun hunt is Jan. 2–3.

Duck season opens Saturday, Nov. 21, and runs through Nov. 29, then reopens Dec. 10–23 and Dec. 26 through Jan. 31. Canada, white-fronted, snow, blue and Ross’s geese follow the same three segments. Note that the first segment ends Nov. 29, not Dec. 1 as some printed guides wrongly said; check agfc.com for daily limits. The youth, veteran and active-military waterfowl hunt is Feb. 6–7.

Small game: quail is open through Feb. 7 (six a day), rabbit through Feb. 28 (eight a day) and squirrel through Feb. 28 (twelve a day). With the leaves mostly down, this is good squirrel weather for a young hunter between deer sits.

The Week in Review

At home

Two long-loved Jonesboro businesses announced they are closing: Cooksey's Flower Shop after 74 years and The Golden Grotto after 41 years.

Jonesboro's new ban on camping in city parks (Ordinance 26-038) took hold as shelters and volunteers worked to help people without homes ahead of the cold.

A Brookland man, Rickey Brown, was sentenced to 12 years in federal prison for trafficking methamphetamine.

Georgia-Pacific announced a $20 million expansion of its Anchor Packaging plant in Paragould, expected to add 10 to 20 jobs.

Hundreds turned out in the cold Saturday for Jonesboro's Veterans Day parade, which also marked the nation's 250th anniversary.

Around the state

A budget meets a new Legislature. Gov. Sarah Huckabee Sanders proposed a $6.96 billion budget with more money for Medicaid, food-aid administration and school vouchers, and lawmakers began debating tax cuts after an election that cost House Republicans their three-fourths supermajority.

Medicaid work rule stays on schedule. Arkansas declined a federal offer to delay its new Medicaid work requirement and says enforcement for many expansion enrollees will begin Jan. 1 as the state builds a replacement for its ARHOME program.

Bar exam scandal widens. Nancie Givens, who ran the Arkansas bar exam for nearly 13 years, faces felony records-tampering charges, and the state Supreme Court accuses the administrator of altering scores from 2020 to 2025, leaving past test-takers questioning results.

New pick for the federal appeals court. President Trump withdrew U.S. District Judge Lee Rudofsky's nomination to the 8th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals and named Arkansas Supreme Court Justice Cody Hiland instead, with Republicans aiming to confirm him before Democrats take over the Senate in January.

The nation and the world

Midterm aftermath. Democrats' wave took clearer shape as counts finished: Rep. Lauren Boebert lost in Colorado, Mary Peltola moved toward a Senate win in Alaska, and the outgoing House narrowly tabled an effort to impeach Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, 211-204.

Nvidia shake-up. Jensen Huang resigned as chief executive of Nvidia, the chipmaker at the center of the artificial intelligence boom, and members of Congress summoned him and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick to explain the circumstances.

Skies go quiet. A nationwide ground stop Friday canceled thousands of flights; officials blamed a newly installed router at a remote air traffic site and said there was no sign of a cyberattack.

Gulf tensions flare. Iran launched drones toward U.S. assets in Bahrain and claimed tankers struck mines near the Strait of Hormuz, while the U.S. naval blockade of Iran stayed in place and peace talks remained stalled.

A summit without Ukraine. The White House and Kremlin confirmed a first-ever meeting of Presidents Trump, Xi and Putin in Shenzhen, China, on Nov. 19, even as U.S. envoys floated a Donbas-for-NATO trade to Ukraine.

The sporting week

It was a split week for the Red Wolves: football fell 28–21 at Coastal Carolina, and men’s basketball lost 82–65 to UT Martin on Tuesday before routing Dillard. Arkansas found its best night of a hard season against South Carolina, and Tennessee kept its playoff hopes on course at Kyle Field.

The week ahead: Wednesday, A-State basketball vs. Arkansas-Pine Bluff, 7 p.m., ESPN+. Saturday, Arkansas at Texas and LSU at Tennessee, both 2:30 p.m. Central on ABC or ESPN; A-State football at Louisiana Tech, 6 p.m., ESPN+. Monday, Nov. 23, A-State basketball meets Southern Utah at 2:30 p.m. in the FIU Thanksgiving event in Miami.

It was a week of beginnings and endings: an election counted out to its last ballots, a new Legislature taking the measure of a budget, a gun season opened on a hard frost, and on Main Street a parade for the veterans. Thanksgiving is now eleven days off. May the quiet, dry days ahead give us time to gather what the year has given, and to share it.

Postscript

That time of year thou mayst in me behold,
When yellow leaves, or none, or few do hang
Upon those boughs which shake against the cold,
Bare ruined choirs, where late the sweet birds sang.
In me thou seest the twilight of such day,
As after sunset fadeth in the west,
Which by and by black night doth take away,
Death’s second self that seals up all in rest.
In me thou seest the glowing of such fire,
That on the ashes of his youth doth lie,
As the death-bed, whereon it must expire,
Consumed with that which it was nourished by.
This thou perceiv’st, which makes thy love more strong,
To love that well, which thou must leave ere long.

— William Shakespeare, Sonnet 73 (1609). The last yellow leaves are hanging on the boughs this week; the poet’s closing couplet is a good Sunday reminder to love well what we have.
