The day before Thanksgiving starts cold and gray, about 36 degrees at six o’clock under a full deck of cloud, with a north wind near 9 miles an hour. It stays dry through the morning, about 40 by nine, and the thermometer struggles to the low 40s at midday, near 43 to 44 for the high. Then the rain moves in: the chance is about 31 percent at noon, 79 percent by three o’clock and 88 percent by six, with the temperature already slipping back to about 40 by midafternoon and 36 by suppertime. Anyone driving to the grandparents’ house will want to leave early or go slowly after dark.

Tonight the rain may mix with or turn to a little wet snow as the air cools, with the low near 34 to 35; amounts look light, and ground this warm should keep most roads merely wet. Thanksgiving Day itself stays raw: about 34 in the morning and no warmer than the mid- to upper 30s by the hourly figures (the Weather Service’s own forecast is a bit milder, near 45), with rain and snow showers possible much of the day (the chance runs about 40 percent in the morning and near 76 percent by midafternoon) and a gusty northwest wind that will make it feel colder still. Then the weather turns kind for the long weekend: sunshine and about 54 on Friday, the low 60s Saturday and Sunday, with a chance of a shower Sunday, and the low 50s with sun next Monday and Tuesday.

The moon, a day past full, still shows its whole face, though tonight’s rain clouds will keep it to themselves; it rises a little later each evening now and will wane toward its last quarter in early December.

In field and garden: a cold rain on the eve of a holiday is an old Delta tradition, and the farm ditches along the Cache River bottoms will be glad of it after this autumn’s long dry spell. Cotton stalks stand shredded in the fields and the rice levees are bare, so this is the week the snow geese and ducks look for any standing water, and a slow drive past a flooded field at dusk on Friday may show them by the hundreds. In the garden, pull the last of the kale and collards before the hard freeze, or throw an old sheet over them tonight; they are sweeter for a touch of frost but not for a hard freeze. In the kitchen, the pie crust likes this weather: cold butter, cold hands and a cold counter make it flaky, and a damp day keeps the dough from cracking.

Weather lore, not a forecast: the old saying runs, “If there’s ice in November that will bear a duck, there’ll be nothing after but sludge and muck,” meaning an early freeze promises a mild, muddy winter. Tonight may bring a few flakes, but no ice fit for a duck, so the saying will have to wait.

Of Local Interest

On the eve of Thanksgiving the news at home is a wet, cold day with a few snowflakes possible, a new county budget, a longer runway now open for business and four more days to vote early in the runoffs.

The storm’s heavy snow stays to the north

The rain and possible wet snow for Jonesboro are set out under The Day Ahead; little or no accumulation is expected in town. The heavy snow from this storm stays well to the north. As of Tuesday afternoon there were no winter weather alerts in Northeast Arkansas, while winter storm watches stretched along Interstate 55 from Cape Girardeau, Mo., to Springfield, Ill. Forecasters say the northeastern counties of the region could see about an inch, and that travel south of Clay County should see very limited trouble; travelers heading north toward St. Louis today should expect possible snow-covered roads, poor visibility and heavy bands of snow.

Longer runway opens at the airport, six years after the tornado

Jonesboro Municipal Airport formally opened its strengthened and lengthened main runway at a ribbon-cutting Tuesday morning, attended by U.S. Sen. John Boozman, Rep. Rick Crawford, who represents Northeast Arkansas in Congress, and Mayor Harold Copenhaver. The main runway, known as Runway 5/23, now runs 6,700 feet instead of 6,200 and can carry heavier aircraft; it was designed to handle planes as large as an Airbus A320, the single-aisle airliner common on domestic routes. The 500-foot extension was a $5 million federal project, and another $9.9 million went to strengthening the runway.

Airport manager George Jackson said the most pressing need was corporate jets, which had been exceeding the old runway’s weight limit, and the companies that fly them and may want to locate industry in Jonesboro. The work began as an idea around 2017 and was slowed by the EF-3 tornado that struck the airport on March 28, 2020, by the COVID-19 pandemic and by several federal government shutdowns. Copenhaver said that when he took office in 2021 the tornado had devastated most of the airport, that the airport has since received more than $20 million in funding, and that it is a gateway to the city. Boozman said the upgrades could open the way for heavy private jets and possibly future jet service.

Jackson called the runway “the first steppingstone,” and said more regulatory steps remain before any larger service. A new terminal, replacing the one destroyed by the tornado, has been under construction since an August 2025 groundbreaking and is expected to be finished in the first quarter of 2027. New aircraft storage hangars, resurfacing of the secondary runway and a repaved parking lot are also planned, and a new fixed-base operator, the hangar-and-fuel business that serves private pilots, should open within about six months, with its own ribbon-cutting. The airport’s scheduled passenger service is Southern Airways Express, with flights to St. Louis and Nashville.

Quorum Court passes a $60.7 million county budget for 2027

The Craighead County Quorum Court, the county’s legislative body of elected justices of the peace, voted unanimously Monday night to approve the county’s 2027 operating budget of $60,665,743.73. That is about 1.4 percent more than this year’s budget of $59,808,690.70, according to County Judge Marvin Day, the county’s chief executive. Because it is an appropriation ordinance, a measure that sets spending, it passed on a single reading.

Most county employees will receive a 4 percent raise next year, and health insurance premiums are rising 4 percent. The budget creates a new county public information officer, whose main job will be answering Freedom of Information Act requests, the public-records requests citizens and reporters file, along with news releases and social media. The sheriff’s office budget grows to cover rising fuel costs and a step pay plan, approved in October, meant to keep experienced deputies from leaving.

The court also set next year’s property tax rates, measured in mills; a mill is $1 in tax for every $1,000 of a property’s assessed value. The county rate is 7.10 mills in all (4 for general operations, 2.10 for roads and 1 for the library). The city of Jonesboro levies 2 mills, Brookland levies none, and Bay levies 4, the highest among the county’s towns. School district rates range from 33.10 mills in the Jonesboro Public Schools to 43.38 mills in the Nettleton district.

Trial set in the Walmart parking-lot shooting

A Santa visit for children of all abilities

Arkansas State University students studying communication disorders, the field that trains speech-language pathologists and audiologists, will hold the campus’s first Sensory Santa Toy Drive from 4 to 6 p.m. Monday, Nov. 30, at the Speech and Hearing Clinic in the Donald W. Reynolds Center for Health Sciences on the A-State campus. The aim, organizers say, is to give children of all abilities, including those with different communication needs, a chance at the holiday fun.

The free event, with activities and a visit from Santa, is put on by the university’s Speech and Hearing Center, the Department of Communication Disorders and the A-State chapter of the National Student Speech-Language Hearing Association, a student professional group. Toys collected will go to Toys for Tots, the Marine Corps Reserve’s Christmas toy program; Dr. Kate Gillespie, an assistant professor and the group’s co-adviser, said students have already filled one Toys for Tots box.

Undergraduate and graduate students planned the activities and decorations and will serve as volunteers, communicating with children in American Sign Language and through augmentative and alternative communication, the picture boards and speech devices used by people who cannot rely on speech alone.

Telling medicinal seeds apart. A-State chemistry professor Dr. Argelia Lorence and colleagues at the Arkansas Biosciences Institute and two Mexican universities used DNA “barcoding,” reading short, distinctive stretches of a plant’s genetic code, to test seeds sold as five medicinal species of morning glory and one hybrid. They found that seeds sold under two different names were the same species, a sign that labels on herbal products cannot always be trusted; the study appears in the journal Ethnobotany and Economic Botany.

Our Neighbors Farther Afield

Hardy. Hardy Mayor Ethan Barnes, an alumnus of nearby Highland High School in Sharp County, has apologized “from my heart” to anyone hurt after he held a Confederate flag in the stands at the Highland Rebels’ final regular-season home football game, against Southside, on Nov. 5. West Memphis. Work on the Kings’ Crossing bridge, the planned new bridge over the Mississippi River between West Memphis and Memphis that will replace the nearly 75-year-old Memphis & Arkansas Bridge, is moving into its next phase, the Tennessee and Arkansas transportation departments said this week. The next step is choosing a general contractor in the coming months, with construction expected to begin early next year. The two states have set aside up to $200 million and plan to use a federal grant of nearly $394 million; officials call it the largest transportation investment in the history of either state.

Wynne. The Federal Emergency Management Agency, the federal disaster agency, has approved more than $743,000 for Wynne Water Utilities to cover grant-management costs from its recovery after the severe storms and tornadoes that struck Wynne in 2023.

From the State

Car-Mart files for bankruptcy and agrees to sell itself

America’s Car-Mart, the Rogers-based used-car chain that sells and finances cars largely for buyers with weak credit, filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy Tuesday in federal bankruptcy court in Texas. Chapter 11 lets a company keep operating while it reorganizes its debts under a judge’s supervision. Car-Mart has agreed to sell substantially all of its business to Cardinal Asset Holdings LLC, a group whose leaders include Tilman J. “Skip” Falgout III, a former Car-Mart chief executive and chairman, and principal Chad Melchi.

The company, public since 2002, has been struggling for more than a year under heavy debt and falling sales. It closed 60 dealerships over the past two years, including 42 in April, leaving 94, and cut more than a tenth of its workforce; it lost about $69 million in the quarter that ended July 31. Its debt balance stands at about $311.6 million. Cardinal is the “stalking horse” bidder, meaning its offer, about $33.6 million not counting vehicle inventory and debt, sets a floor that other bidders may top. The company has also lined up $60 million in new money and asked the court for up to $125 million in financing in all. Its stock fell more than half Tuesday, to about 31 cents a share.

Chief Executive Doug Campbell said the deal provides “a committed buyer for the business and the financing to rebuild our inventory” before tax season. Dealerships remain open, and customers are told to keep making their car payments as usual; service contracts and accident-protection plans stay in place. The plan would pay trade creditors in full but needs the bankruptcy judge’s approval, as do proposed retention bonuses of up to $5.6 million, including $2 million for Mr. Campbell.

License-plate cameras at center of two police misconduct cases

A Little Rock police officer pleaded not guilty Tuesday to a stalking charge, and court papers say she used the city’s Flock cameras, a network of automated license-plate readers that log passing cars and let officers search where a vehicle has been, to track a woman she went to high school with. Officer Jadan Mobley-Duckworth, who was relieved of duty and arrested Monday on a misdemeanor charge of third-degree stalking, was released from the Pulaski County jail without bail.

A detective’s affidavit says she searched the woman’s license plate at least 10 times in May, September and October, starting the day the woman moved out of an apartment complex without telling her where she was going; the officer later moved into that complex as its courtesy officer. The woman reported in October that the officer had come to the beauty shop where she works and smiled and laughed at her. The officer told investigators she ran the plate because the car was in an area with many car break-ins. The charge is an allegation.

The case follows new details in North Little Rock, where Sgt. Jhailan Rathey was fired Nov. 20. An internal investigation found he used Flock to search plates tied to eight women he knew, including a girlfriend, her mother and former girlfriends, one plate 469 times between April and July, usually listing “drugs/narcotics” as the reason. He told investigators he wanted alerts so he could avoid women he feared might damage his car, and denied stalking. North Little Rock will ask the state’s police-licensing commission to revoke his certification, and a new policy taking effect Nov. 18 requires monthly audits of searches. Little Rock, which earlier suspended three officers amid claims of Flock misuse, has also moved to monthly audits.

From the Nation

Iowa families leave home as the Army readies this morning’s blast

Iowa Gov. Kim Reynolds, a Republican, declared a disaster emergency Tuesday in Des Moines, Lee and Van Buren counties in southeastern Iowa, and federal officials urged people living near the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown to leave home before the Army blows up nearly five tons of degraded lead azide, a highly sensitive explosive used in detonators, at about 9 a.m. today. The Army says the material, kept in 68 barrels, has crystallized on the outside of the drums and their lids and could go off on its own; “Inaction is no longer an option,” said Maj. Gen. Eric Shirley of the Army Sustainment Command. A federal appeals court cleared the way on Monday, and the two environmental groups that sued, the Iowa Environmental Council and the Sierra Club’s Iowa chapter, said they would not take further legal action.

The recommended evacuation zone is a five-mile cone running southwest of the plant, the direction the wind is forecast to carry any lead dust, plus a mile beyond it; residents were told to stay away until Friday at the earliest, and those who stay are urged to remain indoors. A federal health official, Sean Kaufman, said pregnant women, children and people with chronic illnesses should leave. Up to 100 Iowa National Guard soldiers are contacting about 150 households, the Federal Emergency Management Agency is providing transportation, hotel rooms and food, and Iowa farm officials asked farmers to move livestock away or into barns.

The Army’s forecast shows the plume could reach about 40 miles, into northeastern Missouri, where Gov. Mike Kehoe opened the state’s emergency operations center; an Agriculture Department official said about 350 farms could be affected. Barriers of water, foam, gel blocks and sand are meant to hold down the dust, the Environmental Protection Agency will test air, water and soil before and after, and NASA is flying three aircraft to watch the plume. Cleanup could take up to three months. The outcome was not known at press time.

Huawei convicted of fraud and plotting to steal trade secrets

A federal jury in Brooklyn, N.Y., on Tuesday convicted Huawei, the Chinese maker of telephone network equipment and smartphones, on 10 of the 12 criminal counts against it, including conspiracy to steal trade secrets from American companies, bank fraud, doing business in Iran and North Korea in violation of U.S. sanctions, and lying to U.S. officials and banks. The jury acquitted the company of the two most serious charges, racketeering conspiracy, which would have treated Huawei as a criminal enterprise, and conspiracy to obstruct justice. No individuals were on trial, and the company, not a person, faces the penalties, which could run to billions of dollars. Sentencing is set for next September.

The case, first brought in 2019 and expanded in 2020, ended a trial that began in September. Prosecutors said Huawei sold routers with copied software code, had an employee photograph a rival’s networking device at a Chicago trade show and took a robotic arm from a competitor’s lab to measure it; companies named in court records include Cisco, Motorola Solutions and T-Mobile. Iran tightens its grip on Hormuz as more tankers are hit

Iran’s Revolutionary Guards navy said two more oil tankers struck mines and caught fire in their engine rooms early Wednesday after taking what it called an “unauthorized route” through the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow passage at the mouth of the Persian Gulf that normally carries a large share of the world’s oil; the claim, carried by Iranian state media, has not been independently confirmed, and the ships were not named. Ratings are rule-of-thumb estimates from the forecast, the rut calendar and the sun, not measurements of animal or fish behavior.

Deer outlook: Fair

The calendar still helps, since the rut has not quite left its peak window in north Arkansas, and the dawn is a cold 36, but the afternoon’s rain, likely by three o’clock, takes the shine off the day. The barometer has been easing down since yesterday ahead of the storm. Best windows: from 6:19 a.m. to about two hours after sunrise; from 10 a.m. to 2 p.m., while bucks are still cruising and before the rain arrives; and from 2:50 p.m. until sunset at 4:50 if the rain holds off.

Today’s tip: with a north wind near 10 to 12 miles an hour, sit on the south side of the cover you are hunting and approach from the south, and put the morning to work, since that is the dry part of the day. Deer often feed hard in the hours just ahead of a front, so a late-morning sit over an acorn flat or a food plot may be the best of it. In Zone 4, around Jonesboro, it is bow-only now; in Zones 3, 6 and 8 the rifles are still out.

Tomorrow, Thanksgiving Day (estimate: Fair). A raw dawn near 34 with a stiffer northwest wind, about 13 to 16 miles an hour, rain and snow showers possible through the day, and the barometer still falling, which tends to keep deer feeding; the rut is past its peak, though late chasing goes on. Windows: 6:20 a.m. to about two hours after sunrise, and 2:49 p.m. to sunset at 4:49.

Tomorrow’s tip: a short dawn sit before the turkey goes in the oven is the classic Thanksgiving hunt. Dress for wet cold, not dry cold, with wool or synthetic layers and a quiet shell, and choose a stand on the lee side of a ridge where the gusts will not carry your scent and the deer can bed out of the wind.

Fishing outlook: Poor

A fast-falling barometer, a solid gray sky and an afternoon that barely reaches the low 40s add up to a slow day on Craighead Forest Lake and Lake Frierson, and the cold rain arriving after noon will chill the shallows further. If you go, go early in the afternoon before the rain, from late morning until about two o’clock, when the water is as warm as it will get.

Today’s tip: fish slow and deep. Crappie should be holding on brush piles and creek-channel edges in 12 to 18 feet of water; a small jig or a live minnow hung just above the cover, barely moving, will out-fish anything that has to be chased. Catfish are worth a try on cut bait in the deeper water.

Tomorrow, Thanksgiving Day (estimate: Fair). The barometer falls slowly through the day, which often starts a short feed ahead of the next change, but the afternoon stays in the mid-30s with a raw, gusty wind. Best times: late morning through midafternoon, and the last hour before sunset at 4:49, if you can get away from the table.

Tomorrow’s tip: fish the wind-sheltered banks, where you can keep a light line still; a blade bait or a jigging spoon worked off the bottom along the old creek channel is a good cold-water choice for bass. Then plan a longer trip for the sunny, mild weekend, when the water will begin to warm again.
