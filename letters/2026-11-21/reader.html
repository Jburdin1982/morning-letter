Of Local Interest

Fill the Food Bank tops its goal with 442,215 meals

Northeast Arkansas gave the equivalent of 442,215 meals on Friday in the 19th annual Fill the Food Bank drive, comfortably past this year’s goal of 400,000. The one-day drive for the Food Bank of Northeast Arkansas, the Jonesboro-based charity that supplies pantries and meal programs across the region, ran from 6 a.m. to 6 p.m. at a record 17 drop-off sites in Jonesboro, Brookland, Paragould, Blytheville, Wynne, Walnut Ridge and elsewhere; what was given at the satellite sites stays in the county where it was given. Rebecca Hendrix, the food bank’s chief development officer, announced the total Friday evening.

The result matters because the shelves were thin going in: the food bank had less than 30 days of food on hand, its lowest level since 2019, as wholesale food costs rose 15 to 25 percent and year-round giving fell. This year’s total is below last year’s 535,473 meals. Each $1 provides three meals, and gifts are still accepted by texting NEAFOOD to 43414 or at foodbankofnea.org.

Residents weigh in on Jonesboro’s 20-year growth plan

Jonesboro residents got their first public look Thursday evening at the draft Comprehensive Growth Plan and Master Street Plan, the two halves of Plan Jonesboro, at a meeting at First Community Bank. The growth plan sets out where and how the city should expand over the next 20 years as it prepares for a population of 100,000; the street plan covers road connections, traffic flow and safety. The drafts went before the City Council on Tuesday.

Derrel Smith, the city’s director of planning and zoning, said the most common requests have been for more parks and green space, trails, safer streets and better traffic control, and that the city is taking the plans out to neighborhoods rather than waiting for people to come to City Hall, so that no one first sees them on adoption day. Julie Kelso, vice president of planning for the consulting firm Crafton Tull, said projects would be paid for from existing city money, grants and other sources, and that no new tax is being proposed.

The consultants are still revising the drafts, and both plans are expected to go to the council for adoption by the end of June 2027. The drafts and an online comment card are at PlanJonesboro.com.

Judge presses both sides over money records in Lord’s Ranch abuse suits

U.S. District Judge D.P. Marshall Jr. told lawyers at a hearing in Jonesboro this week that both sides were falling short of his expectations in a fight over financial records in the Lord’s Ranch lawsuits. Lord’s Ranch was a residential treatment center for children in Randolph County, taking in children sent by social service agencies from several states, that closed in 2016 after its owner, Ted Suhl, was convicted in a federal criminal case. Since 2023, former residents have filed federal suits alleging sexual abuse at the ranch going back to the 1980s, and seeking damages from Mr. Suhl, Shirley Suhl and two former employees. The defendants have denied the allegations, and none has been proved in court.

The plaintiffs want financial records from the Suhls and related businesses; the ranch’s operators received well over $200 million in Medicaid payments between 2001 and 2014. Defense lawyers said searches of the property and the Suhls’ homes turned up no records, and that Shirley Suhl, 90, testified that all ranch records were destroyed. They asked the judge to quash subpoenas to 17 financial institutions and 17 related entities. Judge Marshall urged the defense to “turn over more rocks” and asked whether Mr. Suhl would allow the IRS to release his records.

The case also got new life from a recent Arkansas Supreme Court ruling that such suits are not too late to file. Judge Marshall said he would set aside an earlier order that had dismissed some claims on those grounds, and the plaintiffs have asked him to reinstate 24 former residents whose claims were dropped last year. Trial is set for June 2028.

From the State

Five probable measles cases reported in Lawrence County, the state’s first this year

The Arkansas Department of Health announced Friday that it has identified five probable measles cases in Lawrence County, in northeast Arkansas. They are the first cases in the state this year. All five people are unvaccinated residents who appear to have caught the virus while traveling in another state with a current outbreak; the department did not say which state. The cases are called “probable” because they rest on symptoms and exposure history and have not yet been confirmed by laboratory tests. No hospitalizations were reported.

Measles is a highly contagious viral illness that begins with fever, cough, runny nose and red, watery eyes, followed a few days later by a rash; a person can spread it from four days before to four days after the rash appears. State Epidemiologist Dr. Austin Porter said there is no evidence yet of spread within Arkansas and no other known cases in the state, so officials are not calling it an outbreak. Dr. Meghan Repp, president of the Arkansas chapter of the American Academy of Pediatrics, said community spread is “very likely” given the state’s low vaccination rates.

Only about 90 percent of Arkansas toddlers have had the measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) shot, among the lowest rates in the country and below the roughly 95 percent needed to keep the disease from spreading; Lawrence County is about the same as the state. Two doses give long-lasting protection, and the department says a dose given soon after exposure can stop the infection. People born before 1957 are generally considered immune. The United States has recorded 5,838 cases this year, Missouri 25 and Tennessee 11, and the country lost its measles-elimination status this week.

Federal judge refuses to approve deal freeing $6 billion data center from Pulaski County rules

U.S. District Judge Lee Rudofsky on Friday declined to sign a proposed settlement between Pulaski County and AVAIO Digital Partners, the developer of a planned $6 billion data center outside Wrightsville, south of Little Rock. Data centers are large buildings full of computer servers, of the kind that power artificial intelligence, and they use great amounts of electricity and water. AVAIO sued in October over a county ordinance regulating such projects, which the Quorum Court (the county’s governing body of elected justices of the peace) passed in the fall and then enacted over County Judge Barry Hyde’s veto by a 10-4 vote.

The deal, worked out between AVAIO and Mr. Hyde, who leaves office in about six weeks after losing his primary, would have declared that the ordinance does not apply to the project, because its site plan was approved before the rules took effect. In return AVAIO would have dropped its claims and its demand for legal fees. The judge said the agreement “all looks a little too perfect” and that it “leaves a very bad taste in my mouth,” in part because it could bind future county officials. Justice of the Peace Julie Blackwood, whom AVAIO accuses of withholding public records, objected through her lawyer.

Wendell Griffen, a former circuit judge who becomes county judge Jan. 1 and will have to enforce the ordinance, said he was “pleased” the deal was rejected; Judge Rudofsky had earlier denied his request to join the case. The hearing on AVAIO’s request for an order blocking the ordinance resumes at 8:30 a.m. Monday.

Arkansas jobless rate dips to 3.8 percent as payrolls set a record

Arkansas’ unemployment rate fell to 3.8 percent in October from 3.9 percent in September, the state Department of Commerce reported Friday, below the national rate of 4.1 percent. The number of nonfarm payroll jobs, a count of positions at businesses and government offices, rose by 8,700 in the month to a record, and both of the government’s job surveys reached all-time highs. The share of adults working or looking for work rose to 59.2 percent.

Over the past year, leisure and hospitality added 4,300 jobs, the most of any sector; trade, transportation and utilities added 2,600; and construction added 2,000, reaching a record. Financial services were the only sector to lose jobs. Michael Pakko, chief economist at the Arkansas Economic Development Institute at the University of Arkansas at Little Rock, said “labor market conditions in Arkansas have strengthened over the course of 2026.” The figures are preliminary and may be revised; some year-over-year comparisons are unavailable because last year’s federal shutdown halted the October 2025 survey.

Texas water firm offers Texarkana up to $1.65 million as Millwood Lake sale advances

Heartland Water Partners, a Texas company, is offering Texarkana, Ark., up to $1.65 million in three installments of $550,000 as it pursues a plan to pipe water from Millwood Lake, a reservoir on the Little River in southwest Arkansas, about 127 miles to Bois d’Arc Lake in North Texas for the North Texas Municipal Water District. The payments would come as the project clears three steps: a state application, approval by the Legislature and a signed final contract. The towns of Ashdown and Ogden are offered the same terms.

Texarkana could commit up to 110 million gallons a day. The preliminary price is 45 to 55 cents per 1,000 gallons, which City Manager Tyler Richards estimated could bring the city up to $20 million a year, rising toward $50 million by the end of the proposed 40-year contract; he cautioned that “the rate is not final.” The cities could not entertain other offers for that water through 2027.

The sale cannot go forward without the General Assembly’s approval. The Arkansas Natural Resources Commission, the state body that manages water resources, will hold a public hearing in southwest Arkansas next month and then make a recommendation to lawmakers, and each city council must also approve a final contract. The proposal comes as more than half the state sits in exceptional drought.

Federal appeals court halts Army’s planned explosive blast in Iowa

The U.S. Court of Appeals for the 8th Circuit, based in St. Louis, has temporarily blocked the Army from blowing up nearly five tons of degraded lead azide, a highly sensitive explosive used to set off larger charges, at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, in southeast Iowa near the Mississippi River. The Army had already postponed the detonation from Friday to Monday. The court’s order, issued late Thursday, pauses the Environmental Protection Agency’s emergency permit for the blast while the judges consider a longer halt.

The Iowa Environmental Council and the Sierra Club’s Iowa chapter asked for the stay, arguing that the EPA approved the plan without weighing its health effects or alternatives and that lead dust, a toxin especially harmful to children, could drift over an area about 25 miles long and 5 miles wide, including homes and waterways. The Justice Department said it would file its opposition by Sunday.

The Army, the EPA and the Department of Health and Human Services said Friday that the material is too unstable to move and that destroying it in place is the only responsible option, with “extreme mitigation measures.” Acting Army Secretary Adam Telle has said Monday was the latest safe date before a forecast cold snap that could make the material more dangerous. No date has been set for the court’s decision.

Senate rejects bid to make Trump end the Iran war or seek Congress’ approval

The Senate on Thursday voted 51 to 48 against a resolution that would have directed President Trump to remove U.S. forces from hostilities with Iran unless Congress authorized the war. The measure was brought under the War Powers Resolution, a 1973 law meant to limit a president’s ability to keep troops in combat without Congress’ approval; the Constitution gives Congress the power to declare war, and Mr. Trump never sought authorization. It was the first such vote since the Nov. 3 midterm elections.

Three Republicans, Sens. Susan Collins of Maine, Lisa Murkowski of Alaska and Rand Paul of Kentucky, voted with Democrats in favor, and Sen. John Fetterman of Pennsylvania was the only Democrat opposed. Sen. Tim Kaine, a Virginia Democrat and a longtime advocate for Congress’ war powers, said the war’s unpopularity “was made absolutely, manifestly clear in the election results.” Sen. Thom Tillis, a North Carolina Republican who backed a similar measure in June, voted no, saying, “This one doesn’t go to the president, so it’s just a statement from Congress.”

The House passed the resolution 220 to 204 in September. Democrats take control of the Senate on Jan. 3 and have pledged to make ending the war a priority. Before then, Congress must pass a spending bill by Dec. 11 to avoid a partial government shutdown.

Penny formally retired as Trump signs law allowing cash totals to be rounded

President Trump on Friday signed the Common Cents Act, a bipartisan law that formally ends the minting of pennies for circulation and lets stores round cash purchases to the nearest 5 cents. The U.S. Mint stopped making pennies in November 2025, after Mr. Trump called for an end to production, but until now merchants had no federal rules for making change without them.

Under the law, a cash total of $10.02 would round down to $10, and $10.04 would round up to $10.05. Card and other electronic payments will still be charged to the exact cent. Pennies remain legal tender, and the Treasury says the Federal Reserve will keep recirculating the roughly 114 billion still in use for as long as possible. The law also allows the nickel to be made with a cheaper zinc core.

The bill’s author, Rep. Lisa McClain, a Michigan Republican, said each penny costs nearly four cents to make and projected savings of $85 million a year; the Mint has estimated $56 million. It passed both chambers by voice vote. Retailers welcomed it: the National Association of Convenience Stores said stores had sought federal guidance on cash transactions since production ended.

From Abroad

Ukraine counts its dead as Poland asks NATO for more air defense

The death toll from Russia’s overnight air attack on Ukraine into Friday, one of the largest of the war, rose to eight, Ukrainian officials said: six in Kyiv, including three workers at a water facility, and two in the western city of Lviv, with 28 people wounded. President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said Russia fired more than 400 drones and more than 110 missiles, 27 of them ballistic, at energy, transport and rail sites. Ukraine’s nuclear power plants had to cut their output, and emergency power cuts hit Kyiv and 10 regions, according to First Deputy Energy Minister Artem Nekrasov. Russia’s Defense Ministry said it struck Kyiv’s road and rail bridges because they carry military equipment, and the attack followed President Vladimir Putin’s vow to answer Ukraine’s strikes on the Kerch Bridge to occupied Crimea.

In Poland, a NATO member, where two Russian missiles and a jet-powered drone are believed to have come down in the southeast during the attack, Defense Minister Władysław Kosiniak-Kamysz called it “another provocation” and said Warsaw was formally asking NATO for more forces under Operation Eastern Sentry, the alliance’s air-defense mission on its eastern edge. Prime Minister Donald Tusk said “nobody can say that this was an innocent coincidence.” Some early reports said Poland had invoked Article 4 of the NATO treaty, which calls for formal consultations among allies; it had not done so as of midday Saturday in Europe. NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte, who called the incident “clearly dangerous,” was due in Warsaw on Saturday for talks with the Polish government.

In Washington, President Trump told a nearly two-hour White House news conference Friday that he and Mr. Putin had “very good talks” in China and that there is a “real good chance” of a peace deal, but he declined to discuss security guarantees for Ukraine, saying “we got to get a deal first.” His envoy Jared Kushner had said Russia was comfortable with NATO-style guarantees for postwar Ukraine, while Russian Deputy Foreign Minister Sergei Ryabkov said any form of NATO “quasi membership” for Kyiv is unacceptable; Mr. Trump called the mixed signals negotiating tactics.

Iran says it shot down two U.S. drones as Washington adds sanctions

Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, the elite branch of its armed forces, said Saturday that a new air-defense system shot down an American MQ-9 Reaper drone over the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow Gulf waterway that carried about a fifth of the world’s oil and gas in peacetime, and later said it downed a smaller MQ-1 drone over Iran’s Qeshm Island in the strait. The claims were carried by Iranian state broadcasting, and the U.S. military did not immediately comment. The U.S. and Israel went to war with Iran on February 28; a June agreement between Washington and Tehran was never fully carried out, and attacks have flared periodically since July, including recent tit-for-tat strikes and Iranian attacks on ships.

On Friday the U.S. Treasury sanctioned four Iranians it called regime “oil trustees,” who it says collect money from illicit oil sales abroad to get around sanctions and the U.S. naval blockade of Iran’s southern ports, and who often kept the money for themselves. They include a former Revolutionary Guard intelligence officer, the brother of Iran’s football federation president and the sons of a Revolutionary Guard general and of the head of Mahan Air, an Iranian airline.

Iran’s foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, told state media Friday that the strait will not reopen until seven Iranian conditions are met, including an end to the war and the lifting of sanctions and the blockade, and he again ruled out shipping Iran’s highly enriched uranium abroad. Mr. Trump said at his news conference that Iran wants to make a deal, but not one he can accept, adding that the U.S. wants “a real deal, not a fake deal.”

Climate summit runs into overtime over fossil fuels

The United Nations climate summit known as COP31, in Antalya, Turkey, missed its Friday deadline with nearly 200 countries still deadlocked, and negotiators worked through the night toward a third draft of the final agreement promised for Saturday morning. The fight is over the latest draft, which dropped every mention of fossil fuels after objections from Saudi Arabia and other oil and gas producers, and which also cut a proposed $120 billion target for helping poorer countries adapt to climate change.

A group of about a dozen European and developing countries, including France and Panama, signed a letter demanding “a clear transition away from fossil fuels.” Panama’s environment minister, Juan Carlos Navarro, blamed a “petroleum mafia” and threatened to walk out or formally object, and the European Union warned the text “risks turning this climate COP into a fossil summit.”

The summit’s president, Turkish Environment Minister Murat Kurum, said, “We are close to the finish line, but we are not there yet,” and Australia’s climate minister, Chris Bowen, who is leading the negotiations, said he would work “for as long as it takes.” Expectations for big changes are low. The United States is not taking part for a second year. On the sidelines, host Turkey says it has signed deals and plans worth more than $26 billion during the conference, including a preliminary $15 billion renewable-energy agreement with China.

China tells Japan to “correct” its Taiwan stance after a summit snub

China’s Foreign Ministry said Friday that Japan’s top priority must be to “correct its wrong words and actions on the Taiwan issue as soon as possible,” a day after Chinese President Xi Jinping declined to hold a formal meeting with Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi at the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit in Shenzhen, China. The two leaders shook hands twice but did not sit down for talks.

The quarrel dates to about a year ago, when Ms. Takaichi, a conservative in Japan’s ruling party, suggested Japan might respond militarily if China tried to take Taiwan, the self-governed island Beijing claims, by force. China answered with trade restrictions and a warning to its citizens not to travel to Japan. Ms. Takaichi said Japan’s position is unchanged and that it still follows the 1972 agreement that restored ties with Beijing, adding that because the two countries have unresolved issues, “it is important to engage in dialogue.”

At the same summit she held her first face-to-face talks with Mr. Putin, at a time when relations between Tokyo and Moscow are also strained.

Saudi-led coalition launches a new air campaign in Yemen

The Saudi-led military coalition fighting Yemen’s Houthi rebels, the Iran-aligned movement that holds the capital, Sanaa, announced a new operation Friday that it said targets the sources of Houthi missile and drone attacks on Saudi Arabia. The coalition’s spokesman, Maj. Gen. Turki al-Maliki, said strikes hit a main Houthi supply depot and missile and drone workshops in Sanaa and weapons facilities at the Red Sea port of Hodeidah, whose workers were told to leave beforehand, and that strikes were continuing in Saada and Taiz provinces. There was no immediate Houthi response. (single source: Middle East Monitor)

The campaign comes as the Houthis push toward Aden, the southern port city that serves as the seat of Yemen’s internationally recognized government. Government forces pulled back from the Red Sea coast near the Bab el-Mandeb strait last weekend to defend Aden, and the Houthis have fired missiles at Riyadh and Saudi oil sites. Yemen’s civil war had been mostly quiet since 2022 but reignited this summer as a spillover of the Iran war; the United Nations says nearly 357,000 people have been displaced since August.

Young people march across Europe over living costs and military spending

Tens of thousands of students and young workers took to the streets Friday in a coordinated day of action in more than 25 European countries, protesting the rising cost of living, tuition fees, cuts to public services and growing military budgets. In Italy, where unions called a national strike and train disruptions were planned through Saturday, protesters filled more than 100 town squares, including Milan’s Piazza del Duomo; Maurizio Landini, head of the CGIL, Italy’s largest labor union, claimed more than a million took part, a figure not independently confirmed.

In Britain, students marched toward Parliament Square in London, and organizers said more than 350 schools, colleges and universities planned walkouts; there were also rallies in Germany, France and Spain. Demonstrators also opposed military conscription and what they called European governments’ complicity in Israel’s war in Gaza. “Fight for our future, not their wars,” said Adam Powell-Davies, national chair of the group Socialist Students. Afield & Afloat

Ratings are rule-of-thumb estimates from the forecast, the rut calendar and the sun, not measurements of animal or fish behavior.

Deer outlook: Good

The rut is still within its peak window for north Arkansas, the dawn is calm, under 5 miles an hour, and a cool start near 46 degrees comes with the barometer sliding ahead of Sunday’s front, a combination that tends to keep deer on their feet early. The catch is the heat: an afternoon near 74 will send most deer to shade by late morning. Best windows: from about 6:15 a.m. to roughly two hours after sunrise, and from 2:51 p.m. until sunset at 4:51; in the rut, 10 a.m. to 2 p.m. is realistic for bucks cruising between doe groups.

Today’s tip: with a light southerly breeze, hunt stands on the south side of bedding cover so your scent drifts away from it, and give the morning sit everything you have; on a day this warm, a buck seeking does is far more likely to be moving at 8 a.m. than at 4 p.m.

Tomorrow, Sunday (estimate: Fair). Gray skies and a mild dawn near 51, with a 64 percent chance of rain over the day and wind rising toward 15 miles an hour by evening as the front comes through. The rut still helps, but the warm start and the wind trim the odds. Windows: about 6:16 a.m. to two hours after sunrise, and 2:51 p.m. to sunset at 4:51, with midday worth a sit between showers.

Tomorrow’s tip: if the rain holds off at dawn, be in the stand early, because deer often feed hard just before a front; once the wind picks up, move to a sheltered bottom or the downwind edge of a thicket, where deer go to escape it. It is the last day of modern gun in Zone 4, so plan accordingly.

Fishing outlook: Fair

A warm, sunny afternoon near 74 and a light chop are in the fisherman’s favor on Craighead Forest Lake and Lake Frierson, but the barometer is falling fairly quickly ahead of the front, which can make fish moody. Best times: late morning through mid-afternoon as the shallows warm, and the last hour before sunset at 4:51.

Today’s tip: the sun will warm the shallow, dark-bottomed coves and the north banks that face it; work a slow crankbait or a shaky-head worm along those banks for bass, and look for crappie around brush piles in about 8 to 12 feet of water on small minnows or a 1/16-ounce jig.

Tomorrow, Sunday (estimate: Fair). Overcast and mild, an afternoon near 64, with rain chances and a wind that stiffens late in the day. Best times: late morning to mid-afternoon, before the wind builds, plus any lull near sunset.

Tomorrow’s tip: clouds and a light rain can draw bass shallow and make them less wary, so try a spinnerbait along wind-blown points and the edges of cover; once the rain sets in or the wind passes 15 miles an hour, catfish on cut bait along the dam or the deepest channel are the safer bet.

Deer: Zone 4’s last gun weekend. Modern gun season in Zone 4, which includes Craighead County, ends Sunday, Nov. 22; after that, Zone 4 hunters have archery through Feb. 28 and the Dec. 26-28 holiday gun hunt, and Zone 4 has no muzzleloader (Alternative Firearms) season. In Zones 3, 6 and 8, modern gun runs through Sunday, Dec. 6, followed by the Dec. 12-14 Alternative Firearms weekend and the same Dec. 26-28 holiday hunt. Each zone’s limit is four deer, no more than two bucks; in Zone 4 only one of the antlerless deer may be taken with a modern gun, while Zones 3, 6 and 8 allow three antlerless by gun. Craighead, Jackson, Mississippi and Randolph counties fall under the Game and Fish Commission’s chronic wasting disease (CWD) rules, the measures meant to slow a fatal deer disease; inside the CWD Management Zone there is no antler restriction, while elsewhere in Zones 3, 4 and 8 a buck must have at least three points on one side. Dogs are not allowed for deer in Zones 3 and 4; in Zones 6 and 8 they are allowed through Dec. 6, and nonresidents may use them only Nov. 28 to Dec. 6.

After 14 months of drought, the Commission expects fewer ducks and harder hunting and urges hunters to check water levels for their spot at agfc.com/wetlandmanagement before they go.

Postscript

My sorrow, when she’s here with me,
Thinks these dark days of autumn rain
Are beautiful as days can be;
She loves the bare, the withered tree;
She walks the sodden pasture lane.

Her pleasure will not let me stay.
She talks and I am fain to list:
She’s glad the birds are gone away,
She’s glad her simple worsted grey
Is silver now with clinging mist.

The desolate, deserted trees,
The faded earth, the heavy sky,
The beauties she so truly sees,
She thinks I have no eye for these,
And vexes me for reason why.

Not yesterday I learned to know
The love of bare November days
Before the coming of the snow,
But it were vain to tell her so,
And they are better for her praise.

— Robert Frost, “My November Guest,” from A Boy’s Will (1913). With the last summery day giving way to Sunday’s rain and a gray, chilly Thanksgiving week, Frost’s young poem is a reminder that the bare, gray end of November has a beauty of its own.
